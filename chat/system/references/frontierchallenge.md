# FrontierChallenge: Evaluating Scientific Workflow Completion

- **Source:** Liangcai Su, Zhaopeng Feng, Zhuo Chen, Zhen Zhang, Xiang Lin, Ruilin Li, Handuo Zhang, Ning Wang, Kailong Wen, Yueqi Guo, Feng Xing, Yiling Guo, Brian Wang, Chenxiong Qian, Simon Shaolei Du, Lidong Bing, and Xinyu Wang (credited as the Apodex Team), "FrontierChallenge: Evaluating Scientific Workflow Completion," arXiv preprint, 2026, arXiv 2608.24979 (version 1 posted August 25, 2026; version 2, the one read here, posted September 9, 2026)
- **Type:** preprint (not peer-reviewed); a benchmark paper from a team that also builds one of the systems it evaluates
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24979
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/system/prompt.md`

## What the source covers

The paper builds a benchmark of scientific workflows in which an agent must hand over a complete bundle of artifacts, and finds that the best systems complete about one task in five even though they collect most of the partial credit. For this prompt the result that matters most is a side analysis: in three of every four failed runs, the agent's last message said the work was done.

### Benchmark design

The authors assembled 300 end-to-end workflows from scientific and engineering practice and released 97 of them, drawn at random from the tasks that need no GPU to grade. The other 203 stay private as a held-out set. The released tasks are 74 hard and 23 medium, spread over six reporting domains: quantum chemistry (20), molecular dynamics (16), materials characterization (22), analytical chemistry (23), life science (10), and electrochemistry or environment (6). The authors say the domain sizes reflect what they released, not the fields themselves, so domain differences are not rankings of difficulty.

Each task is a package with a description of the scientific objective, fixed input data, a software environment (domain tools such as ORCA, CP2K, LAMMPS, AmberTools, and PLUMED), an output contract listing every required deliverable, and an executable grader. Deliverables include reports, structured tables, diagnostic figures, analysis code, and simulation outputs, and they must agree with one another. The grader checks required files, numerical results, formats, figures, whether the code runs, and whether the artifacts are consistent. Criteria that need semantic judgment go to a language-model judge (GPT-5.6 Sol, run three times and averaged), which counts as part of the grader. Scores run from 0 to 100. A task passes only at 99.9 or above, a threshold that absorbs rounding from averaging the judge's three scores and relaxes no requirement. The primary metric is the pass rate; the mean score is reported as a measure of partial progress.

Three appendix cases show what the contracts look like: a wound-healing image assay that must produce a script, two tables, two plots, and a methods report; a thin-layer chromatography plate with deliberately tilted geometry that must produce six tables, an annotated plate, lane profiles, and a report; and a reaction-calorimetry safety screen with a traceable chain of calibration, correction, summary, and decision tables.

### Models and scaffolds

The study ran 12 models in 13 configurations, one run per task each. Codex ran GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.6 Terra (max). Claude Code served as a common scaffold for ten models, including Claude Opus 5, Grok 4.6, Kimi K3, Qwen 3.8 Max, two DeepSeek V4 models, Gemini 3.7 Flash, GLM-5.2, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, and the authors' own Apodex 1.1. The authors' Frontier Agent scaffold ran Apodex 1.1 in a team configuration.

### Main findings

Pass rates ranged from 3.1 to 20.6 percent while mean scores ranged from 67.5 to 87.9. The top pass rate, 20 of 97 tasks, was shared by GPT-5.6 Sol in Codex and Grok 4.6 in Claude Code. Claude Opus 5 in Claude Code passed 17.5 percent: 39.1 percent of medium tasks and 10.8 percent of hard ones. Eight configurations averaged above 80 points, and none passed more than 20.6 percent of tasks, so most high-scoring bundles still missed at least one requirement.

The gap was widest in two domains. In electrochemistry, mean scores reached 94.9 and every configuration passed 0 of the six tasks. In analytical chemistry, the best mean score was 87.6 and only one configuration completed any task, for a pass rate of 4 percent. Quantum chemistry was the easiest domain, with pass rates up to 60 percent.

The paper also scores frozen trajectories on six process dimensions (coherence, evidence, alternatives, scope, tools, and repair) using a framework from the same group, graded by a panel of language models. Across the 12 configurations with usable trajectories, the process score correlates with the pass rate at a Pearson r of 0.83. Claude Opus 5 had the highest process score, 3.77 on a 4-point scale. Scope, which covers boundary checks and reasoning about failure, was the weakest dimension for every configuration.

### Completion claims

The failure analysis uses the 970 Claude Code trajectories (ten models on 97 tasks), since that scaffold logged a common event schema. Of the 849 runs that did not pass, 641 (75.5 percent) ended with a final message containing completion language, found by matching words, and only 13 (1.5 percent) said that work was still running or unfinished. Completion language appeared in 90.1 percent of passing runs. It was less common among runs scoring below 50 (61.2 percent) and stayed above 78 percent in every failing score band above 50. The closer a run came to passing, the more likely it was to say it was finished.

The paper reports these rates without computing what they imply for a reader of the final message. From its figures, about 109 of the 121 passing runs used completion language, so roughly 750 runs claimed completion and about 15 percent of those passed, against a 12.5 percent base rate across all 970. Of the roughly 220 runs that made no such claim, about 5 percent passed. A claim of completion barely raised the odds of real completion; the absence of a claim was the more informative signal. (These figures are my arithmetic from the reported percentages.)

Tool errors told the reader little. At least one error appeared in 80.7 percent of failing runs and 94.2 percent of passing ones, so successful runs hit errors and recovered.

### Strength of the evidence

The design has real strengths. Inputs are fixed, graders are executable, the pass criterion is strict, and the completion-language analysis rests on 970 trajectories under one scaffold. The headline gap between partial credit and full delivery holds across every configuration, so it does not depend on one model.

The weaknesses are also substantial. The paper is a preprint. Its authors evaluate their own model, their own scaffold, and their own process framework. Every configuration ran once, and no result carries a confidence interval. Part of each grade comes from a language-model judge, and at a 99.9 threshold a single missed rubric item fails a run, so some failures may reflect judge strictness or rubric detail that the agent could not see. The completion-language classifier is lexical, and the paper gives no precision figure for it. The analysis does not separate sincere false beliefs from habitual sign-off phrasing, and it does not break completion language out by model, so Claude Opus 5's own rate is unknown. Two domains rest on six and ten tasks. The analysis is descriptive, as the authors say, and it tests no remedy.

On transfer: the agents worked unattended on multi-artifact tasks for 20 to 110 minutes on average, with no person to check them. Chat tasks are usually shorter, and a person is present. The mechanism, a closing message that overstates what was delivered, is not specific to science, and the measured agents include Claude Opus 5, close to the models this prompt runs on. The size of the gap should be smaller on short requests with one deliverable and similar on long, multi-part ones.

## What the prompt applies

**External evidence of completion (Working loop, While working, operational goals, third bullet).** The prompt counts a goal as done only when something outside the model's own judgment shows it, and tells the model to use the evidence the work already produces instead of adding a separate review pass. The rule comes from the paper's completion-language result: the model's own statement that it is done carried almost no information, so the prompt makes that statement insufficient. The prompt's examples (passing tests, the saved file, the rendered page) are the same kind of checks the benchmark's graders run on files, numbers, and code execution. Two gaps apply. The paper measured a failure and tested no instruction, so it cannot say whether telling a model to distrust its own sense of completion lowers false claims. And many benchmark failures came from rubric criteria graded by a model the agent never saw. The prompt's rule catches a missing file or a failed run; it cannot catch a deliverable that exists, runs, and is wrong in a way no available check reveals.

**The goal list as a stopping rule (Working loop, Before starting, first two bullets).** The benchmark's output contract is a list of required deliverables, and the typical failure was a bundle that met most of the list and missed one item. That supports writing every required part down and treating the list as the definition of done, and the prompt's instruction to keep the list in a to-do tool or a file on longer tasks matches the explicit contract tracking the paper recommends. The paper's conclusion calls for explicit contract tracking, but as a recommendation, not a tested intervention. The settings also differ: the benchmark hands the agent its contract, while the prompt asks the model to derive its goals from what the user is trying to accomplish, which the paper does not study.

**Full scope (operational goals, second bullet).** The prompt asks the model to deliver what was asked at the scope the user intended, to finish every part of it, and to say when it cannot. High partial scores with one missing piece are the failure this bullet targets.

**Reporting open goals (Composing answers, fourth bullet).** The prompt asks the model to say what it verified, what it could not check, and which goals remain open. In the benchmark, only 1.5 percent of failing runs said anything was unfinished. This bullet asks for the report the agents almost never gave.

## What the prompt omits

The prompt leaves out cross-artifact consistency checks as a separate rule. They are specific to multi-deliverable work, and the general completion criterion covers the part that applies to chat (each deliverable must show its own evidence). It leaves out the process dimensions, including the weak scope scores, because they come from a model-graded framework the authors built and are not tied to any instruction one could write. It adds nothing about tool errors, and the paper gives reason not to: errors appeared in most passing runs, so treating them as a failure signal would mislead. Domain rankings, token use, and run times bear on choosing a model, not on writing a prompt.

## Conflicts with other references

**Prompting Claude Opus 5 (`anthropic-opus-5-prompting.md`; worked from the source text).** The guide says Claude Opus 5 checks its own work without being told, and that explicit verification instructions (a final verification step, verifying with a subagent, double-checking, re-verifying) make it over-verify by adding tokens without improving quality, so they should be removed. This paper complicates the premise. Claude Opus 5 in Claude Code, presumably checking itself in the way the guide describes, passed 17.5 percent of tasks, and the pooled Claude Code runs claimed completion in three of four failures. The model's own checking did not make its completion claims reliable on long, multi-deliverable work. The two sources can both hold if the prompt adds no extra self-check and instead changes what may count as done, which is how the prompt is worded. It names a criterion, not a step. Its examples are observations the work usually produces anyway (test output, a saved file, a rendered page), and its second sentence tells the model to use that evidence and not to add a separate review pass. It asks for no final pass, no double-check, and no verifier, and a separate bullet tells the model not to use subagents to recheck its own work. The residual risk is that a model still runs fresh checks on goals whose work produced no evidence of its own, which would be over-verification by another route. That risk is untested; the instruction to scale the loop to the task limits it. `progress-mirage.md` carries the fuller version of this analysis.

**When Do Agent Loops Mistake Stagnation for Progress? (`progress-mirage.md`).** The two papers agree that an agent's own report of success is close to uninformative. The progress-mirage paper adds a boundary: when success is visible in the artifact, such as a file that exists or a test that passes, a capable reader judges it well. Most of this benchmark's grader checks sit on that visible side, while its model-judged rubric items sit partly outside it, which may explain why near misses were so common.

**Push Your Agent (`pushbench.md`).** PushBench agrees that a final claim must not decide completion, and goes further: blocking unsupported completion claims in the harness recovered little success, while tracking verified progress outside the model recovered a lot. This paper tests no remedy, so it neither supports nor contradicts that result.

**Harness design for long-running application development (`anthropic-harness-design.md`; worked from the local copy of the post).** The post reports that agents grading their own work praise it, and that a separate evaluator exercising the running application catches real bugs. That is consistent with this paper's finding. The post's remedy, a second agent, differs from the prompt's choice to forbid rechecking subagents; `progress-mirage.md` discusses why the two can be reconciled.

**Claude prompting best practices (`anthropic-prompting-best-practices.md`).** That file records the over-verification tension between the page's general self-check advice and its Opus 5 exception, and describes how the prompt's completion criterion, with no separate review pass, sits between them. It agrees with the reading here.
