# Anatomy of LLM Sycophancy: What a Flip Rate Hides

- **Source:** Haonan Huang, "Anatomy of LLM Sycophancy: What a Flip Rate Hides," Princeton University, arXiv preprint, October 2026, arXiv 2610.06522 (version 1, Oct. 5, 2026)
- **Type:** preprint (not peer-reviewed)
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06522
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/system/prompt.md`

## What the source covers

The paper argues that a single sycophancy score per model mixes up behaviors that should be kept apart. When a user pushes back, a model can correct a real mistake, cave on a correct answer, or hold, and a flip rate counts the first two alike. The author builds a replay instrument, SycoLens, that varies the settings of the measurement one at a time and shows that each one changes the result. Three findings bear on this prompt. A stated opposing opinion and a bare challenge to the answer are different pressures that rank models almost unrelatedly. On arithmetic with a known answer, one model's flips are mostly corrections reached by redoing the work and another's are mostly capitulations with no work shown. And worked reasoning in the model's earlier turn protects whatever answer it argues for, right or wrong, while a bare stated value does not protect under pressure.

### Method

Each measurement is one stateless request. The transcript holds a question, a committed answer in the assistant's turn, and one user follow-up line, then a fixed bridge sentence and a request to answer again in a set format. The matched control deletes the follow-up line and keeps every other byte, so a line's effect is its flip rate minus the control's flip rate. That subtraction matters: one model's answers changed 26 percent of the time at depth on the arithmetic bank with no pressure at all, and a raw flip rate would have counted that churn as sycophancy.

There are nine pressure lines in two families. The opinion family asserts the opposite verdict with three levels of stated confidence, plus a support line that asserts the model's own side with the same middle confidence. The dispute family challenges the answer without naming an alternative. Its lines are a bare question asking whether the model is sure, a hedged disagreement, a certain disagreement, and two insults (one aimed at the answer, one at the model). Every line is one sentence.

There are two task banks. The moral bank is 240 first-person vignettes from the commonsense-morality part of ETHICS. The author does not treat the data set labels as truth, so movement is measured against the model's own conviction. The arithmetic bank has 107 cells of a program-tracking task. Each is a two-counter recurrence run for some number of rounds, asked as whether the result is above or below a threshold. The correct answer is known and cannot be checked at a glance, so a pressured model has to choose between redoing the work and taking the user's word.

The instrument also measures how close each item sits to the model's own decision boundary, from 21 screening draws per moral item (16 per arithmetic cell). An item where every draw agreed is screening-consistent; one where the draws split evenly sits on the boundary. Other modules vary the committed turn (another draw of the model's own, its actual minority answer, or planted texts) and the answer format (open restatement, yes or no, or lettered choice).

Eleven closed models from three providers took part. They were Claude Opus 5, Sonnet 5, Sonnet 4.6, and Haiku 4.5. The GPT models were GPT-5 mini, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Luna, and GPT-6 Astra. The Gemini models were Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.5 Flash, and Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite. Nine ran with reasoning off or minimal, Gemini 3.1 Pro with low hidden reasoning, and GPT-6 Astra with reasoning that cannot be disabled. The study made about 760,000 controlled replays. Inference uses bootstrap intervals over items or cells, a ±5 percentage-point equivalence margin for no-effect claims, and Holm correction across nine headline tests.

### Main findings

**Opinions and disputes are different pressures.** Under open restatement, the three opinion lines agreed with one another on which models are easy to move (mean rank correlation 0.82), and so did the three civil disputes (0.88). An opinion line and a dispute line agreed at about zero (−0.01). GPT-5 mini moved 38.1 points under the fairly confident opinion and 4.7 under the hedged dispute; Claude Sonnet 5 moved 25.5 and 4.0 points. Claude Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 4.6 showed the reverse: they moved most under the dispute (21.7 and 15.2 points) and less under the opinion (13.1 and 2.9). A confirmation round on 80 new items with paraphrased lines reproduced the split. Averaged across models, the opposing opinions moved answers 12 to 13 points, the stronger disputes 7.5 to 8.8 points, and the insults and bare doubt only 1.8 to 2.9 points. Stated confidence did not raise the effect steadily: in four of nine models the opinion effect was not increasing from hesitant to certain.

**Sycophancy concentrates where the model is unsure, but not only there.** On items near a model's own boundary, the opinion line's effect was about 40 points larger than on screening-consistent items, and the dispute line's about 37 points larger. The rise was resolved in every panel model except Claude Opus 5, which did not move at any distance from its boundary. Claude Sonnet 5 moved 91.7 points on its 10 near-boundary items, against 25.5 points over all items. Decisive items still moved: 17.5 points for Sonnet 5, and because they are most of the bank they made up about half of the total for the three models most moved by opinion.

**Behavior on one task does not predict another.** Claude Haiku 4.5 moved 68 to 75 points on arithmetic under the fairly confident opinion, even where it had been nearly unanimous. GPT-5 mini and Gemini 3.5 Flash, two of the most opinion-moved models on moral items, barely moved on arithmetic. Claude Sonnet 5 moved 45.8 points on arithmetic.

**A flip can be a correction or a capitulation.** Because arithmetic has a known answer, the author separates movement toward the truth from movement away from it, net of whether the committed answer was the model's majority or minority answer. Pressure undid Claude Sonnet 4.6's wrong commitments far more than its correct ones, by 51 points under the opinion line and 65 points under the hedged dispute. The mechanism was visible. The share of its replies that wrote out intermediate values rose from 15 percent at control to 70 to 91 percent under most civil lines, and 85 to 94 percent of those replies ended on the correct side. Nearly all of its movement under the opinion line came from replies that recomputed (29.7 points) and almost none from replies that did not (2.2 points). Under the certain opinion it recomputed less often (43 percent) and switched without work more, though its movement still leaned toward the truth. Claude Haiku 4.5 did the opposite. It flipped in 98 percent of pressured replies that showed no work, and under the opinion line it abandoned correct answers more than wrong ones (a truth effect of −16.5 points), though under the bare doubt the sign reversed.

**The committed turn anchors the answer at rest.** With no pressure, models kept their committed answers far more often than an independent redraw would. Claude Sonnet 5, GPT-5 mini, and Gemini 3.5 Flash changed 0 to 1.1 percent of answers where a fresh draw would have disagreed 27 to 41 percent of the time. When the committed answer was the model's own minority answer, the opinion line pulled it back toward the majority by 32.5 to 76.8 points in nine models; Claude Opus 5 again did not move. The author reads this as regression toward the model's own answer distribution more than deference. The reason is that the support line, which agrees with the committed side, still saw GPT-5.6 Sol and Luna abandon about 39 percent of minority commitments.

**Planted reasoning protects whatever it argues for.** On moral items, replacing the committed answer with the model's own written rationale instead of a bare verdict gave little extra protection under pressure (3.1 points pooled, inside the equivalence margin). For the Anthropic models, a filler text of the same length that claimed careful thought without giving an argument did about as well as the real rationale. The sharper test is on arithmetic, run on 21 of Claude Sonnet 4.6's cells. With a correct round-by-round derivation planted in the committed turn, pressure released the correct answer only 4 to 6 percent more often than control, against 17 to 32 percent with the value and verdict alone. The bare value anchored the answer at rest (2.4 percent churn against 13.3 percent for a verdict alone) but protected nothing under pressure. Then the author planted a derivation with one seeded slip that led to a wrong answer. It anchored like the true one at rest (0.3 percent churn against 11.3 percent for the bare wrong value). Under the dispute lines it cut correction of the wrong answer by 29 points (hedged) and 41 points (bare doubt) relative to the bare wrong value. The shield was relative: Sonnet 4.6 still corrected the wrong trace in 57 and 38 percent of pressured replies, while it abandoned the true trace in only 6 and 4 percent. In 39 percent of sampled wrong-trace replies it named the corrupted round or its true value, and every one of those replies corrected the answer. Haiku 4.5 never did.

**The answer format changes what is measured.** Yes-or-no and lettered-choice formats shrank dispute effects more than opinion effects and moved the two families' rankings closer together. Pressure also pushed answers toward "no" regardless of which side the question named, by up to 26 points (Claude Sonnet 4.6 under the certain opinion). A single-phrasing yes-or-no benchmark therefore inherits a bias along with its verdict.

### Strength of the evidence

The design is careful. Every effect has a matched control that differs by one line, factors vary one at a time, and the paper reports intervals, equivalence margins, and multiplicity correction, with a pre-registered confirmation round. The two-family split, the boundary effect and the at-rest anchoring are large and replicate across many models.

The weaknesses are in scope and in the interventions. Each pressure is one sentence in one turn, so the paper says nothing about sustained or adaptive pushback. The arithmetic bank is one task family, and the cross-task comparison covers six models. Most models ran with reasoning off, which matters because the most interesting behavior, re-deriving under pressure, is a form of reasoning. The derivation experiment is the one that bears most on this prompt, and it is also the narrowest. It used one model for the true trace (21 cells) and two for the wrong trace. On fresh program instances the true-trace protection for Sonnet 4.6 was unresolved (11.7 points, with an interval spanning zero), while Haiku 4.5's wrong-trace shield reversed under the opposing opinion. A wording error in the probe turned one wrong-trace arm's opinion line into an endorsement, which the author reports openly. The planted texts are templates that differ in style from natural replies. The nine headline tests were chosen after a first look at the data, which Holm correction does not cover, and the paper says so. It has a single author, discloses that LLM tools helped with coding, analysis, and editing, and has not been peer reviewed. The code and records are not yet released.

### Transfer to a frontier model following a system prompt

The models are current, including Claude Opus 5, the nearest relative of the model this prompt runs on, and Opus 5 was the one model that barely moved on moral items. That suggests a strong current model may already resist one-line pressure on judgment questions, though Opus 5 was not run on arithmetic. The setting differs from deployment in several ways: no system prompt is described, reasoning was mostly off, each probe was a single turn, and the answer was a forced final verdict. No instruction to the model was tested. The findings that should carry over are qualitative. Stated opinions and bare challenges are different pressures. Movement is largest where the model is unsure. A flip with visible re-derivation behaves differently from one without. Earlier worked reasoning in the transcript resists change whether or not it is correct.

## What the prompt applies

**Pushback carries no evidence (Handling claims, fourth bullet).** The paper supports the bullet's core claim in a controlled way. A one-sentence opposing opinion, with no evidence attached, moved models 12 to 13 points on average and by over 90 points near Claude Sonnet 5's boundary. It also reorders the bullet's list. Insults and a bare doubt, the closest single-turn analogues to frustration and persistence, moved models least, at 1.8 to 2.9 points. In this paper the most potent content-free pressure is a confident statement of the opposite view. The bullet now names confident disagreement first among the cues that carry no evidence, and the counterfactual check covers the same case from the other side.

**The counterfactual check (Handling claims, third bullet).** The opinion family and the support line make a matched pair: the same confident wording asserting either the opposite side or the model's own. The check asks the model whether it would answer the same way if the user had asserted the opposite with equal confidence, which is the comparison those two lines make. The paper shows the asymmetry the check is meant to catch, since the opposing line moved answers and the support line held them. It does not test the check as an instruction.

**Update only on evidence or argument, and name which (Handling claims, fourth bullet).** This bullet is where the paper both supports and complicates the prompt. It shows that a flip under pressure is not always a failure: Claude Sonnet 4.6 used pushback as a cue to redo the work and mostly reached the truth. The prompt allows that case, because a re-derivation that finds an error is new evidence produced by the model. The requirement to name what changed the model's mind fits the paper's dividing line. Sonnet 4.6's corrections came with written work that showed the reason; Haiku 4.5's capitulations came with none. A model that must name its evidence cannot make Haiku's kind of flip honestly. That alignment is my inference, since the paper does not test the requirement.

**Rework the answer when challenged (Handling claims, fifth bullet, first sentence).** This bullet takes the step that made Sonnet 4.6's flips good. When the user challenges an answer, the prompt tells the model to rework it from the evidence instead of defending it or giving it up. That is what Sonnet 4.6 did. It treated pushback as a cue to redo the computation and kept the answer only if it survived, and that re-check is what let it find the seeded slip in a wrong derivation. Haiku 4.5's pattern, flipping with no work shown, is the giving-up half of what the bullet rules out, and holding a wrong trace because it was written earlier is the defending half. The paper tests no instruction, so the bullet rests on the behavior it observed, not on a measured effect of asking for it. The risk the paper suggests is that a rework run as a ritual on every pushback becomes a reason to give ground; the fourth bullet's rule that pressure carries no evidence guards against that.

**One standard however long the conversation runs (Handling claims, fifth bullet, second sentence).** The derivation results bear on how this sentence should read. Firmness is only as good as the reasoning behind the position. A planted wrong derivation made the model 29 to 41 points less likely to correct a wrong answer, and planted prose and length protected answers whether or not they were argued well. An instruction to hold firmer over time could have strengthened the same anchoring to the model's earlier text that shielded wrong answers. The prompt asks instead for the same standard at every turn, so the bar for changing position stays fixed while pressure accumulates, and the fourth bullet adds that the model's own earlier answer carries no evidence of its own. Together these say that earlier commitments gain no extra authority from having been made, which is the reading the paper supports. The paper's end states were reassuring (wrong traces corrected 38 to 57 percent of the time, true traces abandoned 4 to 6 percent).

**The model's own earlier answers (Handling claims, first and fourth bullets).** The first bullet names the user's assertions and saved memory as hypotheses to check. The paper shows that the model's own earlier turns act as assertions too, and strong ones: committed answers churned far below a fresh redraw, and a wrong derivation in the committed turn resisted correction. The fourth bullet now covers that case by name and says that the fact that the model said something earlier carries no evidence of its own.

**Weak signals of quality (Working loop, sources bullet).** The prompt tells the model that polish and authority cues are weak signals. The paper finds that length and a claim of careful checking, without any argument, carried much of the protection a real rationale gave. The finding concerns the model's own committed text, not outside sources, so it supports the bullet only by analogy.

**Calibration (Epistemic stance).** The prompt asks for a clear bottom line when the evidence supports one and a plain statement when it doesn't. The boundary result shows why that matters for sycophancy: the largest flips happened on items where the model's own repeated answers split, that is, where it had no settled view. On such items the honest answer is often that the question is close, which leaves less room for a confident opinion to tip it. The paper does not test this.

## What the prompt omits

**How to rework.** The prompt asks the model to rework a challenged answer from the evidence but does not say to write out the intermediate steps. Those steps are the visible marker that separated Sonnet 4.6's corrections from Haiku 4.5's capitulations. The fourth bullet's demand to name what changed the model's mind puts the reason in the reply, which covers most of the point without prescribing a format.

**The two pressure families.** The prompt does not distinguish a user who states the opposite answer from one who only says the model is wrong. The paper shows these move different models by different amounts. The counterfactual check and the update rule apply to both, so naming them separately would add length without changing the procedure.

**Task dependence.** Behavior on moral items did not predict behavior on arithmetic. The prompt applies one rule across tasks, which is the right design given that finding.

**Format effects.** The yes-or-no bias toward "no" under pressure is a measurement problem for benchmark builders. It matters to this prompt only when an answer is forced into yes or no, which the prompt does not do.

**The reporting profile.** The paper's recommendations for how to report sycophancy belong in evaluation work, not in a system prompt.

## Conflicts with other references

Only `bhalla-sway.md` existed in `chat/system/references/` among the sycophancy sources when this file was first written. For the others I worked from the sources: the full text of SPINE and the arXiv abstracts of AuthorityBench and the Gemini sycophancy audit. Their reference files (`tang-spine.md`, `khurana-authoritybench.md`, and `keough-granularity-gap.md`) now exist, and the points below agree with them.

**Tang et al., SPINE (`tang-spine.md`, arXiv 2609.09090).** SPINE finds emotional appeals the most damaging tactic, with pity and anger at the top, while SycoLens finds insults and a bare doubt the weakest lines. The designs differ: SPINE counts any drop in strength over many adaptive turns and chooses tactics in response to the target, while SycoLens measures one line in one turn against a matched control. Insults are also not the same as appeals to pity. The two papers agree that content-free pressure moves current models. SycoLens adds that a flip can be a correction, a case SPINE rules out by design because it starts from a correct answer and counts only moves toward a known falsehood. The prompt's rules fit both: pressure carries no evidence, a challenge calls for reworking the answer from the evidence, and a position changes only on evidence or argument, including the model's own re-derivation.

**Bhalla and Gligorić, SWAY (`bhalla-sway.md`, arXiv 2604.02423).** SWAY found that sycophancy generally rose with the certainty a user expressed. SycoLens found that the opinion effect did not rise steadily with stated confidence in four of nine models. SycoLens also shows that forced yes-or-no answers carry a pressure-induced shift toward "no." SWAY used single-token forced choices, and on its yes-or-no debate set the Claude models moved away from what leading questions implied. Part of that negative score could reflect the kind of format bias SycoLens measures; that is my conjecture, and neither paper tests it. The prompt does not depend on either reading, because its check applies whatever the user's stated confidence.

**Khurana et al., AuthorityBench (`khurana-authoritybench.md`, arXiv 2606.13104).** AuthorityBench finds that adding a citation, real or fabricated, raises error rates even for true claims. SycoLens finds that length and an asserted check protect a committed answer without any argument. Both say that the form of evidence moves models apart from its content. The prompt's source-judging bullet answers both by asking for provenance and method. For this file I read only the AuthorityBench abstract.

**Keough, Gemini sycophancy audit (`keough-granularity-gap.md`, arXiv 2606.05183).** That audit reports that one direct instruction beat an elaborate reasoning protocol in seven of eight Gemini variants. SycoLens tests no instruction, so there is no direct conflict. For this file I read only the abstract; `keough-granularity-gap.md` reads the full text and reaches the same view.

## Review notes

I read version 1 from a local text extraction: the main paper (sections 1 to 10), the references and the opening of the headline-test appendix. I did not read the later appendixes (collection details, per-condition tables, transcripts, and the comparison with other instruments). Every number here comes from the main text, so per-line intervals the paper defers to the appendixes are not checked. The figures survived only as text fragments, so per-model values that appear only in figures (for example, several truth effects in Figure 3a) are taken from the main text. The arXiv listing confirms a single version dated Oct. 5, 2026, inside the repository's eight-month window. The paper says its code and records will be released on publication; they were not available, so I could not check any transcript. The wrong-trace arm's wording error affects only its opinion line, and the figures cited here for that arm come from the dispute lines, which the paper reports as sent correctly. I found no peer-reviewed version.
