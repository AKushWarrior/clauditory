# The Collaboration Tax: How Much LLM Multi-Agent Systems Pay to Coordinate

- **Source:** Weixiang Sun, Zehong Wang, Hong Huang, Colby Nelson, Yijun Ma, and Yanfang Ye (University of Notre Dame and Simon Fraser University), "The Collaboration Tax: How Much LLM Multi-Agent Systems Pay to Coordinate," arXiv preprint arXiv:2608.22152, first version August 23, 2026, revised September 22, 2026 (version 2 read).
- **Type:** Preprint (not peer-reviewed), academic
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22152
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/system/prompt.md`

## What the source covers

The paper measures how much two language models lose when they must pool private information to solve a problem either could solve alone with all the facts. It calls the loss the collaboration tax: the gap between one model given the whole problem and two copies of it, each holding half, talking until they agree. Across 11 models and 32 puzzle tasks the tax was positive almost everywhere, larger for weaker models, and caused mainly by conversational lapses (unsupported claims, missing questions, skipped integration of both halves, unchecked acceptance) rather than by reasoning limits. A prompt that targets those lapses recovered about 38 percent of the gap.

### Method

Each task is a procedurally generated puzzle with a program that computes the answer: shortest paths in mazes and grids (the spatial family), queries over relations and orderings (relational), and constraint-satisfaction puzzles (CSP). Every instance is split into two views so that neither alone determines the answer, and the tasks are chosen to be easy for one agent holding both views. Answers are scored on a continuous 0-to-1 scale; a fixed model (gpt-4o-mini) converts the free-text answer into structured form and a program scores it.

Three modes run on each task. In the solo mode one model gets the whole instance, answers, and gets one self-critique pass to offset the extra turns the pairs receive. In the homogeneous mode two copies of the model each get one view and exchange messages until one declares an answer or 50 exchanges pass. In the heterogeneous mode the two seats go to different models. Each cell runs 50 times at temperature 0.7.

The 11 models span a wide capability range: gpt-5, gpt-5-nano, gpt-4.1-mini, gpt-4.1-nano, gpt-4o-mini, Claude Sonnet 4.5, gemini-2.5-flash-lite, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Llama-4-Maverick, Phi-4, and Qwen3-8B. The heterogeneous runs pair gpt-4.1-nano with each of three strong models (gpt-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and DeepSeek-V4-Pro), in both seat orders.

To find where pairs fail, the authors clustered 700 model-written failure descriptions into themes and grouped them into four stages drawn from the psychology of conversational grounding: grounding (every claim traces to something one agent stated), querying (an agent asks the partner for a specific fact), integration (someone writes out the combined state of both views before proposing an answer), and re-derivation (the receiving agent recomputes the answer before accepting it). A model judge labeled every rollout on each stage. Four human annotators relabeled 100 rollouts; agreement between the judge and the human majority was substantial on the first three stages and moderate on re-derivation.

### Findings

**The tax follows two orderings with no exceptions.** For every model, spatial tasks lost the most, relational tasks less, and CSP tasks least. Across models, the tax fell as capability rose: the weakest models lost roughly half their solo success to coordination, while gpt-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and DeepSeek-V4-Pro lost about 6 to 8 percent overall in the heterogeneous-pair heatmap's diagonal cells. The three weakest models came from three different families, so the capability ordering isn't a family artifact. The tax turned slightly negative only for Claude Sonnet 4.5 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro on CSP.

**Splitting the information costs more than reasoning over it.** In a control, one agent received both partial views as separate pieces, with no partner. It recovered 93 to 99 percent of its full-instance score. The further drop to the two-agent pair was three to ten times larger on every model and made up 80 to 86 percent of the total gap. Combining two views is easy for one agent and hard for two.

**Four conversational lapses explain the failures.** Each of the four stages separated failed rollouts from successful ones. Grounding acted as a hard prerequisite: once an agent invented a value neither view contained, no later stage recovered. Integration was the strongest single predictor. A regression on conversation features alone (length, disclosure, agreement markers, and the like) predicted held-out tasks with an out-of-fold R-squared of 0.48 and transferred to the heterogeneous pairs at 0.41. In that regression, model capability set the baseline level of the tax and conversation features set how it varied.

**A prompt closes part of the gap.** Adding four clauses to the system prompt, one per stage, raised the pairs' mean score from 0.402 to 0.439 and recovered about 38 percent of the tax overall (27 percent on spatial, 41 percent on relational, and 53 percent on CSP). The clauses required citing the source of every quantitative claim, asking the partner for missing facts, writing out a combined state before proposing an answer, and showing recomputation before accepting one. Dialogues got shorter (18.2 to 15.9 turns), so the lift isn't an artifact of talking longer. Dropping one clause at a time showed that each family has a different bottleneck: re-derivation for spatial, grounding for relational and querying for CSP.

**A weak partner drags a strong one down, though less than averaging would predict.** In the heterogeneous pairs, the gap measured against the stronger model's solo score sat close to the strong model's own pair gap, far below the midpoint between the strong and weak models. The pairs still did worse than the strong model paired with itself: Claude Sonnet 4.5 scored 0.869 with a copy of itself and about 0.73 with gpt-4.1-nano, against 0.352 for two copies of gpt-4.1-nano. Every member's share of the pair's value fell below what it earned alone, which the authors express in cooperative game theory as a failure of the pair to be worth at least as much as its best member.

### Strength of the evidence

The design isolates coordination cleanly: tasks are easy for one agent with full information, so the gap can't be blamed on hard reasoning, and the single-agent control with split views pins the loss on the two-agent exchange. Fifty rollouts per cell and 11 models from 7 providers give the orderings real weight. The weaker parts are the mechanism and the intervention. The four stages come from model-written failure descriptions clustered by a model and labeled by a model judge, with only moderate agreement on re-derivation; they are a useful taxonomy, and the intervention result is the only causal test of it. The paper reports the intervention's per-family lifts but doesn't make clear which models it ran on, and its 0.402 baseline is far below the strong models' scores, so the 38 percent figure may describe weaker models. The heterogeneous matrix has three pairs, which the authors themselves call an existence proof. The tasks are synthetic puzzles with exact answers, and all pairs are two agents talking as peers.

### Transfer to a chat agent following a system prompt

The paper's structure differs from a chat agent's delegation. Its agents are peers that each hold half the facts and must talk to combine them. A chat agent's subagent instead gets a written task, works alone, and returns one report, and the lead does the combining. The findings still transfer in two ways. First, the cost of splitting information across agents is large and mostly a matter of communication, so work whose parts depend on each other belongs with one agent. Second, the lapses the paper names have direct analogues when a lead reads a subagent's report: accepting an unsupported claim (grounding) and accepting a conclusion without rechecking it (re-derivation). For current frontier models the tax measured here is small, about 6 to 8 percent for the strongest models tested, but those models are a generation or more behind the ones a current prompt targets, and the puzzles are easier than most real work.

## What the prompt applies

The paper supports the shared-context rule in the "While working" subsection most directly, and two of the rules for informational goals indirectly.

- **Keep work that needs shared context or a chain of linked decisions with a single agent.** The split-view control is the clearest evidence behind this rule among the prompt's sources. One agent given both halves as separate pieces lost 0.03 to 0.05 on the 0-to-1 scale; splitting the halves between two agents cost 3 to 10 times more on top of that. The cost came from the exchange. The paper tested two peers pooling facts, not a lead and a subagent, so the rule extends the finding to a structure it didn't measure.
- **Hand a goal to a subagent only when it is sizeable and independent of the rest.** Independence is what removes the tax: a subagent that needs nothing from the lead's context has nothing to pool. The paper didn't test independent subtasks, so this is an inference from its mechanism.
- **Before you rely on a citation, including one from a subagent, open the cited source and check it.** The rule matches two of the paper's four stages. Its grounding clause required every claim to name its source, and its re-derivation clause required the receiving agent to recompute before accepting. Dropping re-derivation erased most of the lift on the spatial tasks. The paper's agents could recompute from their own half of the data; a chat agent checking a subagent has to open the source, which is the same move in a different medium.
- **Judge sources by provenance and method; confirm load-bearing claims independently.** The paper doesn't bear on source judgment, but its finding that an invented value can't be recovered once accepted gives a reason to check claims before building on them.

On cheaper models, the paper says the tax is largest for them in every task family, and that pairing a cheap model with a strong one as an equal partner pulls the pair below the strong model working with a copy of itself. The prompt sends research to cheap Haiku subagents through the `spawn-research-subagent` skill and operational work to Sonnet. Its research subagents aren't equal partners: each reports once, and the lead verifies the load-bearing claims. That structure avoids the peer exchange where the paper found the cheap model's cost, but the paper never tested it, so the claim that cheap research subagents escape the tax is an inference.

## What the prompt omits

- **The four-clause protocol for agents that talk to each other.** The prompt has no rules for agent-to-agent dialogue, because its subagents don't converse. If a harness ever lets subagents message the lead or each other, the paper's clauses (cite sources, ask for missing facts, write out combined state, recompute before accepting) are the evidence-backed starting point.
- **Integration as an explicit step.** The paper's strongest predictor was whether someone wrote out the combined state before proposing an answer. The prompt asks the agent to say what it verified and what is still open, which is close, but it doesn't tell the lead to write out what each subagent established before concluding.
- **Task-type differences.** Spatial tasks with state that persists across steps lost far more than constraint puzzles. The prompt doesn't distinguish task types when deciding whether to delegate.
- **The cooperative-game theory.** The formal propositions explain why the tax is usually positive; they add nothing a prompt could act on.

## Conflicts with other references

When this file was drafted, the other new reference files did not exist yet, so the points below were first worked from the sources themselves. Before finishing, I read the relevant passages of `anthropic-opus-5-prompting.md`, `anthropic-opus-5-5-prompting.md`, `anthropic-harness-design.md`, and `progress-mirage.md`, which other reviewers had written in the meantime, and checked that the points below agree with them.

- **Dual-control multi-agent study (`dual-control-multi-agent.md`, arXiv 2610.27655).** This paper finds the tax shrinks as models get stronger. The dual-control study found its largest multi-agent losses on its strongest cells, though its test of the trend was inconclusive. The setups differ (peers pooling private facts here, an orchestrator with role agents serving a user there), and both agree that dividing coupled work costs accuracy at every capability level they tested.
- **Teams of coding agents (`coding-agent-teams.md`, arXiv 2610.17471).** That paper found teams worth their cost when cheap workers gathered facts for a strong lead. This paper found cheap partners costly in a peer exchange. The two fit together: the coding paper's lead did all the integration, which is the stage this paper found pairs skip.
- **Anthropic, Prompting Claude Opus 5 (`anthropic-opus-5-prompting.md`).** The guide says Opus 5 verifies its own work without being told and that explicit verification instructions cause over-verification. This paper found that a re-derivation requirement was the most valuable clause on its spatial tasks. The two concern different checks. The guide is about a model rechecking its own output; the paper is about one agent checking what another agent told it, which is the case the prompt's citation rule covers.
- **Progress mirage (`progress-mirage.md`, arXiv 2607.25152).** That paper reports that an agent judging its own progress accepted changes that made things worse. This paper's re-derivation finding points the same way from the receiving side: an answer accepted without independent recomputation is where pairs lose. Neither paper conflicts with the other. I read the progress-mirage abstract and its reference file's sections on subagent rechecks.

## Review notes

I read the main text in full and the appendix passages on the theory's remarks, the split-view control, the critic ablation, the cap analysis, the heterogeneous heatmap, the intervention clauses, and the hyperparameters. I did not read the appendix proofs, the failure-description clustering procedure, or the per-task tables in full. Several figures came through as garbled text; the per-model numbers I give (about 6 to 8 percent for the strongest models, about half for the weakest, 51 percent for gpt-4.1-nano) come from the heatmap's diagonal and the main text, and I did not try to read per-category values for individual models from the garbled main heatmap. I could not find which models the intervention ran on. The paper calls its grader deterministic in one place and describes a gpt-4o-mini extraction step at temperature 0.5 in another; the program does the scoring, but the extraction step adds some noise the paper doesn't quantify. The first version is dated August 23, 2026, within the recency window.
