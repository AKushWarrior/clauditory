# Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information

- **Source:** Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew, "Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information," Teachers College Record 121(11), pages 1 to 40, November 2019. DOI 10.1177/016146811912101102. An earlier version circulated as Stanford History Education Group Working Paper No. 2017.A1, September 2017 (SSRN abstract 3048994).
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed paper (an exploratory expert and novice study of human participants). It makes no claims about AI systems, so the repository's recency rule for AI-performance sources does not apply to it.
- **Where to find it:** https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102 (journal version); https://purl.stanford.edu/yk133ht8603 (Stanford Digital Repository); https://ssrn.com/abstract=3048994 (2017 working paper)
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/system/prompt.md`

## What the source covers

The study asks what skilled evaluation of online information looks like. Earlier research had documented how ordinary users, mostly students, judge websites by relevance, looks and search rank. Wineburg and McGrew set out to watch people who might be expected to do it well and to describe what separated the best performers from the rest. The paper is the origin of the term lateral reading, which later became the core of a large body of classroom curricula, and of the companion idea of click restraint.

### Participants

The authors recruited 45 people in three groups:

- 10 historians, all with a history Ph.D. and faculty posts at four-year institutions in California or Washington state, aged 39 to 69.
- 10 professional fact checkers employed at news and political fact-checking organizations, eight of them in New York or Washington, aged 23 to 60.
- 25 first-year Stanford undergraduates recruited with campus fliers and paid a $25 gift card, all aged 18 or 19 and all reporting at least four hours online a day.

The choice of groups carries the design. Historians are trained in sourcing, the habit of asking who wrote a document and under what circumstances, so they were a natural candidate for expert evaluators. Fact checkers do comparable work on screens for a living. Stanford students were chosen as a stringent test of the idea that young people who grew up online are naturally skilled at it.

### Tasks and procedure

Each participant worked through six tasks in about 45 minutes on a laptop with live websites, thinking aloud while a researcher watched and recorded the screen. Participants could search, open tabs and leave the page at will, and were reminded to do what they normally would. Each task had a time limit, with a one-minute warning. The paper reports three of the six tasks. A footnote in the working paper says the other three (quick judgments of four static sites, an open search on a historical question and finding a site's registrant) gave broadly consistent results, but it does not report them.

1. **Bullying (10 minutes).** Participants compared an article on the website of the American Academy of Pediatrics, the field's main professional body with about 64,000 members, with one on the website of the American College of Pediatricians, a small splinter group that broke away in 2002 over adoption by same-sex couples and has been widely criticized for its positions on LGBT issues. The College's site has an official-looking logo, an .org domain, an abstract, references to journals and a physician author.
2. **Minimum wage (up to 8 minutes).** Participants judged an article on minimumwage.com. The site's About page names the Employment Policies Institute, which calls itself a nonpartisan research nonprofit. Both are run by Berman and Company, a Washington public relations firm working for restaurant and hotel interests, and neither site says so. Participants who hadn't found the institute after five minutes were told the site was funded by someone else and given three more minutes to find out who.
3. **Vergara v. California (5 minutes).** After reading a short local TV news story that described a teacher-tenure lawsuit as nine students against the system, participants searched the open web to find who paid the plaintiffs' legal bill of more than $1 million. The answer is David Welch, a Silicon Valley entrepreneur, through his group Students Matter.

The authors scored each participant's conclusions with rubrics built after pilot testing: 0 to 2 for the bullying sites, 0 to 4 for minimum wage (higher scores for identifying the institute, then recognizing it as an advocacy group, then tracing it to the public relations firm) and 0 to 4 for Vergara (higher scores for finding Welch through a well-established outlet and confirming him with an additional source). Two coders scored about a quarter of the data and agreed 92 percent of the time (Cohen's kappa 0.90). The authors also logged how long people took to leave a page, which sites they visited and whether they looked into the people or groups behind a site. Group differences were tested with Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney tests, which suit small samples and ordinal scores.

### Findings

**Bullying.** Every fact checker judged the Academy the more reliable source and scored the maximum on both sites. Historians averaged 0.7 out of 2 on the College site and students 0.16. Fact checkers left the College's landing page after an average of 32 seconds to look the organization up elsewhere. Historians took about 88 seconds, and two never left. Nine of the 25 students never left; the 16 who did took about 100 seconds. Most historians took the College's surface at face value, and seven of the 10 cited its reference list as a reason to trust it, although several of those references were not to journals at all. Sixty percent of students picked the College as the more reliable site, mostly for its scientific look, its layout, its name and logo and its lack of ads. Only three students told the two organizations apart, and only one went looking in a way that resembled the fact checkers.

**Minimum wage.** Before prompting, fact checkers averaged 3.3 on the 0 to 4 rubric, historians 1.3 and students 0.52. Fact checkers found the parent institute in 51 seconds on average without help; historians took 3 minutes 40 seconds and six of the 10 needed the prompt, and students took more than 5 minutes and about four in five needed it. All 10 fact checkers traced the sites to the public relations firm, in about 3.5 minutes on average, after visiting about six sites each. Six historians got there, in about 6 minutes, and so did 40 percent of students, in about 7 minutes. The paper's central example is a fact checker who glanced at the article for a few seconds, went to the About page, found the institute's self-description unhelpful, searched for it, skipped the first several results in favor of a watchdog wiki, then followed that wiki's citation to a public radio story and searched it to confirm the claim before returning to the original site. Some historians tried to read laterally and failed for lack of search skill, for example by not putting a name in quotation marks, and then went back to analyzing the site's own research pages.

**Vergara.** Here students were fastest to Welch's name (1 minute 42 seconds on average, against about 2 minutes for both expert groups), but fact checkers scored best on quality: 3.6 against 2.4 for historians and 2.3 for students. All but one fact checker scored 3 or 4; 15 of 25 students scored 2 or lower. Only the difference between fact checkers and students was statistically significant. The authors contrast a student who found Welch's name on a partisan teachers' union site and accepted it without comment with a fact checker who spent nearly 20 seconds reading the results page before clicking anything, skipped the results tied to the parties, opened several news outlets and Wikipedia, and confirmed Welch's role in a second outlet. One historian spent most of the task reading the court filing, which never named Welch, and ran out of time.

### Concepts the paper introduces

- **Taking bearings:** getting a sense of what a source is before reading it closely. Fact checkers did this every time on the pediatrics task, historians about a quarter of the time and students almost never.
- **Lateral reading:** leaving a site and opening new tabs to learn what the rest of the web says about it and its claims. It contrasts with vertical reading, which judges a site from its own features (design, typos, citations, apparent logic).
- **Click restraint:** scanning a whole results page, reading the URLs and snippets and thinking about where each result comes from before clicking, instead of taking the top result.

The authors argue that lateral reading is a strong heuristic in the sense used by Gigerenzer, faster and often more accurate than elaborate procedures, while the cues most people relied on (domain, logo, nonprofit status, reference lists) are weak heuristics that resemble the representativeness bias. They also argue that the checklists common in media-literacy teaching, such as the CRAAP test, point readers at exactly the features that are easiest to fake, and may leave them more exposed. Lateral reading, they add, depends on knowledge of particular sources and their leanings, knowledge of how search results are ranked and gamed, and practical skills such as opening tabs and phrasing queries.

### Limits and strength of the evidence

The study is small, descriptive and exploratory, and the authors say so. The specific weaknesses:

- **Sample size and selection.** Ten people per expert group, all recruited by convenience. The authors concede that small samples exaggerate differences and that a larger sample might give different results. Two of the 10 historians behaved like fact checkers, so the group labels capture tendencies, not fixed traits.
- **Task selection favors the method.** Two of the three reported tasks used sites built to disguise who runs them, and the third asked a who-paid question. These are exactly the cases where finding out who is behind a source settles the question. The study does not test claims whose truth depends on the evidence itself rather than on its sponsor, and the authors note that other sites might have given other results.
- **Outcome definitions.** The rubrics reward identifying the sponsor and, in Vergara, confirming it with an additional well-established source. That is a reasonable standard, but it means the finding that fact checkers corroborate more is partly built into how quality was scored.
- **Artificial setting.** Researchers chose the sites, watched over participants' shoulders and imposed time limits. The authors defend the limits as realistic, since most real checks take seconds or minutes, but the tasks are still not what people meet in daily use.
- **No causal test.** The study shows that fact checkers did better and describes how they worked. It cannot show that the behaviors caused the better results, or that teaching them would help.
- **Reporting.** Three of six tasks are reported. The 2017 working paper, which is the version this review read in full, prints the same Kruskal-Wallis statistic for the Vergara task as for the first bullying comparison. That figure looks too large for the reported means, given that only one pairwise test was significant, and may be a transcription error.

Within those limits, the qualitative contrast is large and consistent across three tasks: the fact checkers' advantage on the two sponsor tasks is several-fold in both accuracy and time, and every fact checker reached the right judgment on the pediatrics sites. The descriptive account of how they did it is detailed and has held up well as a teaching target.

### Later causal evidence (context only)

The same group later tested whether lateral reading can be taught. Wineburg, Breakstone, McGrew, Smith and Ortega, "Lateral reading on the open Internet: A district-wide field study in high school government classes," Journal of Educational Psychology 114(5), 893 to 909, 2022 (DOI 10.1037/edu0000740), ran six one-hour lessons over three months in high school government classes in an urban district, with 271 students in treatment classrooms and 228 in control classrooms, and reported significantly greater growth in judging online credibility for the treatment group in a cluster-randomized, repeated-measures analysis. This review verified the citation details through Crossref and the design, sample sizes and direction of the result through the SSRN abstract. It did not read the full paper and did not verify effect sizes or how the outcome was measured.

### Transfer to a model following a system prompt

The study is about people with browsers, not language models, so it supports the prompt by analogy. The analogy is closer than it first appears. A model that reads retrieved pages sees text, not layouts, but text carries its own surface cues: confident analytic prose, reference lists, credentials, an institution's self-description and the order of search results. Those match the weak cues that misled historians and students. Recent AI work in this folder finds similar shortcuts in models (see the conflicts section), which makes the human finding more relevant, not less. What the study cannot show is that telling a model to read laterally makes it do so, or makes it more accurate. That is an empirical question about models, and the AI sources behind the prompt give only partial answers.

## What the prompt applies

The study shaped two bullets under "While working," in the list for informational goals.

- **Judge sources by provenance and method: who produced the claim, how they know it and what independent sources say about them.** The first and third parts restate lateral reading: find out who is behind a source, and find out by asking other sources instead of the source itself. This is the study's central finding, and the prompt applies it directly. The middle part, how the source knows the claim, goes beyond this study. Fact checkers in it largely skipped the content, and historians who examined an article's apparent method (abstract, references, physician author) were misled by it. The method clause comes from the historians' sourcing tradition and from general research practice, and it carries a risk the study illustrates: method can be judged from its appearance. The prompt does not say how to tell real method from its look.
- **Polish, popularity and authority cues are weak signals.** Polish and authority cues map directly onto the study's weak heuristics: logo, domain, nonprofit status, scientific formatting, references and credentials. Popularity has partial support. The study reports that students trusted top-ranked search results and that fact checkers knew ranking can be gamed, and click restraint is a response to that. The study did not measure popularity as such; that part of the bullet rests mainly on the AI evidence.
- **Weigh each source on its own record instead of discounting every source equally.** The study supports this indirectly. Fact checkers were not uniformly skeptical: every one of them trusted the Academy without reservation once its standing was clear, and they used Wikipedia and a watchdog wiki as stepping stones while checking those against news outlets. Their skepticism was selective. This sentence was added mainly in response to AI evidence that verification instructions make models distrust everything (see Pradhan below), and the study gives that sentence a human model of what selective trust looks like.
- **Confirm load-bearing claims with an independent second source where you can.** The fact checkers did this. In the minimum wage task a checker confirmed a watchdog site's claim in the public radio story it cited; in Vergara a checker found Welch on Wikipedia and confirmed him in a national newspaper. The Vergara rubric gave its top score for exactly this pattern. The caveat above applies: the study built corroboration into its quality score, so it shows that experts corroborate more than it shows that corroboration produced their accuracy.
- **Before you rely on a citation, open the cited source and check that it says what the citation claims.** The watchdog-wiki episode is a human instance: the checker followed the wiki's citation and searched the cited story to confirm it. This is an illustration, not a tested finding, and the prompt's main support for the rule is AI evidence on citation accuracy (Onweller below).

A looser connection: the prompt's instruction to change the angle when an approach stops producing information fits the historians who stayed with an unproductive method, one rereading a court filing and another retreating to the site's own pages after a failed search. The study does not frame this as a rule, and the prompt bullet has other sources.

## What the prompt omits

- **Click restraint.** The prompt does not tell the model to read the whole results list and its snippets before opening a page. A model with a search tool sees results as text, and the habit would plausibly transfer, but it would add another rule for a single study's concept that was illustrated by examples and never counted. The provenance bullet covers its purpose.
- **Taking bearings and the instruction to leave a site quickly.** Speed was part of the fact checkers' advantage, and the time pressure was a human one. A model's cost of reading is different, and the prompt's parallelism and stopping rules already govern effort.
- **Search technique.** The study found that some historians failed at lateral reading because they couldn't phrase queries, for instance with quotation marks. Tool use and query writing are left to the model.
- **Knowledge of particular sources.** Fact checkers relied on knowing where outlets sit politically and which kinds of organization deserve trust. A model brings its own (imperfect) prior knowledge of sources, and the prompt does not try to supply a list.
- **The critique of checklists.** The prompt states principles instead of a checklist, which follows the paper's argument in spirit, but it does not cite or restate the argument.
- **The education findings and the 2022 intervention.** These concern teaching students and have no direct counterpart in a system prompt.

## Conflicts with other references

None of the files cited in this section existed in `chat/system/references/` when this file was written, so this section works from the local full texts of the sources.

- **Pradhan and Goley, "Trust, but Don't Verify" (`pradhan-trust-dont-verify.md`, arXiv 2606.05403).** Pradhan and Goley find that models give fabricated statistics the same weight as valid ones when the source is written in the register of careful analysis, and that prompts telling models to check statistics, even a checklist naming the exact anomalies, make them distrust valid and invalid sources alike. The two papers agree on the mechanism: historians in the study trusted the College's article because it looked like science, which is the human version of a methodology-register shortcut. They point different ways on remedies. Wineburg and McGrew suggest the cure is to check what others say about a source; Pradhan suggests that an instruction to scrutinize can produce blanket skepticism instead of discernment. The prompt answers with its last sentence on sources, asking for selective weighing instead of equal discounting, and the fact checkers' selective trust in the Academy is the human model for it. Whether that sentence achieves selectivity in a model is untested; Pradhan's evidence suggests it may not, and the prompt should be read as aiming at the right behavior without proof that a prompt can produce it.
- **Ashkinaze et al., "Information Discernment in Large Language Models" (`ashkinaze-information-discernment.md`, arXiv 2607.19355).** Across 13 models and about 670,000 trials, models updated about twice as much on a source's popularity as on its reliability. This is the main support for listing popularity as a weak signal; the human study supports it only through students' trust in top search results. The two sources agree in direction. They differ in what helps: Ashkinaze found that asking a model to rate a source's reliability before updating improved source discernment somewhat, while Wineburg and McGrew found that experts did better by consulting other sources than by judging a source from the inside. The prompt asks for both, since its "what independent sources say about them" clause adds the lateral step to the self-assessment Ashkinaze tested.
- **Onweller et al., "Cited but Not Verified" (`onweller-cited-not-verified.md`, arXiv 2605.06635).** Onweller finds that deep research agents' citations usually resolve and are usually on topic but support the claim only 39 to 77 percent of the time, and that accuracy fell by about 42 percent on average as tool calls grew from 2 to 150. This agrees with the study's picture of experts who read less and check more, and it is the direct evidence for the prompt's rule on opening cited sources. There is no real conflict; the human study supplies an example and Onweller supplies the measurement.
