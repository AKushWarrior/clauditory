# Is Deep Research Reliable? Misleading Knowledge Induces False Conclusions

- **Source:** Pengyu Zhu, Lijun Li, Longju Yang, Sen Su, and Jing Shao (Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, and Chongqing University of Posts and Telecommunications), "Is Deep Research Reliable? Misleading Knowledge Induces False Conclusions," arXiv preprint, version 1 submitted July 23, 2026, version 2 July 30, 2026, arXiv 2607.20891
- **Type:** preprint (not peer-reviewed)
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20891
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/system/prompt.md`

## What the source covers

The paper asks whether deep research agents, which plan, search, read, and write long reports over many steps, can resist a document that looks credible but supports a false conclusion. The authors build a framework they call MisKnow-Agent that manufactures such documents for each research task, plant them in the agents' search results, and count how often the final report adopts the false conclusion as its own. Their main result is that one planted document takes the adoption rate from zero to just over half. Authority and academic presentation make adoption more likely, search rank barely matters, documents that arrive just before the agent writes its report are adopted most often, and a verification prompt reduces adoption without stopping it.

### How the misleading documents were built

The tasks are 100 research questions from DeepResearch Bench. For each task, a generator model (DeepSeek-V4 Pro, chosen after a qualitative pilot against three other open models) writes a blueprint. The blueprint holds one false conclusion with specific numbers, a pool of invented institutions at high, medium, and low authority, and a rule for judging adoption. The model then writes documents that state the same false conclusion with the same numbers in four styles (academic paper, news article, blog post, and social media post), with five institutions per authority tier. That gives up to 60 documents per task. The generation prompt tells the model to keep writing quality equal across tiers, so that only the publisher name, the web address, and the style signal authority. Every invented address follows a pattern by tier: high-authority documents get plausible .edu or journal-like .org addresses, and low-authority ones get blog, forum, or newsletter addresses.

Each candidate then goes to five search-enabled verifier models (GLM-5, Kimi 2.6, DeepSeek-V4 Pro, Qwen3.5-397B, and Intern-S1-Pro). A document is kept only if all five, after searching the web, judge its central claim false. Candidates that any verifier judged real were sent back for rewriting until they failed every check or ran out of rewrites. Human screening removed low-quality documents, and the authors audited every task's false conclusion against primary sources and confirmed all 100 as false. The final corpus has 5,933 documents, so about 1 percent of the 6,000 possible documents were dropped.

The false conclusions are of a particular kind. The worked example in the appendix invents a software feature, a review in a named journal that never appeared, and an adoption figure from a survey that doesn't exist. Claims like these are false in a way that a targeted search can expose, which is why all five verifiers caught them.

### How the agents were tested

The main experiments pair two open source research frameworks, DeerFlow and WebThinker, with three open models: DeepSeek-V4 Pro, Qwen3.5-397B, and Intern-S1-Pro, which score 44, 34, and 22 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. Both frameworks search through the same commercial search service and see the top 10 results per query. Planted documents go into each framework's own retrieval pool rather than onto the public web. All runs use temperature 0. The default condition is a high-authority, paper-style document, at most three planted documents per run, and one factor changed at a time. Gemini Deep Research is the one closed system. Its retrieval can't be configured, so the planted documents went in as uploaded local files while its own web search stayed on. The paper doesn't say which Gemini model or version ran.

The outcome is the false-conclusion adoption rate: the share of valid, completed reports whose own conclusion or recommendation endorses the false conclusion. A report that quotes the planted claim, attributes it, calls it uncertain or refutes it doesn't count. Runs that failed because of tool or API errors were rerun, and each report was checked for validity by hand. DeepSeek-V4 Pro was the judge. On a random sample of 300 reports labeled by two blinded annotators, it agreed with the adjudicated human label 99.7 percent of the time (Cohen's kappa 0.993).

### Main findings

- **One document is enough.** With no planted documents, every configuration avoided the false conclusion. One planted document raised the mean adoption rate across the six open configurations to 54.7 percent. More documents added little: 59.5 percent with two, 61.0 percent with three, 60.3 percent with four, and 58.3 percent with five. No configuration showed a steady rise with dose. Gemini Deep Research behaved differently: it rose from 27 percent with one document to 54 percent with three and then leveled off between 50 and 56 percent.
- **Search rank barely matters.** Putting the planted documents at the front, back, or their natural position in a result list gave mean adoption rates of 66.2, 64.5, and 65.5 percent, and no configuration varied by more than 6 percentage points. Once a document is retrieved, its place in the list does little to protect against it.
- **Timing matters most.** Planting the documents in the first search gave 40.5 percent adoption, and making them available in later searches gave 44.2 percent. Appending them to the last search before the agent wrote its report gave 85.5 percent, with a range of 77 to 94 percent across configurations. WebThinker resisted early exposure better than DeerFlow (27.7 percent against 53.3 percent at the first search) but not late exposure (87.7 percent against 83.3 percent). The authors read this as later research displacing early misleading evidence, not as the agent rejecting it on inspection.
- **Authority and style persuade.** High-, medium-, and low-authority sources gave mean adoption rates of 61.0, 57.7, and 46.2 percent. Low authority was lower than high in every configuration, but the order was strictly descending in only four of six. Style had the larger and more consistent effect: 61.0 percent for paper-style documents, 53.0 for blogs, 44.0 for news, and 37.5 for social posts, with papers highest and posts lowest in all six configurations. The gap between paper and post, 23.5 points, exceeds the 14.8-point gap between high and low authority. Gemini Deep Research showed a much steeper authority effect: 54 percent for high authority, 46 for medium, and 8 for low.
- **Framework and model interact.** Under the default condition, adoption ranged from 50 to 76 percent across the six configurations, and averaged 67 percent for DeerFlow and 55 percent for WebThinker. A more capable model did not mean less adoption: Qwen3.5-397B, the middle model by the index, had the lowest rates in both frameworks. An appendix splits the rate into how often a planted document was retrieved at all (72 to 98 percent of tasks) and how often a report adopted the false conclusion once its agent had retrieved one (51 to 81 percent). In DeerFlow, differences between models came almost entirely from adoption after retrieval.

### Defenses

The authors tested two defenses on DeerFlow under the default condition. The pre-research defense appends a fixed instruction to the user's query. In substance it tells the agent to treat retrieved information as unverified until reliable evidence supports it. A claim that decides the conclusion may be used only when a primary source or independent sources that don't just repeat the same claim support it. The agent should qualify or drop claims resting on weak, outdated, or conflicting evidence, match stated confidence to the evidence, and not trust a claim because it is repeated, well written or presented with authority. The post-research defense runs a second, search-enabled agent on the same model. That agent picks out the report's material claims, searches for evidence on each in the same contaminated retrieval pool, and keeps, corrects, qualifies, or removes each claim.

Against baselines of 60 to 76 percent, the pre-research defense brought adoption to 37 to 57 percent, the post-research defense to 20 to 58 percent, and the combination to 15 to 62 percent. The combination helped most for DeepSeek-V4 Pro and Qwen3.5-397B but did worse for Intern-S1-Pro (62 percent) than either defense alone (57 and 58 percent). The authors suggest that the checking agent re-retrieved the planted documents from the same pool and accepted them again. Their conclusion is that checking has to happen whenever evidence enters the agent's working state, not only once at the end.

### Strength of the evidence

The design is careful in the ways that matter for its central claim. The no-injection control at zero ties adoption to the planted documents. One factor changes at a time. The judge agrees with humans almost perfectly on 300 reports. The authors audited every false conclusion. The finding that a single credible-looking document flips the conclusion about half the time is well supported for these systems.

The limits are substantial for a reader who wants to apply the result to a current frontier model.

- **The models are not the frontier.** The six main configurations run on open models scoring 22 to 44 on the intelligence index. The one closed system is Gemini Deep Research in an unstated version, tested through file upload, a channel users may trust more than open web results. No Anthropic or OpenAI model was tested.
- **No measure of over-skepticism.** The paper counts only adoption of false conclusions. It never plants true documents, so it can't show whether the verification prompt made agents reject or hedge on good evidence as well. A defense that lowers adoption by making the agent doubt everything would score well here.
- **Small samples per cell.** Each condition rests on about 100 tasks per configuration. The paper reports a statistical test only for the framework comparison, where the difference held for one of three models. Gaps of a few points, such as the rank conditions, are within noise.
- **One family of models plays several roles.** DeepSeek-V4 Pro generated the documents, served as one of the five verifiers, judged adoption, and ran as one of the research backbones. The high agreement with humans limits the concern for judging, but the documents may suit that model's own sense of credibility.
- **The false claims are checkable.** Every planted claim invents things a search can show don't exist, and five search-enabled models caught every one when asked directly. The study measures failure to use a capability the models have, not inability. That distinction makes the result more pointed but says nothing about subtler errors, such as a real study misreported.
- **The defense prompt went in the user turn.** The pre-research instruction was appended to the query, not placed in a system prompt, and tested on one framework. The judge prompt printed in the appendix refers to uploaded local files, which fits the Gemini setup; the paper doesn't say whether the open source runs used a different wording.

For a frontier model under a system prompt like this repository's, the findings that should transfer are qualitative. A source's style and claimed authority move adoption. A single source can decide a conclusion. Late evidence is the least examined. An instruction to seek independent corroboration helps only partly. The size of each effect is specific to these systems.

## What the prompt applies

**Judging sources by provenance and method (Working loop, While working, informational goals, second bullet).** The prompt tells the model to judge a source by who produced the claim and how they know it, to treat polish, popularity, and authority cues as weak signals, and to weigh each source on its own record. The paper supplies the reason for naming polish and authority. Paper-style presentation raised adoption by 23.5 points over social posts, and high authority by 14.8 points over low, with the writing quality held constant, so these cues moved the agents without any change in substance. The paper shows the cues persuade; it doesn't test whether an instruction to discount them works, so the rule rests on the diagnosis, not on a tested fix. The paper's verification prompt did include a line against trusting well-written or authoritative-looking claims, but it was tested only as part of the whole instruction.

**Independent corroboration (same section, third bullet, first sentence).** The prompt asks the model to confirm load-bearing claims with an independent second source where it can. This rule is the closest match in the paper. Its pre-research defense asks for the same thing in nearly the same terms: conclusion-critical claims need a primary source or independent sources that don't repeat the same unsupported claim. That instruction cut adoption from 60 to 76 percent down to 37 to 57 percent. Two gaps remain. The paper tested the instruction in the user turn, on open models in one framework, so its support for a system-prompt rule on a frontier model is indirect. And the instruction left adoption above a third in every configuration, so the paper supports the rule as a partial safeguard, not as a solution.

**Load-bearing claims as the unit of checking (same bullet).** The prompt limits corroboration to load-bearing claims instead of every claim. The paper's defenses make the same choice: both target claims that decide the conclusion. The paper offers no comparison with checking every claim, so the scoping is a design choice that both defenses share and the paper did not test.

**Opening a cited source (same bullet, second sentence).** The prompt asks the model to open a cited source and check that it says what the citation claims. The paper explains why this check alone isn't enough. Every planted document did say exactly what an agent would cite it for. An agent that opened it would find the citation accurate. The protection against this failure comes from the corroboration rule and the provenance rule, not from opening the source. The paper therefore supports keeping all three rules side by side; the Onweller reference covers the citation check itself.

**Changing the angle (same section, fourth bullet).** The prompt tells the model to change its angle when an approach stops producing new information. The paper bears on this only indirectly. Its post-research checker searched the same contaminated pool and in one configuration accepted the planted documents again, and early planted documents were displaced only when later research brought in other evidence. Both observations suggest that more of the same search in the same place doesn't correct an error, while evidence from elsewhere can. The paper never tests an instruction to change search strategy.

**Calibrated reporting (Composing answers, third and fourth bullets).** The prompt asks the model to hedge only uncertain inferences and to say what it verified and what it couldn't check. The paper's verification prompt includes the same calibration step: separate well-supported findings from uncertain ones and qualify or drop claims on weak evidence. Its judge counted a report that qualified the false claim as not adopting it. The calibration step was not tested on its own.

The prompt's general stance that every assertion is a hypothesis to check (Handling claims, first bullet) echoes the paper's instruction to treat retrieved information as unverified until supported. The prompt aims that rule mostly at user claims and memory, which the paper doesn't study.

## What the prompt omits

**Checking at each point where evidence enters.** The paper's main design lesson is that checking once at the end isn't enough. Evidence that arrives just before the report is written is adopted 85.5 percent of the time, and a final checking pass in the same environment can re-accept it. The prompt states its verification rules without saying when they apply. Its stated reason for the rules (load-bearing claims) implies checking before relying on a claim, which covers the paper's point in part. An explicit rule about late-arriving evidence would rest on one study of open models, so leaving it implicit is defensible.

**The post-research checking agent.** The prompt doesn't ask for a separate agent to re-verify a finished report, and it tells the model not to use subagents to recheck its own work and, for operational goals, not to add a separate review pass. The paper's checking agent helped on average but made things worse in one configuration, and the Opus 5 prompting guide warns against verification subagents on that model. The omission fits both.

**Search rank and dose.** The findings that rank barely matters and that one document suffices are diagnostic. They don't translate into an instruction a model can follow, beyond the general rule not to trust a claim because it ranks high or appears often, which the provenance rule already covers.

**Framework design.** Differences between DeerFlow and WebThinker, such as how aggressively each compresses retrieved evidence, are harness choices outside a system prompt's reach.

## Conflicts with other references

The reference files for `pradhan-trust-dont-verify.md`, `khurana-authoritybench.md`, `ashkinaze-information-discernment.md`, `onweller-cited-not-verified.md`, and `wineburg-mcgrew-lateral-reading.md` were not in the folder when this file was written, except where noted, so the points below come from the sources' abstracts and the passages named.

- **Pradhan and Goley, "Trust, but Don't Verify" (arXiv 2606.05403).** That paper reports the same split this one finds: models can spot fabricated statistics in isolation but don't use that ability when they synthesize several sources. It adds a finding this paper can't test. Prompting fixes, even a checklist naming the exact checks, produced blanket skepticism instead of selective judgment. Zhu and colleagues measured only false adoptions, so their verification prompt's gains may include some of the same blanket doubt. The prompt's answer is the instruction to weigh each source on its own record instead of discounting every source equally, which targets the failure Pradhan and Goley describe. Neither paper tests that wording.
- **Khurana and colleagues, AuthorityBench (arXiv 2606.13104), and Ashkinaze and colleagues, information discernment (arXiv 2607.19355).** Both point the same way as this paper. AuthorityBench finds that adding a citation, real or fabricated, raises hallucination rates, and Ashkinaze and colleagues find that models rely on a source's popularity about twice as much as on its reliability. Together with this paper's authority and style effects, they support the prompt's claim that authority and popularity cues are weak signals. No conflict.
- **Onweller and colleagues, "Cited but Not Verified" (arXiv 2605.06635).** Onweller and colleagues find that citations often fail to support the claims attached to them, which motivates opening the source. This paper shows a case where the citation is accurate and the source is wrong. The two findings complement each other: the first motivates the prompt's citation check and the second its corroboration and provenance rules.
- **Wineburg and McGrew, lateral reading (2019).** Professional fact-checkers judged unfamiliar sites by leaving them and checking what other sources said about the publisher. This paper's planted documents carry invented institutions, so a lateral check on the publisher would expose them. The study supports the prompt's provenance rule and fits this paper's results. It concerns human readers, not models, and was not reread for this file.
- **Anthropic prompting guides (`anthropic-prompting-best-practices.md`; Prompting Claude Opus 5).** The Opus 5 guide says that model checks its own work unprompted and that explicit verification instructions cause over-verification. This paper's verification prompt is an explicit instruction that helped, but on open models that may not check their sources unprompted. The guide's warning concerns checking one's own work; the prompt's corroboration rule concerns external sources, and the prompt keeps it. Whether it causes wasted searching on Opus 5 or later models is untested here.

## Review notes

I read the full text of version 2 from the local copy. That includes the appendixes on generator selection, judge agreement, the audit of false conclusions, the defense prompt, the checking agent, the worked example, the framework and model breakdown, the rank and timing setup, and the prompt templates. The figures were not available as images, so numbers in the text and tables are the basis for every figure cited here; values the paper shows only in charts were not checked. I confirmed the submission dates from the local copy of the arXiv abstract page: version 1 on July 23, 2026, within the recency window. I did not reach the code or the data set. The paper doesn't name the Gemini model or say how many reports each Gemini condition used. The judge prompt's reference to local files may mean the published template is the Gemini variant; I couldn't confirm what the open source runs used. The conflict notes on Pradhan, Khurana, and Ashkinaze rest on their abstracts and introductions, not full readings, and the Wineburg and McGrew notes rest on the study's widely reported findings.
