# When Do Agent Loops Mistake Stagnation for Progress? Self-Evaluation Bias and Externally Grounded Verification in Long-Running Autonomous LLM Agent Loops

- **Source:** Hyundoo Park and Byungho Choi, "When Do Agent Loops Mistake Stagnation for Progress? Self-Evaluation Bias and Externally Grounded Verification in Long-Running Autonomous LLM Agent Loops," arXiv preprint, 2026, arXiv 2607.25152 (version 1 posted 27 July 2026; version 2 posted 29 September 2026). The authors label it a preliminary draft.
- **Type:** preprint (not peer-reviewed); a small controlled pilot by two independent researchers, plus one anonymized field observation
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25152
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/system/prompt.md`

## What the source covers

The paper shows, in a small controlled pilot, that an agent loop that accepts its own claims of progress keeps deploying changes that make things worse, that a stronger and more critical model reading the same transcript does only somewhat better, and that the gap closes when the success criterion is visible in the work itself. The authors conclude that what limits an evaluator is what it can see, not how capable it is. They call the failure the progress mirage: a loop that records steady advancement while the outcome it exists to improve stalls or falls.

### Setup

The task asks an agent to edit a signup-funnel web app (landing page, funnel steps and copy) to raise signups. There is no single correct answer, and no unit test can confirm success. Success is measured by a synthetic user simulator that reads four properties of the deployed artifact (whether a clear call to action exists, page size, the number of funnel steps and copy length), applies a weighted logistic model and returns a conversion count over a fixed cohort. Because the same random numbers are reused across cycles, the sign of every change is deterministic. The agent and the simulator sit on separate networks. At the start of every run the authors confirm the agent cannot reach the simulator, and in every cycle they scan the agent's output for a secret string the simulator holds; it never appeared.

The independent variable is the gate that decides whether each change is kept, implemented in three arms:

1. **Self:** accepts a cycle when the agent edits files and gives a reason. Because the agent claimed an improvement every time, this arm behaved as accept-all.
2. **Judge:** a stronger model reads the agent's summary, the full text of every produced file, the diff against the deployed version and its own past verdicts, under a prompt that demands evidence, defaults to rejection and warns that decorative edits can hurt conversion. It never sees the simulator.
3. **Out-of-band:** accepts only when the simulator's measured change is positive, and returns the numbers to the agent.

Rejected changes are rolled back. Each arm ran three repetitions of six cycles from a baseline of 33.3 conversions. A boundary task used the same arms on a different criterion: five structural requirements (a form, an email field, a submit control, exactly three steps and copy of 40 to 80 words) that can be checked from the artifact alone. A sign-only variant of the out-of-band arm returned only the accept or reject verdict, without numbers. The text does not name the agent model or the judge model; it says only that the agent is a frontier model and the judge is stronger.

### Findings

- **Self-report carried no signal.** The agent claimed an improvement in all 54 cycles across the three arms, and 56 percent of those cycles changed conversions by zero or less. The self arm accepted all 18 of its cycles. Its deployed state rose to a mean peak of 95.0 and then slid to 76.7, a 19 percent loss, as later regressions were accepted. The slide happened in all three repetitions.
- **A strong judge was better but not good enough.** The judge accepted 40 percent of regressions and 63 percent of real improvements. Of the cycles it accepted, 44 percent were regressions, and it rejected 37.5 percent of real improvements. Its deployed state peaked at 100.0 and ended at 86.7. The authors had preregistered the hypothesis that a strong judge would close the gap to the out-of-band arm to within 0.05; the gap was 0.44, so they rejected it. The judge's written reasons were sound conversion-optimization advice; it accepted a styled hero section that cut conversions by 23 and rejected a step removal that added 7. Nothing in the transcript told it which way the simulator would respond.
- **Grounding preserved progress.** The out-of-band arm ended at 113.0, equal to its peak, in every repetition. The authors stress that its zero acceptance-error rate and its never-falling deployed state follow from the gate rule by construction and are not findings.
- **The gap vanished when the criterion was visible.** On the boundary task, the judge accepted no unproductive cycle and rejected no productive one, matching the out-of-band arm. The self arm still accepted unproductive cycles at a rate of 0.50, because it checks nothing.
- **The gate mattered more than the numbers.** The sign-only variant reached a mean of 110.0, against 113.0 with full feedback, with overlapping ranges. The share of candidate changes that made things worse was 0.56 in all three arms and 0.50 in the sign-only variant, so grounding the gate did not make the agent propose better changes; it stopped bad ones from being kept.
- **Field observation.** Before the pilot, an autonomous loop ran a consumer web service for six weeks and 61 cycles, accepting all 57 evaluated cycles on the strength of the analysis each produced, while every external ledger stayed flat. When acceptance moved to real outcomes checked by a separate process, the loop switched from analysis to shipping. The authors present this as motivation, not evidence.

The paper also proposes four supporting mechanisms (append-only state, handoff through files, periodic attacks on the agent's own assumptions and a scheduler that restarts dead sessions). The authors say plainly that the pilot did not test any of them.

### Strength of the evidence

The design is cleaner than most work in this area. It manipulates one variable, verifies isolation in every run, includes an adversarial hypothesis that the authors expected might sink their claim and adds a control task on which the claim should fail. It also discloses its weak points: the out-of-band zero is structural, the definition of the main metric changed after data collection (results are reported under both definitions), and the simulator rewards some changes a person would reject, such as dropping email verification.

The sample is small: three repetitions per arm, 18 cycles each, with intervals that the authors say are observed ranges, not confidence intervals. There is one task family, one agent model and one judge model, neither named. The preregistration was never frozen. The simulator has four features with weights chosen so that many plausible edits hurt; the authors claim only that the direction of the gap generalizes, not its size, and cite the A/B-testing literature to argue that plausible ideas often fail in real experiments. The self arm is an accept-all rule, not an agent asked to check its own work, so it tests trusting a claim, not careful self-verification. The paper does not test an agent told to confirm its claims against observable evidence; the nearest analogue is the judge on the boundary task, which did well.

For a chat model following a system prompt, the transfer is partial. A chat agent cannot build a separate evaluator with its own access to the world. What it can do is tell apart goals whose success shows up in its tool output (a test result, a file's contents, a rendered page) from goals whose success lies beyond its reach (whether a message persuaded someone, whether a change will raise sales). The boundary result says a capable model judges the first kind well from what it can see. The main result says no amount of judgment settles the second kind.

## What the prompt applies

**External evidence of completion (Working loop, While working, operational goals, third bullet).** The prompt counts a goal as done only when something outside the model's own judgment shows it, and tells the model to use the evidence the work already produces instead of adding a separate review pass. The first sentence is the paper's central claim, that grounding matters more than judgment, and its finding that the agent's claims of improvement were uninformative. The prompt's examples (passing tests, the saved file, the rendered page) are the class of evidence the paper calls visible in the transcript, where it found a competent reader suffices. So the prompt applies the paper inside its boundary condition: for those goals, an observation from a tool is the outside evidence, and the same model reading it is enough. The gap is the other side of the boundary. The paper's remedy for goals whose success lives in the world is a separate process with its own access to that world, and a prompt cannot create one. For such goals the prompt relies on two other lines: the stopping rule allows a goal to end when the model has shown it cannot be met, and the reporting bullet asks the model to say what it could not check. The prompt does not say outright that a goal with no reachable evidence should be reported as unverified rather than done; the combination implies it.

**No subagents to recheck the model's own work (Working loop, While working, third bullet).** The judge result supplies a mechanism for this rule, whose main source is the Claude Opus 5 guide. A second model reading the same transcript added no grounding where the signal was out of view and added nothing a capable first reader lacked where it was in view. The paper shows such a reviewer is insufficient, not useless: the judge did filter many regressions and ended about 10 points above the self arm. The rule trades that partial gain for cost and latency, which is reasonable for chat but is a judgment the paper does not test.

**Goals judged by what the user is trying to accomplish (Working loop, Before starting, first two bullets).** The field case shows a loop that rewarded producing analysis and so never stopped on the right condition. Defining goals by the user's purpose, and treating that list as the stopping rule, is the prompt-level counterpart of the paper's advice to reward outcomes over process. The support is indirect: the paper's remedy is a harness-level reward function, not a self-written goal list.

**Reporting what was and was not verified (Composing answers, fourth bullet).** The progress mirage is a gap between the record and the world. Asking the model to say how it verified each result, and what it could not check, keeps that gap visible to the user, who can act as the out-of-band check the model lacks.

## What the prompt omits

The architecture itself is left out: the separately scheduled evaluator, rejection files that the next cycle must read first, append-only state, file-based handoff, assumption attacks and the restart scheduler. These are harness components that a system prompt cannot install, and the four supporting mechanisms are untested even in the paper.

The prompt also does not warn that outside evidence can be the wrong evidence. The paper's simulator counted only conversions, so it rewarded removing email verification, a change the judge rightly rejected. A passing test can likewise test the wrong thing. The prompt's instruction to judge goals by what the user is trying to accomplish addresses this in part, but it gives no explicit rule about checking that the evidence measures the goal.

The paper's warning about over-constraint applies to the prompt as well. An outcome-only gate rejected legitimate work that moved no metric, such as refactoring or exploratory analysis. The prompt limits its completion criterion to operational goals and judges informational goals by sources and method, which avoids forcing outside evidence onto analysis that cannot produce any.

## Conflicts with other references

**Prompting Claude Opus 5 (`anthropic-opus-5-prompting.md`; written after this section, which works from the source text): is the completion criterion a verification instruction?** The guide says Claude Opus 5 verifies its own work without being told, and that explicit verification instructions cause over-verification and should be removed. Its examples are adding a final verification step for non-trivial tasks, verifying with a subagent, double-checking an answer and re-verifying before responding. It reports that removing them cuts wasted tokens with no loss in quality, and gives no numbers.

Read against this paper, the guide's examples and the prompt's line do different things. Each of the guide's examples asks for more of the model's own judgment: a second pass over work it has already assessed. This paper suggests such a pass has little value on either side of its boundary. Where the success criterion is visible, a capable model already judges it correctly (the boundary task), so a second look adds little. Where the criterion is out of view, even a stronger and more critical reader failed (the main task), so a second look cannot fix it. Both results agree with the guide that repeating the check is waste.

The prompt's line does not add a check. It sets what may count as completion: an observation produced outside the model's judgment. Its second sentence says so outright, telling the model to use the evidence the work already produces and not to add a separate review pass, and the same prompt forbids using subagents to recheck its own work. On checkable goals the evidence is usually already in the transcript when the work ends (the output of a test run, a file read after an edit), so the criterion should cost little beyond what Claude Opus 5 does on its own. On this reading the prompt is consistent with both sources: it drops repeated self-review, as the guide advises, and ties completion to grounded evidence, as the paper advises.

An earlier wording added that the model's sense of being finished is not evidence, which could have been read as a general reason to distrust and re-run. The current second sentence replaces it and points the other way, so that risk is mostly closed, though the wording is untested. One risk remains: when the work does not produce an observation on its own, as when sending a message, the model has to settle for whatever the action returns (a delivery receipt, a sent item) or report the goal as unverified. The prompt's ban on a separate review pass means it will not add a check there, which keeps the cost down at the price of some unverified completions. The guide's claim covers Claude Opus 5; the Opus 5.5 guide says Opus 5 prompts carry over and itself recommends treating a text-only end of turn as a report, not as proof the task is done, so the direction should hold for the newer model. Neither guide nor paper tested the prompt's wording. Whether it is consistent in practice can be settled by running the prompt with and without the line and comparing tool calls, tokens and the rate of false completion claims.

**Prompting Claude Opus 5.5 (`anthropic-opus-5-5-prompting.md`; written after this section, which works from the parts of the source I read).** For unattended runs the guide suggests stating the completion condition up front and having a separate, smaller model check the conversation against it at the end of each turn. In this paper's terms that checker is an in-band judge. It should work for conditions visible in the transcript (the boundary task) and fail for conditions that depend on the world outside it. The guide does not draw that line.

**Harness design for long-running application development (`anthropic-harness-design.md`; written after this section, which works from the local copy of the post).** The post reports that agents praise their own work, that a separate skeptical evaluator is easier to tune than a self-critical generator, and that its evaluator drove the running application through a browser automation tool before grading. That evaluator had its own access to the artifact, which fits this paper's grounding claim. It seems to conflict with the prompt's ban on rechecking subagents, but the two describe different things: the harness evaluator gathered new observations, while the prompt bans a subagent that rereads the model's work. The prompt's wording does not draw that distinction, so a subagent that opens a deployed page to collect evidence the main agent cannot reach falls in a gray area. The post also says its evaluator was worth the cost only on tasks beyond what the model did reliably alone, which agrees with this paper's section on costs and with the Opus 5 guide.

**FrontierChallenge (`frontierchallenge.md`) and Push Your Agent (`pushbench.md`).** All three papers find an agent's own report of success unreliable. This paper's observation that grounding left the agent's rate of bad proposals unchanged lines up with PushBench, where blocking false completion recovered little success. Grounding protects what gets claimed and kept. It does not by itself make the agent get more done.

**Claude prompting best practices (`anthropic-prompting-best-practices.md`).** That file has been revised to describe the completion bar instead of a check before marking a goal done, and it resolves the over-verification tension the same way this section does: the prompt drops the self-check step and keeps a bar that requires outside evidence.

## Review notes

I read the full text from a local copy of the PDF, which the arXiv listing shows as version 2; the extracted text does not state its version, so I assume it matches the current listing. The appendix the paper cites for its preregistration and apparatus history was not in the copy I read, and I did not open the public repository, so I could not check the judge prompt, the amendments or the model identities. The extracted tables were partly garbled; I took numbers from the prose where the two overlapped. The 10-point gap between the judge and self arms is my subtraction of the reported final values. For the conflicts section I read the Claude Opus 5 guide in full, the Claude Opus 5.5 guide only in part (its section on unattended runs and a keyword search) and the harness post's sections on self-evaluation and the evaluator. I did not read the contemporaneous studies the paper compares itself with, such as Advani's work on false success.
