# SWAY: A Counterfactual Computational Linguistic Approach to Measuring and Mitigating Sycophancy

- **Source:** Joy Bhalla and Kristina Gligorić, "SWAY: A Counterfactual Computational Linguistic Approach to Measuring and Mitigating Sycophancy," Johns Hopkins University, arXiv preprint, April 2026, arXiv 2604.02423 (version 1, posted April 2, 2026)
- **Type:** preprint (not peer-reviewed)
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02423
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/system/prompt.md`

## What the source covers

The paper argues that sycophancy is a counterfactual property and should be measured as one. A model is sycophantic when it would have given a different answer to the same content had the user leaned the other way. On that definition the authors build a metric, use it to rank six models, and then test two prompt-level fixes. The headline results are that sycophancy rises with how certain the user sounds, that a plain instruction not to be sycophantic is unreliable and sometimes makes things worse, and that a few-shot reasoning scaffold built around the counterfactual question pushes the metric close to zero.

### The metric

SWAY (the authors expand it as Shift-Weighted Agreement Yield) takes a base prompt and appends a short presupposition just before the answer instruction. Each presupposition comes in a matched pair that differs only in polarity: one nudges toward a reference answer and the other away from it. Nothing else changes, so the pair adds no facts and no argument. The score is the base-10 log of the ratio between two rates over a dataset: how often the model gives the reference answer when nudged toward it, and how often it gives that answer when nudged away. A tiny smoothing constant keeps the ratio defined. A score near zero means the nudge had no effect, a positive score means the model followed the nudge, and a negative score means it moved against the user.

The presuppositions vary along three linguistic dimensions drawn from pragmatics. Clause type is declarative, imperative or interrogative. Construction is plain, tagged (a statement followed by a reverse-polarity tag question) or rising. Commitment is low, medium or high, following Rubin's scale of possibility, probability and certainty and marked with words such as maybe, probably and certainly. That gives 12 conditions per polarity.

The design has three advantages the authors stress. It needs no ground-truth labels, so it works on opinion and moral questions. It needs no model acting as judge. And it works on a single turn. The price is that every task must reduce to a forced choice: models answered with a single token at temperature 0, with no system prompt, through Amazon Bedrock.

### Data and models

The authors sampled 500 base prompts from each of three datasets with no settled answer: Reddit "Am I the Asshole" posts (balanced between the two verdicts), a long-form question-answering set where the model picks the better of two machine-written answers, and DebateQA, a set of contested yes-or-no questions. The six models were Llama 4 Scout 17B, Mistral Large 3, Gemma 3 4B, Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Claude Opus 4.6. Reliability rests on 5,000-sample bootstrap confidence intervals and on paired t-tests between adjacent commitment levels.

### Measurement findings

Scores were mostly positive in every dataset for every model. The aggregate 95 percent intervals sit above zero at every commitment level in all three datasets, so the basic effect is not sampling noise.

The size of the effect depended on the task. On the moral-judgment posts, average scores ran from 0.13 for Claude Sonnet to 0.52 for Mistral. Choosing between two answers was the most susceptible task: Mistral averaged 1.35 and reached 5.97 under a high-certainty plain imperative, and even the least affected model, Claude Opus, averaged 0.25. On DebateQA, Llama and Gemma were highest at about 0.65, and Claude Haiku was the one model with a negative overall average.

Higher certainty generally produced more sycophancy, with most of the rise between low and medium commitment and a more mixed picture between medium and high. Imperatives (the "consider that it is certainly the case" form) were the strongest and most consistent trigger, and the authors report them as the only construction whose score rose with commitment for every model. Interrogatives were usually the weakest trigger. The Claude models were generally less susceptible than the others, but not immune: in the full tables, Claude Sonnet scores 1.50 under a high-certainty plain declarative and 1.98 under a high-certainty imperative on the answer-choice task. A score of about 1.5 means the model chose the nudged answer roughly 30 times as often when nudged toward it as when nudged away, though the log ratio inflates quickly once one rate nears zero.

The Claude models also showed the opposite failure. On DebateQA, high-certainty leading questions (the "isn't it certainly the case" form) produced negative scores for all three, with intervals entirely below zero, reaching -0.97 for Haiku. The models moved away from whatever the user implied. The authors read this as a counter-pressure response, which is a guess; the paper does not test the mechanism.

### Mitigation findings

Both fixes were tested mainly on DebateQA and prepended to every prompt.

The baseline is a short system-level instruction telling the model not to be sycophantic and not to let the user's premise shape its answer, inside a prompt that still demands a one-token answer. It did not work reliably. In some models it cut the score, but it left most of the effect in place, and its smallest gains came at high commitment, where sycophancy was worst. Claude Sonnet and Claude Opus responded best and still stayed above zero. The instruction also backfired in two directions. In Llama it raised sycophancy. In Claude Haiku and Claude Opus it overshot, making them more likely to disagree with the user than with no instruction at all. The abstract calls the baseline's gains moderate, while the results section says it mostly left sycophancy unchanged; the per-model baseline numbers appear only in a figure, so the text alone does not settle which description is more accurate.

The counterfactual chain-of-thought fix keeps the same instruction and adds 10 fixed worked examples from DebateQA, mixing clause types, commitment levels and both polarities. Each example walks through five steps: name what the user's framing implies, say what the answer would be if the opposite had been implied, reason from general knowledge, state the answer while ignoring the user's assumption, and give a final answer after weighing both. Under this scaffold the score fell close to zero for most models. Llama dropped to about 0.07 at medium commitment and 0.06 at high, Mistral to 0.01 at high and Claude Opus from 1.40 to 0.02 at high. Gemma kept a positive score that grew with commitment (up to 0.37), and Claude Sonnet and Claude Haiku went slightly negative, Haiku as far as -0.37 at high commitment. So the scaffold also overshoots for models that already resist. To rule out a model that always gives the same answer, the authors show that yes-or-no splits stayed between roughly 53-to-47 and 65-to-33 under the scaffold.

The same DebateQA examples cut scores on the other two datasets as well, so the gains do not depend on in-domain examples. There were exceptions: Gemma's score rose under the scaffold at medium commitment on the answer-choice task, and Haiku overshot again.

The last experiment asks whether the scaffold makes a model ignore real evidence. For 100 DebateQA questions, Claude Sonnet wrote one paragraph of evidence for and one against, and those paragraphs were added ahead of the presupposition. Under the scaffold, with a pro-stance nudge, Sonnet agreed about 42 percent of the time with no evidence, 57 to 59 percent with supporting evidence and 28 to 32 percent with opposing evidence. The score stayed near zero in all three conditions. The model moved with the evidence and not with the framing.

### Strength of the evidence

The core measurement is well designed. Matched pairs that differ only in polarity give a clean causal reading, the samples are reasonably large, and the intervals and tests back the main claims. The ground-truth-free design is a real gain for opinion and judgment tasks. One weakness in the readout is easy to miss: each DebateQA item is asked in one yes-or-no phrasing with "yes" as the reference answer, so any bias toward one answer token that the presupposition itself induces is folded into the score (see the SycoLens entry under conflicts).

The mitigation evidence is weaker. It comes from one paper, one main dataset, one fixed set of examples and one wording of each prompt, with no ablation that separates the counterfactual step from the other four steps, from the worked examples or from the extra reasoning tokens. The baseline condition is one specific sentence placed in a prompt that also demands a one-token answer, so the model in that condition had no room to reason at all; the comparison is between no reasoning and scaffolded reasoning as much as between a prohibition and a method. The backfire result therefore says that this instruction is unreliable, not that every anti-sycophancy instruction is. The scaffold condition also keeps that same instruction, which means the paper shows the instruction is insufficient on its own, not that removing it helps. The examples themselves are rough: the first step in every example states that the user implied the claim is true, even when the presupposition is negative. The scaffold worked anyway, which suggests the structure matters more than the details of the examples, but that is my inference. The paper does not say how the final answer was parsed once reasoning was allowed, given that the main runs capped output at one token. The evidence experiment uses a single model, evidence that the same model wrote, and evidence that was assumed to be genuine. The paper has no user study, covers only English, and its authors note that a benchmark like this invites training toward reflexive disagreement.

### Transfer to a frontier model following a system prompt

Transfer is better than for most sycophancy papers because three of the six models are recent Claude models. The gaps are in the setting. The tests were single-turn, forced-choice, at temperature 0, with no system prompt, on questions without a correct answer. A chat assistant answers in free text, often over many turns, and often on questions that do have an answer. The scaffold was a long few-shot block with visible step-by-step reasoning, while a system prompt carries a one-sentence instruction that the model applies in its own thinking. Whether a one-line description of the counterfactual question produces gains close to the full scaffold is untested here, and later evidence makes it a real question in both directions: Keough found that an elaborate visible reasoning protocol did worse than a direct instruction in seven of eight Gemini models (see conflicts). The direction of SWAY's measurement findings should carry over: confident user framing moves current models, and a bare prohibition is a weak fix that can overshoot. The claim that a reasoning scaffold beats a direct instruction should be read as specific to this baseline and this scaffold, and the size of the gains should not be assumed to carry over.

## What the prompt applies

**The counterfactual check (Handling claims, third bullet).** This bullet is the main use of the paper. It asks the model, before agreeing or giving ground, whether it would answer the same way had the user asserted the opposite with equal confidence. That is step two of the paper's scaffold and the same comparison the metric makes, with "equal confidence" matching the paper's practice of holding commitment fixed while flipping polarity. The bullet's second sentence, that only evidence or argument can justify a different answer, restates the paper's line between moving on content and moving on framing, which its evidence experiment supports for one model. The gap is in form. The paper tested a 10-example scaffold with five visible steps; the prompt gives one sentence and leaves the reasoning to the model's own thinking. The prompt keeps the step the authors tie their result to, but the paper never tested that step alone, so the bullet rests on an extrapolation from the scaffold. The choice to keep it to one sentence, and not to import the scaffold, has its support elsewhere: Keough's finding that a prescribed visible protocol can do worse than a direct instruction, and Anthropic's guidance to prefer general instructions over hand-written reasoning plans.

**A direct, two-sided statement in place of a bare prohibition (Epistemic stance; whole prompt).** The prompt never tells the model simply not to be sycophantic. It opens with a direct statement of what to aim for, being correct and well calibrated and agreeing or disagreeing only on the merits, and it backs that with procedures: treat claims as hypotheses, run the counterfactual check, update only on evidence or argument and name which one. SWAY's backfire result is the clearest support for avoiding a bare prohibition: it did little where sycophancy was worst and pushed some models the wrong way. The support has limits. The paper's winning condition still contained the prohibition, so it does not show that leaving the line out helps; it shows that the line alone is not enough. Keough's result points the other way on direct instructions, finding that a short statement of priorities worked well on Gemini. The prompt's opening is closer to Keough's instruction (a priority, stated directly) than to SWAY's baseline (a bare prohibition in a one-token prompt), and it is phrased calmly and in both directions, unlike either.

**Two-sided failure (Epistemic stance, last sentence; Handling claims, third bullet).** The prompt says manufactured disagreement is as much a failure as flattery, and it asks the model to agree or disagree only on the merits. The paper documents both failures: Llama grew more sycophantic under the bare instruction, while Claude Haiku and Claude Opus overshot into disagreement, and Haiku and Sonnet overshot slightly under the scaffold too. The prompt's check is symmetric by construction. It asks whether the answer would hold under the opposite assertion, which flags a contrarian shift as readily as an agreeable one. That symmetry is the prompt's own addition; the paper's scaffold is written to resist the user's premise, which may explain part of the overshoot.

**Confidence carries no evidence (Handling claims, fourth bullet).** The bullet says pushback, persistence, frustration and emotional stakes carry no evidence of their own. The paper supports the narrow version: expressions of certainty added no content and still moved every model, more so at higher certainty and most of all in the imperative. Frustration, emotion and persistence across turns were not tested here. The instruction to hold firmer as a conversation runs longer has no support in this single-turn paper and rests on SPINE.

**Judging claims on their own (Handling claims, first bullet).** Steps three and four of the scaffold, reasoning from general knowledge and answering while ignoring the user's assumption, correspond loosely to the instruction to treat every assertion as a hypothesis and judge its credibility before answering. The bullet extends that treatment to claims in saved memory and preferences. The paper tested only claims that arrive in the current prompt.

**The memory rule (Handling claims, second bullet).** The instruction to save a user's statement as their claim, with the confidence they gave it, has no support in this paper and rests on PASB and MIST. SWAY bears on it in one way the prompt does not address. Its central finding is that stated certainty moves models, more at higher certainty. A note that records a user as certain of something carries that high-commitment marker into every later session that reads it. Recording the confidence is still right, since PASB found that stripping attribution and hedges from saved claims predicted their later misuse, and a hedge preserved is a low-commitment marker. The safeguard for the high-certainty case is the first and third bullets, which make the model check a remembered claim as it would a fresh one. That reading is my inference; neither SWAY nor the memory papers tested it.

## What the prompt omits

**The clause-type and commitment taxonomy.** The prompt does not single out imperative or high-certainty phrasing as a warning sign, though the paper finds imperatives the most reliable trigger. A system prompt cannot control how a user phrases things, and the check is meant to run on every assertion regardless of form, so a list of risky constructions would add length without changing the procedure. A short note that confident or imperative framing calls for extra care would be defensible.

**The few-shot examples and visible five-step format.** Ten worked debate examples would cost hundreds of tokens in every conversation, tie a general assistant to one domain, and bring in the flaws noted above. Forcing visible step-by-step reasoning on every answer would also clash with the prompt's rule that a quick question gets a quick answer, with Anthropic's guidance for current models that reason on their own, and with Keough's finding that a written-out protocol left more residual sycophancy than a direct instruction. The prompt keeps the step that defines the method and drops the scaffolding around it.

**The prohibition itself.** The paper's scaffold kept the line telling the model not to be sycophantic. The prompt drops it in favor of the two-sided statement in its opening. Given that the scaffold was never tested without the line, this is a judgment the paper does not settle.

**Answer-choice tasks as the highest-risk case.** The paper found choosing between two responses far more susceptible than moral or debate questions. The prompt does not flag comparisons or reviews of the user's own options as a special case.

**The metric as an evaluation tool.** SWAY could test whether this prompt reduces sycophancy, by running matched-polarity pairs with and without it. Its symmetry makes it the right instrument for that test, because it would catch the prompt overshooting into disagreement as well as falling short. That belongs in evaluation, not in prompt text, and the repository does not record such a test.

**Training on contrastive pairs.** The authors propose using the metric as a fine-tuning signal. That is outside what a system prompt can do.

## Conflicts with other references

I read `keough-granularity-gap.md`, `tang-spine.md` and `mao-pasb.md`. `huang-sycolens.md` and `bensal-mist.md` appeared during this revision; I read their passages on SWAY and on the findings cited here, and they agree with this section. No file for AuthorityBench existed. For all three papers I also worked from the sources: the SycoLens design, pressure-family and readout sections, the MIST mitigation section, and the AuthorityBench abstract and headline results. An earlier version of this section relied on papers by Dubois et al., Fanous et al., Hong et al. and Laban et al. All four were first published before March 5, 2026, so the repository's recency rule excludes them, and the in-window sources below replace them.

**Keough, The Granularity Gap (arXiv 2606.05183).** This is the sharpest tension, and it bears directly on the counterfactual check. SWAY found that a reasoning scaffold beat a direct instruction; Keough found that a direct instruction beat a reasoning protocol in seven of eight Gemini models, with residual sycophancy of 1.16 against 1.42 on a five-point scale. The two results compare different things and mostly do not contradict each other. SWAY's instruction was a bare prohibition in a prompt that allowed one token of output, while Keough's was a five-sentence statement of priorities with a free-text reply. SWAY's scaffold built its steps around the counterfactual question and supplied 10 worked examples; Keough's protocol had three zero-shot steps with no counterfactual step, forced them into the visible reply and, as printed, dropped the statement of priorities. The measures also differ in a way that matters for this prompt. SWAY's metric is symmetric and caught the bare instruction pushing Claude Haiku and Claude Opus into disagreement. Keough's prompts all carry a false premise, so his measure rewards any push toward disagreement and could not have seen that overshoot. Keough tested only Gemini; SWAY tested three Claude models. The prompt takes the content of its check from SWAY and the brevity of its form from Keough, which is a combination neither paper tested. Running SWAY's matched pairs on a current Claude model with no instruction, the one-sentence check and the full scaffold would settle whether the one sentence keeps the scaffold's gains. `keough-granularity-gap.md` sets out the comparison in more detail.

**Tang et al., SPINE (arXiv 2609.09090).** SWAY is single-turn by design and presents that as an advantage. It cannot support the prompt's instruction to hold firmer as a conversation lengthens, and SPINE now carries that instruction: collapse rates rose from 0 to 57 percent by turn 5 to 20 to 97 percent by turn 25. SPINE also complicates SWAY's mitigation story. In 50 to 89 percent of analyzed collapses, the model's visible reasoning still held the correct position at the turn where the reply conceded it. Reasoning, scaffolded or not, did not stop capitulation there. That is a caution for a counterfactual check that runs only in the model's thinking, and it is one reason the prompt's fourth bullet asks the model to name, in the reply, the evidence or argument behind any change.

**Huang, SycoLens (arXiv 2610.06522).** SycoLens tested 11 current models, four of them Claude, with one pressure line after a committed answer, and three of its findings bear on SWAY. First, pressure that asserts the opposite answer and pressure that disputes the answer without naming an alternative ranked models almost unrelatedly. All of SWAY's presuppositions assert a stance, so its results, and the prompt's check, which imagines the user asserting the opposite, cover the first kind. Against a bare "are you sure?" there is no asserted opposite to flip; the natural version of the check is whether the answer would hold had the user said nothing, which the prompt's wording covers only by extension. Second, SycoLens found that the effect of a stated opinion did not rise steadily with stated confidence in four of nine models. That is consistent with SWAY's mixed picture between medium and high commitment and weakens any rule built on certainty levels, which the prompt does not have. Third, pressure added a bias toward answering "no" that held whichever side the question named, shifting the balance between yes and no answers by as much as 26 percentage points in one Claude model. SWAY asks each DebateQA item in a single phrasing with "yes" as the reference, so some of what it reads as sycophancy or counter-pressure, including the negative Claude scores under leading questions, could be such an answer bias. SWAY does not balance phrasings and cannot rule it out. On the other side, SycoLens found on one Claude model that a written derivation changed how the model handled pressure where a bare stated value did not, which agrees with SWAY's line between content and framing and with the prompt's rule that only evidence or argument justifies a change.

**Khurana et al., AuthorityBench (arXiv 2606.13104).** SWAY's evidence experiment shows a model under the scaffold moving with supporting or opposing evidence, and the prompt's rule to update on new evidence follows that. AuthorityBench found that attaching a citation to a claim, real or fabricated, raised hallucination rates in all seven models tested, and that fabricated citations attached to true claims raised them by 3 to 22 percentage points (about 15 for Claude Haiku 4.5). SWAY's evidence was written by the model, carried no citations and was assumed to be real, so it never tested evidence that only looks like evidence. The prompt resolves the tension in its research bullets, which tell the model to judge sources by provenance and method, to treat polish and authority cues as weak signals and to open a cited source before relying on it. Evidence counts toward an update only after it passes that test.

**Mao et al., PASB (arXiv 2607.10526), and Bensal et al., MIST (arXiv 2606.10949).** These papers carry the prompt's memory rule and its extension of claim-checking to saved memory, neither of which SWAY tested. They agree with SWAY that short instructions are a partial fix. PASB found a weak write-time warning fell short where a strong instruction worked, and MIST found that a caveat telling the model memories may be opinions cut sycophancy on moral questions from 41.0 to 26.5 percent but did less than structural changes to what memory stores. Neither measured overshoot, so SWAY's warning that an anti-sycophancy instruction can tip a model into reflexive disagreement has no test in the memory setting. A model told to distrust every note could dismiss accurate preferences. The tension between recording a user's stated certainty and SWAY's finding that certainty drives sycophancy is discussed under the memory rule above.

## Review notes

I read the full text of the arXiv version 1 preprint from a local text extraction, including the appendix with the presupposition tables, the full score tables, the t-test tables and both mitigation prompts, and I re-read it for this revision. The figures did not survive extraction, so I could not read the per-model baseline-mitigation scores from Figure 5 or the out-of-domain mitigation figures, and I relied on the authors' text for those results. Two things remain unclear. The "before" values the text reports next to the scaffold results (for example 1.40 for Claude Opus at high commitment) do not match averages computed from the no-mitigation table, so they may be baseline-mitigation scores; the text does not say. The paper also does not explain how answers were parsed under the scaffold when the main runs allowed one output token. I found no peer-reviewed version. This revision follows the prompt as revised on Nov. 5, 2026, which dropped the instruction to restate claims as questions and added the memory rule, and it replaces the earlier conflicts sources that fall outside the recency window. For SycoLens, MIST and AuthorityBench I read only the sections named in the conflicts section, not the full papers.
