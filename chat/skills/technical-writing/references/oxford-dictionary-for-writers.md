# New Oxford Dictionary for Writers and Editors

- **Source:** Angus Stevenson and Lesley Brown, editors, *New Oxford Dictionary for Writers and Editors*, published as the second part of *The New Oxford Style Manual* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2016), which pairs it with *New Hart's Rules*. Chief editorial consultant: Rosemary Roberts. The dictionary descends from F. Howard Collins's *Authors' and Printers' Dictionary* (1905) and the *Oxford Dictionary for Writers and Editors* (1981; second edition, edited by Robert Ritter, 2000).
- **Type:** style manual (A-to-Z spelling, form, and usage dictionary for writers and editors)
- **Where to find it:** Printed pages 428 to about 861 of the combined manual, followed by appendixes (proofreading marks, a glossary of printing terms, lists of office holders, alphabets, symbols, and units). The preface says the text is also available through the Oxford Dictionaries website under a paid subscription.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/technical-writing/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

### Purpose and lineage

The dictionary is a desk reference for people who handle text for a living: authors, copy editors, proofreaders, students, journalists, report writers, and website editors. Its preface sets it apart from an ordinary dictionary. It does not try to define the common vocabulary of English. It lists the words and names that send a writer to a reference book because their spelling, capitalization, hyphenation, or punctuation is uncertain, and it gives the settled form. The entries range across ordinary words, foreign words and phrases, proper names, abbreviations, trademarks, titles of works, fictional characters, and rare words.

The book has a long history. It began in 1905 as a printers' handbook, ran through eleven editions under that title, was renamed in 1981, and was expanded into a larger format in 2000. The 2016 text returns to a compact handbook. According to the preface, it was compiled fresh from the Oxford English Corpus (about two and a half billion words of current English) and the Oxford Reading Programme, with spellings aligned to Oxford's current dictionaries. The editors present it as one of three companion books, with New Hart's Rules (the house style guide) and the New Oxford Spelling Dictionary. Its claim to authority is therefore corpus evidence plus a century of editorial practice, not experiment or reader testing.

### How the entries work

The guide to the dictionary, which I read in full, explains a compact notation that carries most of the book's advice.

- **Definitions** are deliberately brief, enough to identify the word without explaining it, and common words included only for their form get no definition at all.
- **Inflections** appear only when they are irregular or likely to trip a writer. Examples are plurals of nouns in `-o`, verbs that double or might double a final consonant (`benefit`, `focus`), verbs whose inflections differ between British and American English (`label`, `travel`), and nouns with a possible Latinate plural (`cactus`, `stadium`).
- **Variants and errors** are graded. The main form is the standard British one. An acceptable alternative follows the word also. An archaic or nonstandard form follows the word not and is treated as an error. Cross-references send the reader from a variant to the main entry, from an American spelling to the British headword, and from an unacceptable form to the correct one with the instruction use.
- **The -ize question** gets a direct statement. Where a verb can end in either -ize or -ise, the dictionary gives -ize as the main form and marks -ise as a British alternative. It says either is acceptable depending on house style, that -ize has been used in English since the sixteenth century, and that it is not an Americanism even though American English uses it consistently.
- **Compounds** get the longest discussion in the guide. The editors say no rule decides whether a compound is spaced, hyphenated, or closed, and that all three forms of a word like `air base` occur and none is wrong. What matters is consistency within a text or a set of texts, which is why compound forms are a main focus of the book. They describe two trends: modern English increasingly avoids hyphens in noun compounds, and American English tends to close them up into one word where British English tends to leave them as two (`endpoint` against `end point`). The dictionary records the usual British form.
- **Capitalization** of titles such as king or president is lower case in ordinary use and capitalized as part of a name, marked by a note at each entry. The guide sends the reader to New Hart's Rules for the full treatment.
- **Abbreviations** such as BBC and NATO appear in capitals without points, which the guide calls the usual British style, while noting that American English more often uses points. An acronym written with only an initial capital (Nato) is described as an acceptable house style.
- **Labels** mark regional usage. Brit. means standard in British English but not in American; US and N. Amer. mean the reverse.
- **Italics** mark foreign words not yet fully absorbed into English. As a borrowed word becomes common it loses its italics, and words whose English form has dropped an accent show the original form in brackets. Titles of complete works are italic and short poems roman.
- **Usage labels** in the abbreviation list include disp. (disputed), derog. (derogatory), offens. (offensive), arch. (archaic), dated, and informal. These carry the book's usage judgments.

### Kinds of advice the entries carry

I counted the notation markers in the OCR text to get a sense of proportion. The numbers are approximate because the two-column layout and scan errors split some entries. The book contains roughly 1,950 entries marked one word, 1,070 marked two words, and 1,030 marked as hyphenated. It has about 1,500 not-forms (errors or rejected spellings), about 590 cross-references marked `cf.` that point to a word easily confused with the headword, and about 580 `var. of` cross-references. About 360 entries give a US spelling in brackets, about 290 are marked `Brit. also -ise`, about 240 are trademarks, and about 140 split a compound's form by part of speech or position. Fewer than 40 entries give an explicit usage verdict with `avoid`, `disp.`, `prefer`, or similar words. The book is overwhelmingly a guide to form. Its usage advice is sparse, terse, and concentrated in a small number of entries, which I list below.

**Spelling and form.** Most entries settle a spelling (`accommodate` with two c's and two m's, `supersede` not `-cede`, `minuscule`, `liaise`) or a plural (`agendas`, `criteria` as the plural of `criterion`, `phenomena`, `appendices` for books). Others settle an inflection (`benefited` or `benefitted`, `focused` or `focussed`, `travelled` with American `traveled`). Still others settle a past tense (`learned` with British `learnt`, `spelled` with British `spelt`, `dreamed` or `dreamt`, `proved` or `proven`, and `dove` as a North American past of `dive`). The entry for `gotten` says British English uses it only in `ill-gotten` while it is common in North America.

**Verbs that never take `-ize`.** About two dozen entries mark a verb as ending in `-ise` in every variety of English because the ending is not the Greek suffix. Among them are `advertise`, `advise`, `chastise`, `comprise`, `compromise`, `despise`, `devise`, `enterprise`, `excise`, `exercise`, `franchise`, `improvise`, `revise`, `supervise`, `surmise`, `surprise`, and `televise`. The `-yse` verbs follow British `-yse` with American `-yze` (`analyse`, US `analyze`).

**American and British forms.** Several hundred entries pair a British headword with its American form. Most are the regular patterns: `colour` and `color`, `centre` and `center`, `defence` and `defense`, `grey` and `gray`, `catalogue` and `catalog`, `ageing` and `aging`, `acknowledgement` and `acknowledgment`, and doubled consonants such as `travelling` against American `traveling`. Some carry a twist. `Dialogue` has `dialog` as an American alternative. `Programme` and `disc` give way to `program` and `disk` in American use and in computing. `Licence` and `practise` are British noun and verb forms beside American `license` and `practice`, `fulfil` and `enrol` become `fulfill` and `enroll`, and `judgment` is the legal and North American form of `judgement`. Word-division pairs follow the same pattern. `Any more` is two words in British use and one in American, and `on to` is two words in British use with `onto` as the American and mathematical form. `Whilst` is marked as British with a direction to prefer `while`. A few entries flag words whose meaning differs between the two varieties. `Momentarily` means briefly in British use and very soon in North American use. `First floor` is the floor above the ground in Britain and the ground floor in North America. `Homely` means simple and comfortable in Britain and unattractive in North America. `Billion` once meant a million times a million in British use, now marked dated.

**Compounds by part of speech and position.** Two recurring formulas carry advice that applies far beyond the listed words. First, many phrasal-verb compounds are written as one word or with a hyphen when used as nouns and as two words when used as verbs. The closed nouns include `backup`, `breakdown`, `buyout`, `checkout`, `cutback`, and `login`. The hyphenated British nouns include `set-up`, `start-up`, `check-in`, `check-up`, `cover-up`, `cut-off`, `break-up`, and `trade-off`. In every case the verb is two words (`back up`, `set up`, `log in`). Second, many phrase compounds are written open when they follow the verb and hyphenated when they precede a noun: `long term` (two words as a noun, hyphenated as an adjective), `common sense`, `well known`, `well off`, `ill at ease`, `middle of the road`, and `first floor`. The `well` entry states the general principle. `Well` with a participial adjective is usually two words after the verb and hyphenated before the noun, so people are `well adjusted` but `well-adjusted` people are. The `ill` entry says `ill` takes a hyphen with participial adjectives (`ill-advised`, `ill-defined`).

**Prefixes and suffixes.** Prefix entries state a default for the whole family. The prefix `re-` usually forms solid compounds except before words beginning with e and sometimes r, and `pre-` generally forms solid compounds. The prefix `un-` forms solid compounds except before a capital, `under-` is usually solid except in age categories such as `the under-fives`, and `super-` is solid. The prefix `inter-` is mainly solid, `ex-` in the sense of former is generally hyphenated, `self-` is hyphenated with stated exceptions, and `half` is usually hyphenated in compounds. `Non-` is hyphenated in most compounds, which is a British convention, and `co-` compounds are hyphenated in the entries I checked (`co-author`, `co-worker`, `co-pilot`, `co-opt`). For `-like`, established compounds are one word, while compounds whose first element ends in l (the OCR is unclear on whether one l or two), new coinages, and long first elements take a hyphen.

**Words for computing and the web.** The 2016 text records forms for the vocabulary an agent uses most. `Email` (with `e-mail` as an alternative), `website`, `weblog`, `webmaster`, `online`, `offline`, `username`, `filename`, `database`, and `smartphone` are one word, and `login` (with `logon` as an alternative) is one word as a noun. `Web page`, `data set`, `data processing`, `end user`, and `user interface` are two words. `User-friendly`, `e-book`, and `e-commerce` take hyphens, and `pop-up` is hyphenated as a noun and adjective. `URL` has the plural `URLs`, `World Wide Web` is three capitalized words with `the Web` as its short form, and `Internet` takes a capital. Units such as `gigabyte` and `terabyte` are defined twice, as a power of ten and, strictly, as a power of two.

**Confusable pairs.** The `cf.` cross-references are the book's main tool for words that are easily mistaken for each other. Most of them distinguish spellings (`desert` and `dessert`, `dyeing` and `dying`, `lightening` and `lightning`, `palate`, `palette`, and `pallet`). A substantial minority distinguish meanings that writers of ordinary prose mix up: `affect` (have an effect on) and `effect` (a result, or to bring about); `ensure`, `insure`, and `assure`; `infer` and `imply`; `disinterested` (impartial) and `uninterested`; `militate` (have an effect) and `mitigate` (make less severe). Others are `flaunt` (display) and `flout` (disregard a rule); `continual` (frequent) and `continuous` (unbroken); `practicable` (able to be done) and `practical`; `discreet` and `discrete`; `principal` and `principle`; `complement` and `compliment`; `adverse` and `averse`; `elicit` and `illicit`; `eminent`, `immanent`, and `imminent`; `stationary` and `stationery`. More pairs are `lay` (place) and `lie` (be horizontal); `forgo` (go without) and `forego` (precede, also accepted as a variant of `forgo`); `biannual` and `biennial`; `enquire` and `inquire`, which the book splits in British use between a request for information and a formal investigation; and `historic` and `historical`.

**Disputed senses.** Nine entries carry the `disp.` label, which records a sense that is common but objected to. They are `alibi` in the sense of an excuse and `dilemma` in the sense of any problem (as against a choice between undesirable alternatives). Two more are `enormity` in the sense of large size (as against extreme seriousness) and `fulsome` in the sense of generous (as against excessively flattering). The rest are `hopefully`, `regretfully`, and `thankfully` as sentence adverbs meaning it is hoped that, it is regrettable that, and fortunately; `protagonist` in the sense of a supporter of a cause; and `refute` in the sense of deny, as against disprove. The label is descriptive. It warns the writer that some readers object; it does not say the sense is wrong.

**Explicit verdicts.** The entries that tell the writer what to do are few, and I believe I found nearly all of them by searching for avoid, prefer, use, strictly, traditionally, best, and better:

- Comprise ends in -ise, and the construction comprise of is to be avoided.
- Due to in the sense of because of has traditionally been condemned as incorrect, and the entry calls owing to or on account of a better choice.
- Bimonthly, biweekly, and biyearly are to be avoided as ambiguous, with every two months and twice a month offered as the clearer alternatives.
- Deceptively can mean a thing and its opposite (a deceptively simple plan, a deceptively spacious room) and is to be avoided where possible.
- `Inflammable` should give way to `flammable` for clarity, and `unthaw` is best avoided because its meaning differs between North American and British use.
- `Whilst` is British, and the entry says to prefer `while`.
- `Cannot` is preferred to `can not`, except in constructions such as `can not only`.
- `Alright` and `irregardless` are cross-referred as errors to `all right` and `regardless`, and the entry for `whence` says that, strictly, `from whence` should be avoided.
- `PIN` is glossed with a note that, strictly, `PIN number` is redundant, and `hoi polloi` with a note that, strictly, `the hoi polloi` repeats the article.
- `Per cent` (American `percent`) takes the `%` symbol, but the entry says to avoid the symbol in running text.
- `E.g.` takes a comma before it but not after it; `i.e.` is preceded by a comma; `viz.` takes a point, is not italic, and is preceded by a comma.
- Decimate receives a tolerant note: only traditionalists insist on its original sense of killing one in ten.
- Centigrade gives way to Celsius, cp. to cf., and USA to US.
- Attendee is cross-referred to attender, a British preference that American usage does not share.

**Inclusive and sensitive language.** The dictionary's longest run of explicit advice concerns words for people. `Disabled` is the standard adjective and is preferred to `crippled`, `defective`, or `handicapped`, and the entry adds that coinages such as `differently abled` have not replaced it. `Handicapped` is to be avoided in British English though still accepted in American English. `Dumb` in the sense of unable to speak, `deaf mute`, `spastic`, and `cripple` are to be avoided, with alternatives such as `speech-impaired`, `profoundly deaf`, and `person with cerebral palsy`. `Black` is the lower-case adjective for people and is not to be used as a noun. Several terms are marked dated and offensive and directed to modern replacements, and `Oriental` is not to be used of people, with `Asian` or a specific nationality in its place. `Tribe` should give way to `community` or `people` in contemporary contexts. `Native American` is the preferred term in the United States, with names of specific peoples better still. For gender, the book prefers `firefighter` to `fireman` and gender-neutral compounds in general. It prefers `the human race` or `humankind` to `man` and `mankind`, `author` and `poet` to `authoress` and `poetess`, `comedian` for all sexes, and `head teacher` to `headmaster`. `Given name`, `forename`, or `first name` is preferred to `Christian name`, with `given name` described as better in non-Western contexts. `Sex` should give way to `gender` for social or cultural differences. `Themself` is marked avoid, with `themselves`, `himself`, or `herself` offered in its place, which is the book's only remark bearing on singular `they` that I found.

**Words the skill names.** Most of the words in the skill's plain-word and exact-sense rules have no usage entry in this dictionary. `Utilize` appears only as a spelling entry with the British alternative `utilise`; nothing prefers `use`. `Literally` appears only with its abbreviation. `Key` appears only as the Caribbean island and in compounds such as `key ring`. `Necessary` appears only as a spelling (one c, two s's). `Data` has a real entry: it is treated as plural in specialized scientific fields and as a singular mass noun in non-scientific use, with `datum` as the singular for a single piece of information. `Media` is treated as singular or plural, and `none` as singular or plural depending on emphasis. `Comprise` has the `avoid comprise of` note above. I found no entries for `prior to`, `impact`, `unique`, `massive`, `transpire`, `whether`, `fewer`, `less`, `that`, `which`, or `need`. None discusses idiom in general, though the book defines Briticism (an idiom confined to British English, preferred to Britishism), Gallicism, and Graecism. It defines a participle as a verb form used as an adjective.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The book's strength is breadth and a consistent notation. A writer can check the settled form of thousands of words quickly, and the patterns behind individual entries (noun closed and verb open, compound hyphenated only before a noun, a family default for each prefix) generalize well. Its corpus basis means the forms reflect actual edited usage in the 2010s rather than a single editor's taste.

Its limits for this skill are equally clear. It is British. Its headwords, its prefix conventions (`non-` and `co-` hyphenated, `set-up` and `start-up` hyphenated) and some preferences (`attender` over `attendee`) follow British practice, and American forms appear only as variants. It says little about usage. The `disp.` label and the rare `avoid` notes are terse, give no reasons beyond ambiguity or tradition, and cover a few dozen words. It does not address style at the level of the sentence, the paragraph, or the register, and it is silent on most of the inflated and misused words that the skill targets. It reports no tests of comprehension or reader preference. As evidence, then, it is a strong source for the form of individual words and for compound patterns, a weak source for usage judgments, and no source at all for the skill's sentence-level rules.

## What the skill applies

The technical-writing skill (version 1.0.0) takes three rules from this dictionary. All three came from proposals tagged for technical text.

**Section 4 (Code, identifiers, and literals).**

- The bullet on gigabytes follows the dictionary's entries for gigabit, gigabyte, megabyte, terabyte, and petabyte (pages 583, 668, 714, and 813). Each gives two values: a power of ten and, strictly, a power of two. The dictionary only records the ambiguity. The instruction to say which value is meant, or to write GiB for the binary unit, is the skill's own response to it.
- The bullet on common computing words takes most of its forms from the dictionary: `email` (page 549), `website` and `web page` (page 848), `online` (page 701), `username` (page 835), `filename` (page 567), `database` (page 526), and `login` as a noun with `log in` as a verb (page 653). The skill departs from the dictionary on one word. It writes `internet` in lowercase, where the dictionary capitalizes `Internet` (page 621).

**Section 6 (Writing for every reader).** The bullet on readers with a different first language now tells writers to avoid words whose meaning differs between American and British readers. Its two examples come from this dictionary. `First floor` is the floor above the ground in Britain and the ground floor in North America (page 568), and `momentarily` means briefly in British use and very soon in North American use (page 678). The fix, naming the floor or the time, is the skill's.

The writing-style skill, which technical-writing builds on, takes more from this source: hyphenation, prefixes, phrasal verbs, American spelling, and confusable pairs. That file maps those rules.

## What the skill omits

- The dictionary's other computing forms: `offline`, `data set` (which American technical writing usually closes to `dataset`), `end user`, `user interface`, `user-friendly`, `smartphone`, `e-book`, `pop-up`, and `URL` with its plural `URLs`. The skill keeps the words agents write most and leaves the rest to the general rule that a term keeps one spelling throughout, which lives in writing-style.
- Capitalized World Wide Web and the Web. These forms appear seldom in current technical prose.
- Homely and billion, my other examples of words whose meaning differs between British and American readers. Neither comes up much in documentation, and two examples carry the rule.
- Everything else in the dictionary. Its spelling, capitalization, and italics entries for proper names and foreign words have no bearing on documentation, comments, or commits.

## Conflicts with other references

When I wrote this file, the technical-writing references folder held no other files, so I compared against the files in `chat/skills/writing-style/references`, which back both skills.

**`Internet`.** The dictionary capitalizes `Internet`. The skill lowercases it, following current American practice. None of the reference files I read states the lowercase rule, so the skill's form rests on the lead's choice and on general knowledge of AP and the technical guides. I did not confirm it in those books.

**Login and log in.** The Red Hat style guide (red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md) gives login as the noun and log in as the verb, which matches both the dictionary and the skill.

**Computing nouns from phrasal verbs.** The dictionary hyphenates several nouns that American technical guides close up, such as `set-up` for `setup`. The writing-style skill follows the American closed forms, and Red Hat lists `setup` and `set up`. The technical-writing skill inherits that rule.

**Units.** None of the technical reference files I read discusses decimal against binary units, so the gigabyte bullet has no conflict.

## Review notes

The reading is the same as for the writing-style reference file. I read the preface, the guide to the dictionary, and the list of abbreviations in full. I searched the A-to-Z entries systematically for usage markers, cross-references, compound formulas, prefixes, regional labels, and computing vocabulary, and read the entries those searches returned. For this file I rechecked the computing entries, the byte-unit entries, and the first floor and momentarily entries against the OCR text.

Doubts. The OCR merged two columns, so a few entries were split, and page numbers come from OCR footers and may be off by one. The terabyte and petabyte page numbers in particular rest on the next footer after each entry. I did not verify the lowercase internet convention in AP, Microsoft, or Google sources. Because the technical-writing references folder held no other files when I wrote this one, its conflicts section draws on the writing-style references.
