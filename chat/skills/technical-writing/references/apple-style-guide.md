# Apple Style Guide (June 2026 edition)

- **Source:** Apple Inc., *Apple Style Guide*, June 2026 edition (Cupertino, Calif.: Apple Inc., 2026). Document number 028-00839. No individual authors or editors are named.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** PDF at https://help.apple.com/pdf/applestyleguide/en_US/apple-style-guide.pdf (244 pages, created June 16, 2026). Apple also publishes the guide as web pages, which this review did not consult.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** chat/skills/technical-writing/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose, audience, and authority

The guide sets editorial rules for text in Apple's instructional materials, technical documentation, reference material, training, and user interfaces. Its stated aim is a consistent voice across those materials. It addresses Apple's own writers and editors and also third-party developers, whom it asks to follow the guidelines in any text their users will see.

The guide does not try to be a complete style manual. Its opening pages tell writers to follow Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary and The Chicago Manual of Style in general, and to treat the guide as a list of exceptions and additions. When the two outside references disagree, Chicago settles style and usage and Merriam-Webster settles spelling. The guide does not say which edition of Chicago it means. It marks its departures from both books inline, with a parenthetical note that a rule is an exception to Chicago or to Merriam-Webster. Its conventions are U.S. English, and it sends writers of localized content to resources for their own language. It also mentions that some Apple departments, marketing among them, keep supplemental guides, and that interface design questions belong to Apple's Human Interface Guidelines.

### How the guide is built

The body is a single alphabetical list, Style and usage A to Z, which runs from page 11 to page 222 of 244. Four short chapters follow it: Writing inclusively (about seven pages), Units of measure (about seven pages, mostly tables of prefixes and unit symbols), Technical notation (two pages), and International style (five pages). There is no general chapter on writing well. Everything the guide says about voice, tone, sentence construction, and word choice sits in scattered A-to-Z entries such as passive voice, future tense, contractions, and first person.

Most entries concern Apple's vocabulary. They fix the names and capitalization of products and features, the verbs for interface actions (click, tap, choose, press, swipe), the names of onscreen elements (dialog, sheet, pop-up menu, More button), and the treatment of hardware terms. A large share of the list is of no use outside Apple's own documentation. The front matter includes a change log for each edition back to February 2019, and the logs show the guide is pruned as well as extended: the June 2026 edition adds 11 entries, changes about 30, and deletes about 100, most of them obsolete hardware terms such as fax, trackball, and World Wide Web. The logs also date the inclusive-language work. Many of the entries on master and slave, blacklist and whitelist, sanity check, kill, grandfathered, and disability terms first appeared in the June 2021 edition, and the disability chapter was revised in July 2022.

Entries vary in form. Some are a single line naming the preferred term. Others give a reason, a rule, and paired correct and incorrect example sentences. The guide states its rules flatly and rarely explains them, so a reader learns what Apple prefers far more often than why.

### General writing entries in the A-to-Z list

Taken together, a few dozen entries amount to a short writing guide.

**Voice and person.** The contractions entry describes Apple's voice as informal and recommends contractions across most documentation, interface text, and marketing. It allows common contractions of be-verbs and auxiliaries with not and with pronouns, and it rules out contractions formed from nouns or product names, multipart contractions such as could've, and rare or awkward forms. It asks writers to keep localization in mind. The first person and we entries forbid we, us, and I, and tell writers to recast the sentence around the reader or the product. The user entry tells writers to address the reader as you when the readers are users, and to keep user for end users when writing for developers or administrators, who are themselves addressed as you. The recommend entry rejects both we recommend and Apple recommends; it prefers a construction built on recommended, or an informal phrase saying something is a good idea.

**Voice of verbs.** The passive voice entry says to avoid the passive when possible and to prefer the active. It grants that the passive is sometimes necessary, naming two cases: when the active would force a convoluted sentence, and when it would require excessive anthropomorphism. It adds a teaching case. In a tutorial, a passive description can keep the reader from trying an action before the lesson asks for it. The prompt entry makes the same trade the other way: if a writer must say that the reader is prompted, the sentence should be passive so that the hardware or software is not the actor.

**Tense and modality.** The future tense entry asks for the present tense wherever it will do, including for a sequence of steps or events and for conditional sentences of the form if this parameter is set, the function returns. Future tense is kept for things that have not happened yet, such as a product that is not yet available. The can, might, may entry assigns can to capability, might and may to possibility (with might suggesting the lower probability), and may alone to permission. The guide has no entry on must or should, and its own corrected example in the we entry uses should to express a recommendation.

**Plain and precise words.** A run of short entries pushes toward the shorter or more exact word. In order to is allowed only when unavoidable. Via is allowed only when space is tight, with by, from, through, using, or with as replacements. And/or should be rewritten. The Latin abbreviations e.g., i.e., etc., and et al. are banned in favor of their English equivalents. Please is to be dropped from instructions and cross-references. Over should not stand in for more than. Comprise is to be avoided altogether because its correct use confuses readers. Functionality gives way to features, capability gives way to a statement of what the reader can do, and access gives way to a more specific verb such as log in or connect. The allow and let entries ask writers to restructure sentences so the reader is the subject, though let is acceptable in text that describes what a feature does. Smaller entries settle if against whether (whether when two outcomes are possible), since against because, while against although and whereas, fewer against less, and that against which.

**Jargon and abbreviations.** The jargon entry is two sentences: avoid it where possible, and define technical terms on first use. The abbreviations and acronyms entry asks writers to spell out a term on first use if the audience might not know it, with the short form in parentheses, and to reverse the order when the short form is the better-known one. It also covers articles before abbreviations, plurals without apostrophes, and pronunciation keys for acronyms.

**Time-bound words.** The new entry tells writers not to call a product or feature new, because the text will date quickly, and to name the software version that introduced the feature. The latest entry makes a similar point about software updates.

**Tone devices.** Exclamation points are allowed now and then in promotional text and dialogue and are to be avoided in documentation. The humor entry approves of humor, mostly in examples, and warns that it can offend and may not translate. The Note entry asks for notes and other notices to be used sparingly, since they lose force when they appear often, and forbids stacking them.

### Punctuation, numbers, and lists

The punctuation entries follow Chicago with a few stated exceptions.

The commas entry requires the serial comma before and or or in a series of three or more. The em dash entry allows the mark to set off an interruption, a change of direction, or a long list that would tangle the syntax, asks writers not to overuse it, and closes it up with no spaces. The en dash covers ranges of numbers, compound adjectives in which one element is itself two words, and the minus sign. Ranges of times are an exception: the time of day entry prefers the word to between the endpoints in running text. The colons entry capitalizes the first word after a colon when it starts a complete sentence, capitalizes the first word after a colon in any heading, and puts a colon before every list whether or not the lead-in is a complete sentence. The guide marks the first and third of these as exceptions to Chicago. Quotation marks are curly outside code, with periods and commas inside them.

The numbers entry spells out cardinal numbers one through nine and ordinals through ninth, and spells out a number that starts a sentence while advising a rewrite. It uses numerals for numbers as numbers, for units of measure and time however small, for approximations, for addresses, bits, slots, and other technical references, and for every number of a category within a paragraph once any number in that category exceeds nine. Commas group digits only from five digits up, so the guide writes four-digit numbers such as 1024 with no comma. Ranges take an en dash and a full closing number. The percent entry always puts a numeral before the word percent, allows the % symbol in technical appendixes, specification lists, and tables, and when the word would repeat to the point of clumsiness, and keeps percentage for vague quantities. Fractions with denominators of 10 or less are spelled out in user materials.

The two list entries give purposes for each kind. A bulleted list stresses the parallelism of items that need no order, and its items must be parallel, start with a capital letter, and take a closing period only when they are complete sentences. A numbered list stresses sequence. In a numbered list of steps, each step is a complete sentence in sentence-style capitalization with closing punctuation, and the guide's example introduces the steps with an imperative lead-in and a colon. The capitalization entry defines sentence style and title style and leaves the choice between them for headings to each department, except in interface text, where onscreen names are reproduced exactly.

### Writing inclusively

This chapter is the one piece of the guide that reads as an argument. It opens by saying that Apple's users reflect the diversity of the world and that the guidance will change over time and differs by locale.

The general guidelines ask writers to imagine the text from the reader's side, to research the origin and current understanding of a doubtful word, and to weigh context (the guide's example is a word that is unacceptable for a person and acceptable for silencing a device). Writers should avoid terms that are violent, oppressive, or ableist when describing technology, and the chapter names kill and hang, master and slave, and sanity check. It advises against describing software or hardware with human or biological attributes, because of unintended hurtful implications. It asks writers to drop idioms and colloquialisms, for the sake of language learners and translation, and to stop using colors as metaphors for good and bad, as in blacklist or white hat. Its final guideline is caution: when a term seems questionable, choose another, since an equally clear one usually exists.

On representation, the chapter asks for example names drawn from many ethnicities and genders, and it reminds writers that not every culture orders names as given name then family name. It asks that depictions of people vary in ethnicity, gender, age, body type, and ability, that examples of holidays, food, and sport not come only from Western culture, and that examples not assume affluence or a single family structure.

On gender, it asks for gender-neutral wording where gender is beside the point and allows specific genders where the context needs them. Singular they is endorsed, with a plural verb, and so are the alternatives of a plural noun or a rewritten sentence that needs no pronoun. Writers should not infer a specific person's pronouns from a name or appearance, should ask when they are unsure, and should ask about titles such as Mx. or else refer to people by name alone.

The A-to-Z list carries the same policy into individual entries: man and its compounds are replaced by neutral words such as staff and workforce, and grandfathered, man-in-the-middle, dummy, rule of thumb, and black box all have entries with replacements.

### Writing about disability

The disability section asks writers to focus on a person's accomplishments, personality, or story, and says a disability may not need mentioning at all unless it is essential to the content. It explains identity-first language (a deaf person, an autistic person), which treats the disability as part of identity and culture, and person-first language (a person who is deaf), which puts the individual first. Preference varies, and the section tells writers to ask the person or group. Its summary table leans toward identity-first language for people who consider the disability part of their identity, and for autism it lists person-with-autism forms among the terms to avoid while noting that many autistic people prefer identity-first wording.

The section asks writers to treat disability as a spectrum and to avoid ableist framing: nondisabled people should not be called normal or healthy, and disabled people should not be described as brave or inspiring or as overcoming their disability. For instructions it adds a rule the original review did not find in the other style guides behind these skills: avoid wording that assumes a sense, such as saying the reader sees a message or hears an alert, and describe the event instead (a message appears, an alert plays). It also bans idioms that carry a negative message about disability, naming crazy, falling on deaf ears, and turning a blind eye. It then limits the rule. Everyday phrases such as seeing someone's point or watching a movie are fine, because readers understand the intent, and so is the cross-reference word see.

The table lists preferred identity-first and person-first forms and terms to avoid for about a dozen groups. Among the terms to avoid are differently abled, special needs, handicapped, hearing impaired, wheelchair-bound, confined to a wheelchair, high-functioning and low-functioning, and mute. It notes capitalization conventions such as Deaf for cultural identity and DeafBlind in some communities.

### Technical notation

This two-page chapter applies mainly to developer documentation. It asks writers to follow the capitalization of the programming language under discussion and to use code font for code. In syntax descriptions, literals go in code font, placeholders in italics, and the brackets around optional parts in plain text. Placeholder names join words with internal capitals and must be named consistently.

In running text, code font covers expressions in a programming language and the names of files, volumes, directories, and libraries. Three rules govern how code words sit in English sentences. A command or function name is not used as a verb, so the reader runs a command on something rather than using the command's name as the verb. A word in code font is not pluralized or otherwise inflected; the sentence is rewritten around it. Punctuation after a code word stays in body font unless it belongs to the code. A placeholder is written exactly as in the syntax and never used as an ordinary English noun; to refer to the thing in prose, the writer uses ordinary words. The chapter also asks writers to avoid foo, bar, and baz in examples and to use names that suggest the kind of item.

The A-to-Z code font entry adds that code font marks text the reader types, code listings, language elements, and commands in running text, and lists places where code font does not belong in user materials, such as headings, cross-references, and web addresses.

### International style and units of measure

The international style chapter serves readers with limited English and translators, human or machine. It reduces to four rules: write in simple structures, avoid idioms and colloquialisms, avoid shortcuts, symbols, and abbreviations that could be spelled out, and express data in standard international conventions unless a house convention has a compelling advantage. Each convention cites a standards body and a rationale. Country and language names use ISO two-letter codes in tables. Currency amounts use ISO three-letter codes after the number, because symbols such as the dollar sign are shared by several currencies. Dates run year, month, day with hyphens, times use a 24-hour clock with offsets from Coordinated Universal Time, large numbers are grouped with nonbreaking spaces instead of commas or periods, telephone numbers follow the ITU international format, and quantities use SI units with symbols, optionally followed by non-SI equivalents in parentheses. The localization entry in the A-to-Z list adds that translated text grows longer, so paragraphs should stay short, and that examples tied to one nation or culture should be avoided.

The units of measure chapter applies to U.S. user documentation. It spells out a unit on first use with its symbol in parentheses, spells out nonmetric units in text, puts a space between a number and a unit symbol, hyphenates spelled-out units in compound adjectives, and never pluralizes or hyphenates symbols. Its tables of SI prefixes and unit symbols are reference material.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The guide is corporate house style. It cites no research, and its authority comes from Apple's editorial practice and the size of the documentation it governs. Its rules are clear and testable, and the paired correct and incorrect sentences make them easy to apply. The reasons it does give are practical: dated text goes stale, symbols are ambiguous across currencies, idioms resist translation, and certain words hurt readers. The inclusive chapter gives the most reasoning, though even there the case rests on respect and caution, with no data on how readers respond.

Three limits matter for this skill. First, the guide's scope is user documentation and interface text for Apple products, so much of it settles questions an agent will never meet. Second, its rules govern terminology and mechanics far more than register. Its example sentences carry a marketing tone, and several of them use words that the writing-style skill's revision item 8 flags as machine-default vocabulary: examples in the entries for Apple Intelligence, AutoMix, and photos use enhance, seamlessly, and showcase, product examples call chips powerful, and the corrected sentence in the gender identity section says that hiring diverse people fosters a culture of innovation. Third, the guide does not follow all of its own advice in its own prose. Its chapters use closed-up em dashes freely, including in the inclusive-writing guidelines, which is consistent with its em dash entry but shows the house tolerates the mark in ordinary explanatory text.

## What the skill applies

Apple's guide is mostly a corroborating source for the technical-writing skill, and on several points it is a dissenting one. For most bullets, Google, IBM, or Red Hat states the rule more fully or closer to the skill's wording. Apple's technical notation chapter is the exception: it gives the clearest statement among the sources of how code words behave in English sentences. My judgment of influence rests on comparing texts, since I have no record of how the skill was drafted.

**Section 1, bullet 1 (imperative steps, numbered when order matters).** Apple's numbered list entry asks that each step be a complete sentence with closing punctuation and stresses sequence as the reason for numbering. The entry supports the bullet without being its source.

**Section 1, bullet 2 (goal before the action).** Apple is divided. Its want and optionally entries offer a purpose-first rewrite in which a to clause leads the step, which fits the skill. The same optionally entry also recommends an if-you-want opening when a writer needs to stress that an action is optional, which is the construction the skill turns into a to clause. Its let entry, meanwhile, rewrites a feature description with the action first and the purpose after. The skill follows the goal-first pattern that Google states as a rule.

**Section 1, bullet 4 (optional steps).** Apple runs against the skill here. The optionally entry asks writers to avoid the word optionally and to give the reason for the action, or to open with if you want, where the skill labels the step Optional with a colon, after Google and IBM.

**Section 1, bullet 6 (please).** Apple's please entry drops the word from instructions and cross-references, which supports the skill. Apple has no entry on thank you or congratulations that the review found.

**Section 2, bullet 1 (you and third person).** The user entry matches the rule to address the reader as you and use the third person for others, including its split between developers or administrators addressed as you and the end users they serve, called users. Apple's first person and we entries forbid we outright, which is stricter than the skill's exception for a named team.

**Section 2, bullet 2 (recommendations).** Apple contradicts the skill. Its recommend entry rejects both we recommend and Apple recommends and offers an impersonal it's recommended construction, or an informal phrase saying something is a good idea. The skill rules out the impersonal form and asks writers to state the recommendation as an instruction or name who makes it.

**Section 2, bullet 3 (present tense).** The future tense entry asks for the present wherever it will do, including for a sequence of steps and for conditional sentences about how a function behaves. The skill agrees on the default and keeps the future for events that come later in a sequence, a small difference of degree.

**Section 2, bullet 4 (modal verbs).** Apple's can, might, may entry assigns can to capability, might and may to possibility, and may alone to permission. The skill agrees on can and might, and it narrows may to permission, so Apple's use of may for possibility is one the skill rewrites. Apple has no entry on must or should, and its own corrected examples use should for recommendations.

**Section 2, bullet 5 (software by what it does).** Apple gives partial support. Its inclusive-writing chapter advises against giving software and hardware human or biological traits, its passive voice entry names excessive anthropomorphism as a reason to accept the passive, and its prompt entry keeps software from acting as a person. Apple's stated reason is possible hurt to readers, where the skill's is accuracy. Apple's own sense-based rule for instructions, which replaces a reader who sees a message with a message that appears, conflicts with the skill's window example; see the conflicts section.

**Section 3, bullet 1 (timeless documentation).** The new entry is the closest match among Apple's entries. It tells writers not to call a feature new, because the text will date, and to name the version that introduced it. The latest entry makes the same point about updates. Both support the bullet and revision item 1's dating words, though the bullet's wording comes from Google.

**Section 3, bullets 2 and 3 (claims and plain steps).** Apple is not a source. Its ensure entry defines the word without caution, and its example sentences describe products as easy and powerful without measurement.

**Section 4, bullet 1 (code formatting and exact names).** The technical notation chapter supports the rule to put code, file names, and other literals in code formatting, and its rules against inflecting a code word or using a placeholder as an ordinary noun are the clearest statement of the clause about using identifiers only as the exact names they are.

**Section 4, bullet 2 (name the kind of thing; no command as a verb).** Apple's rule that a command or function name is never used as a verb, so the reader runs a command on something instead, is a direct source for the skill's ban on a command used as a verb. Apple's fix for an inflected code word, which is to rewrite the sentence around it, often produces the skill's noun after the identifier, though Apple does not require that noun. The rule against starting or ending a sentence with a bare identifier comes from Red Hat.

**Section 4, bullet 4 (ISO dates).** The international style chapter writes dates year, month, day with hyphens, as the ISO standard does. Apple applies the form to content for international and translated use; the skill limits it to logs, file names, changelogs, and data and keeps the written month in prose.

**Section 4, bullet 5 (gigabytes).** Apple's GB entry says the abbreviation means a power of ten for storage and a power of two for memory, so the reader must infer the value from context. The skill asks writers to say which value they mean or to write GiB, which Apple does not use. Apple therefore illustrates the ambiguity the bullet addresses and does not support its remedy.

**Section 4, bullet 6 (common forms of computing words).** Apple agrees with the skill on email as one word, filename as one word, database, lowercase internet in its entries on internet addresses and providers, and login as a noun beside log in as a verb. It departs on two forms: user name, which Apple writes as two words, and webpage, which it writes as one.

**Section 5 (headings, links, and positional references).** Apple is not a source for this section. It leaves heading capitalization to each department, and it has no rule on link text beyond approving see in cross-references. Its above, below entry allows those words for text or an object that closely precedes or follows the current paragraph, which the skill's positional-reference rule rules out.

**Section 6 (writing for every reader).** The international style chapter, written for readers with limited English and for translators, and the localization entry ask writers to use simple structures and avoid idioms and colloquialisms. Both support the bullet's rule on idioms. Apple has no rule on verbs used as nouns, repeated to, or words that differ between American and British readers.

**Section 7 (comments and messages).** Apple has nothing on code comments. On error messages it gives terminology only: it avoids the phrase error message outside developer materials in favor of message or alert, and it quotes a system message in body font inside quotation marks. Neither point shaped the skill, and the second conflicts with it.

**Section 8 (commits and pull requests).** Apple has no counterpart. The bullets rest on the git and kernel references.

**Section 9 (revision pass).** Item 1's new has support in Apple's new and latest entries. Item 3's check for a command used as a verb has support in the technical notation chapter. Apple supports nothing else in the revision pass.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out Apple's interface vocabulary (product names, feature names, interface element names, and interface verbs such as click, tap, and choose), which governs writing about Apple products only.

It leaves out the rest of the international style chapter: currency codes, 24-hour times with offsets from UTC, digits grouped with spaces, telephone formats, and SI-only units. ISO dates are the only part the skill takes, and only for logs, file names, changelogs, and data. Prose mechanics belong to writing-style, which follows American newspaper practice.

It leaves out Apple's typography for technical notation: italics for placeholders, plain-text brackets around optional syntax, and body-font punctuation after a code word. Agents write mostly in Markdown, where code formatting carries the distinction that Apple gives to italics.

It leaves out Apple's inclusive vocabulary for technical terms (kill, hang, master and slave, blacklist and whitelist, sanity check, grandfathered) and its sense-based rule for instructions. The writing-style copy of this file flagged these as candidates for the technical section, since coding agents meet the terms in branch names, process control, and comments. The split into a separate skill did not add them, and they remain the omission with the most practical cost.

It leaves out Apple's advice against foo, bar, and baz in examples, its notes and warnings conventions, and its humor and exclamation-point allowances.

## Conflicts with other references

AGENTS.md ranks the news manuals, Chicago, and the New Oxford Style Manual above the technical guides, and the owner's choices (no em dashes, the serial comma, and the revision-pass bans) above every source. Writing-style wins where technical-writing disagrees with it. Apple is listed third among the technical guides; the files in this folder differ on whether that list is a ranking (red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md reads it as one, ibm-style-guide.md does not), but none of the conflicts below turns on the answer.

**Recommendations (red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple prefers the impersonal it's recommended and rejects we recommend. Red Hat's rule, which the skill follows, rules out the impersonal form and names who recommends; Google and Microsoft accept we recommend. Apple stands alone among the technical guides, and the skill resolves against it.

**If you want and optional steps (google-developer-style-guide.md, ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple recommends an if-you-want opening for an optional action and avoids the word optionally. Google and IBM label optional steps with Optional and a colon, and Red Hat's want entries, as its file records, turn if-you-want sentences into to clauses. The skill follows the other three.

**Appear (ibm-style-guide.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple's disability guidance asks writers to describe an event (a message appears) instead of the reader's perception of it, and its examples use appear throughout. IBM says windows open instead of appearing, and the skill's software-behavior bullet follows IBM. The two rules aim at different faults. Apple's aims at an assumed sense, and IBM's at a vague verb. A writer can satisfy both by naming what happens with a specific verb (the window opens, the alert plays). Google and Microsoft use appear without a rule either way.

**Above and below (ibm-style-guide.md, oxford-new-harts-rules.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple allows above and below for nearby text or figures. IBM, Hart's, Google, and Microsoft rule them out or discourage them, and the skill follows those sources, which give layout changes, accessibility, and localization as reasons.

**May for possibility (ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple uses may for possibility as well as permission. IBM and Red Hat keep it for permission, and the skill follows them.

**User name and username; webpage and web page (oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md, oxford-new-harts-rules.md, google-developer-style-guide.md).** Apple writes user name as two words, against the Oxford dictionary, Google, and the skill. It writes webpage as one word, against the Oxford sources and the skill, though Google and Microsoft agree with Apple on that word. In both cases the skill follows the Oxford dictionary, which ranks above the technical guides.

**Binary units (oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md, oxford-new-harts-rules.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple lets context decide whether GB means a power of ten or of two. Google and Microsoft use GiB for the binary unit, and Hart's reports the IEC prefixes; the skill follows them. oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md says that none of the technical references its author read discusses the point; Apple, Google, and Microsoft all do.

**First person (microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple and Red Hat forbid we; Microsoft allows it where the company must be clear that it is speaking. The skill allows we for a named team or organization, which is closest to Microsoft.

**Quoted messages (microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, oxford-new-harts-rules.md).** Apple puts a quoted system message in quotation marks in body font, as Microsoft does. The skill puts a literal the reader might copy in code formatting, following writing-style section 8.

**Contractions (ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple recommends contractions across documentation; IBM and Red Hat ban them in technical text. Writing-style section 7 settles the question.

**House choices.** Apple requires the serial comma, which matches the owner's choice, and uses the closed-up em dash, which the owner's ban overrides.
