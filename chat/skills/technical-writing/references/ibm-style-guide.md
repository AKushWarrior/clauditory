# The IBM Style Guide

- **Source:** Francis DeRespinis, Peter Hayward, Jana Jenkins, Amy Laird, Leslie McDonald, and Eric Radzinski, *The IBM Style Guide: Conventions for Writers and Editors* (Upper Saddle River, NJ: IBM Press, Pearson, 2012). First printing October 2011; copyright 2012 by International Business Machines Corporation; ISBN 978-0-13-210130-1. Foreword by JoAnn Hackos. The book carries no edition number and is the first published edition of IBM's internal style and word usage guidelines.
- **Type:** style manual (corporate technical writing)
- **Where to find it:** Print and ebook from IBM Press. The copy reviewed is a 416-page PDF of the ebook; book page 1 is PDF page 28, and the body and appendixes (book pages 1 to 380) are complete.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** chat/skills/technical-writing/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose, authorship, and status

The book publishes the guidelines that the IBM Style and Word Usage Council had maintained internally for more than 20 years. The council is a volunteer group of senior editors from across IBM; at the time of writing it met weekly to rule on proposals from writers worldwide and to track industry usage. The six authors were council members, and the preface says the book appeared because people outside IBM had asked for years to see the guidelines. The style guidelines set the corporate style and voice for everything IBM publishes externally; the word usage guidelines record preferred spellings, ambiguous terms, and terms to avoid.

The guide says plainly that it does not teach basic grammar. For anything it leaves open, it defers to Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary and The Chicago Manual of Style, and it repeatedly points to two companion IBM Press books: Developing Quality Technical Information (Hargis and others), for the reasoning behind many rules, and DITA Best Practices, for markup. The foreword stresses three uses: settling editorial arguments by appeal to a rule instead of opinion, giving occasional contributors who were never trained as writers a reference, and keeping topics written by many hands consistent enough to be recombined. That last point explains much of the guide's character. IBM documentation was written as reusable topics in DITA and translated into many languages, often by machine first, so a large share of the rules exist to make English text predictable for translators and for readers whose first language is not English.

The book is a snapshot of 2011 practice. Its examples refer to diskettes, information centers, notebook-style dialogs, and System z products, and some recommendations (two-digit years before 2000, the Webinar trademark, http prefixes in web addresses) reflect that period. The prose rules have aged far better than the product vocabulary.

### Structure

There are ten chapters and three appendixes. Chapter 1 covers language and grammar; chapter 2, punctuation; chapter 3, formatting and organization (headings, lists, procedures, figures, tables, highlighting, and notes); chapter 4, document structure (topic types, books, and white papers); chapter 5, footnotes, cross-references, and links; chapter 6, numbers and measurements; chapter 7, computer interfaces (commands, syntax, code examples, file names, GUI elements, keyboard and mouse, and messages); chapter 8, accessibility and international audiences; chapter 9, glossaries; and chapter 10, indexes. Appendix A lists where marketing content may depart from the technical rules, appendix B maps highlighting to DITA tags, and appendix C is an 80-page word usage list. Almost every rule comes with paired examples marked correct and incorrect, and the guide seldom argues for a rule beyond a clause of reasoning.

### Chapter 1: Language and grammar (pages 1 to 36)

**Abbreviations.** Use an abbreviation when its meaning is clear, when it is easier to recognize than the spelled-out form, or when space is tight; avoid it when it is unclear, when it appears only two or three times in a long text, or when it comes from Latin. Spell out an unfamiliar abbreviation at first use with the short form in parentheses, and leave a familiar one alone, with each team deciding what its audience knows. Choose a or an by how the abbreviation is spoken. Do not use an abbreviation as a verb (FTP the file), do not use one as a noun unless the spelled-out form would also work as a noun (so PDF file, not a PDF), and do not give abbreviations a possessive with an apostrophe. Spelled-out forms take lowercase unless they are proper names. Latin abbreviations are banned outright because readers misread them: for example replaces e.g., that is replaces i.e., and etc. becomes and so on only when the series is an obvious sequence, and otherwise a descriptive phrase such as and other output.

**Anthropomorphism.** The guide asks writers not to give human traits to inanimate objects and to focus technical text on what users do, not on what a product does. Its examples recast sentences in which a window allows the user to do something, a menu enables something, or a page lets the user act, into sentences in which the user acts (In the window, specify the sender). Where a product must be the subject, it bans verbs of mind and speech such as ask, decide, expect, say, see, think, and want, and offers replacements: prompts, switches, detects, displays, operates as if. It accepts verbs that fit both people and machines, such as search and read. Marketing content is exempt.

**Articles.** Keep a, an, and the even in steps, labels, and messages, because they make text clearer and easier to translate. The guide shows clipped instructions such as a bare Create project as incorrect.

**Capitalization.** Use lowercase in running text and sentence-style capitalization for headings, with headline style reserved for book titles and similar standalone titles. Lowercase common terms even when they name features or components, because translators hesitate to translate capitalized words. Do not use capitals for emphasis; use a labeled note instead. Do not coin terms with internal capitals, and do not start a sentence with a lowercase identifier; rewrite so the sentence starts with another word. After a colon, use lowercase unless the text that follows is a vertical-list item, a note after a label, a proper noun, a quotation, or a subheading.

**Contractions.** Technical information takes no contractions, for three reasons the guide states: translation tools and second-language readers stumble on them, a single form can stand for two phrases (its example is the contraction of it is and it has), and they push the tone toward the informal. The one exception is the phrase for what is new in a release.

**Prepositions.** Keep a preposition that makes a relationship clear; the guide's example is adding by before using, so that the phrase cannot be read as describing the object. Drop prepositions that add nothing to a phrasal verb (click on, print out, start up). Cut chains of prepositions by turning a noun into a verb, as when a report that is a list of the status of all of the monitors becomes a report that lists the status of all monitors. End a sentence with a preposition when the alternative is stilted. For abbreviations, brand names, and inanimate objects, use an of phrase instead of a possessive.

**Pronouns.** Make every pronoun's antecedent clear. The guide's examples go further than most manuals here: it treats a bare this or these at the start of a sentence as a vague reference and adds the noun (this practice, these fix packs). For gender, it recommends the imperative, the second person, or plural nouns, and in 2011 it rejected both combined forms such as he or she and singular they after a singular noun. Avoid I and we except in the question half of an FAQ and in signed articles and white papers where authors describe their own work. Use you as much as possible. Use that without commas for restrictive clauses, which with commas for nonrestrictive ones, and who for people.

**Spelling.** US spelling, with Merriam-Webster's first-listed form preferred.

**Verbs.** Do not use verbs as nouns or adjectives (the install, the compile, the debug function); use the noun form. Drop the particle in phrasal verbs when the verb alone means the same thing. Do not leave the verb out of the second of two coordinate clauses. Make an opening participial phrase modify the subject that follows it, and rewrite dangling ones as full clauses (After you configure your environment, you can use the program). Use the imperative for instructions, the indicative for facts, and never the subjunctive; the guide's fix for a sentence built on it is important that the file be saved is a labeled note followed by an imperative. Use the simple present tense wherever it makes sense, and past or future only when present tense would be wrong. Use active voice as much as possible, with passive accepted when the system performs the action, when the receiver of the action matters more, when active voice would blame the user (as in error messages), when passive is clearer, or when the form requires it (glossary definitions).

### Chapter 2: Punctuation (pages 37 to 71)

**Symbols.** Refer to a punctuation mark or symbol by name, with the symbol in parentheses when it is unfamiliar, and never by the bare symbol. Do not use symbols in place of words in running text (ampersand for and, number sign for number, a hyphen for minus). A table gives standard names, preferring for example exclamation point to bang and asterisk to star.

**Colons.** Introduce an inline list with a colon only after a complete independent clause; the guide marks as wrong a colon that splits a verb from its objects. A colon can join two independent clauses when the second amplifies the first. Introductions to vertical lists and procedures end in a colon and should be complete sentences, for translation; if another sentence comes between the introduction and the list, both end in periods. Headings never end in a colon.

**Commas.** The comma section opens with a length rule that recurs in the semicolon section and in chapter 8: if a sentence is complex or runs past 25 words, rewrite it, split it, or turn it into a list. Use a comma between independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction unless they are short. Then is not a conjunction, so two clauses joined by a bare then or a comma and then form a run-on; the fix is and then or a semicolon. Use a comma after most introductory elements. The serial comma is required in technical content; marketing content may drop it except where clarity needs it.

**Dashes.** The guide bans both en dashes and em dashes in technical information. For em dashes it says that commas, parentheses, or a colon do the same job, and it gives four rewrites: a dash before a summarizing clause becomes a plain sentence, paired dashes around an aside become commas or parentheses, and a dash before an explanation becomes a colon. Em dashes are allowed in marketing. Ranges that would take an en dash take a hyphen with a space on each side.

**Ellipses and exclamation points.** Avoid ellipses in running text, since an omission can be unclear and the mark reads as informal. Do not use exclamation points at all, because readers can take them as aggressive, condescending, or too casual; urgency comes from word choice or from a labeled note.

**Hyphens.** Prefixes are closed up (nonzero, preinstall, reenter) except where Merriam-Webster hyphenates, where a homonym would result (re-create), before a capital or numeral, before a multiword root, where doubled letters would confuse, and with self-, all-, and ex-. Hyphenate a compound modifier before a noun when it could be misread, but never after an adverb ending in -ly, and usually not when the compound follows the noun (a case-sensitive password; the command is case sensitive). In a series of hyphenated compounds, repeat the shared word instead of leaving a dangling hyphen, because suspended forms slow comprehension and translation. Ranges use a spaced hyphen, and from and between are banned in ranges because readers cannot tell whether the endpoints are included.

**Parentheses.** Avoid them in running text, because parenthetical text reads as less important and interrupts the sentence: include what matters and drop what does not. If parentheses stay, the sentence must be correct without them. The guide's preferred fixes turn a parenthetical into a colon list, a which clause, or a separate sentence. Never write an optional plural with (s); use the plural or one or more.

**Quotation marks.** Do not use quotation marks for emphasis; use italic for words as words and a note for warnings. Use double quotation marks for a word in a special sense only when no other word will do, since special senses trouble translators. Commas and periods go inside closing quotation marks except where the quoted item is code or a literal value.

**Semicolons and slashes.** A semicolon joins clauses whose relationship is obvious; a conjunction is better when the relationship needs stating. Slashes are ambiguous in running text, and the guide lists four readings of a fraction-like 1/2. And/or is banned, replaced by and, or, or a phrase such as A, B, or both. Slashes stay only in established compounds such as client/server and read/write.

### Chapter 3: Formatting and organization (pages 73 to 114)

**Headings.** Headings are brief and descriptive and in sentence case, with no closing period or colon, and with no number in topic-based material. Task headings take gerunds (Installing the client program), and concept and reference headings take noun phrases; the guide rejects pseudo-task gerunds such as Understanding for overviews. Question headings are allowed only in troubleshooting topics and FAQs. Two headings should not sit together with no text between them; if the text that separates them is trivial, one heading is probably unnecessary.

**Lists.** A vertical list organizes a long series or a set of parallel sentences. The guide warns against overuse: text built from vertical lists strung together with short introductions looks like an outline, not finished work. Use a numbered list only when order matters; arrange bulleted items in some logical order, alphabetical as a fallback. Items must be grammatically parallel, all complete sentences or all fragments; sentences take periods and fragments take none, and a fragment followed by a sentence in any item means every item gets a period. An ordered list needs at least two items, and a list longer than about nine should be split unless it is reference material read selectively. Introduce a list with a complete sentence (machine translation works sentence by sentence), not a fragment such as a phrase ending in include or to; end the introduction with a colon, never continue the sentence after the list, and never put a conjunction at the end of an item. The word following is an adjective, not a noun, so the guide prefers a lead-in such as the following steps to one ending in the following. Do not nest lists more than two levels. Term-and-definition content goes in a definition list, not a bulleted list with bold run-in terms.

**Procedures.** Numbered steps describe tasks with more than one sequential step, introduced by a sentence, an infinitive phrase, or a heading. Write one step per significant action without being too granular, so pressing Enter belongs in the step that types the value. A one-step procedure is written as a sentence, not a numbered list. Do not use please in instructions, because politeness terms are superfluous, set the wrong tone, and carry different weight across cultures. Each step opens with an imperative; any explanation follows in the same step. Put the location or the purpose of an action before the action (In the Installation window, select a feature; To overwrite the previous version, click Replace). Label optional steps Optional and state conditions with an if clause or a condition label. Branches and alternatives are not sequential steps: describe the choice in one step and list the alternatives as bullets under it. End the procedure at its last real action, without a closing statement that only says the task is complete, and point to the next task afterward.

**Figures and tables.** Introduce every figure and table with a sentence immediately before it. Captions are fragments in sentence case with no period, legends are full sentences, and neither repeats the word figure or table. Table cells in a column are grammatically parallel, and a word repeated in every cell moves to the column heading. Tables suit reference material and are rarely right in conceptual explanation, except to show when to use which option.

**Highlighting and notes.** Highlighting is used sparingly so that it keeps its force, and most names get none. Bold marks interface controls, monospace marks file names, paths, values, messages, and text the user types, bold monospace marks commands, parameters, and keywords, and italic marks variables, new terms, and words used as words. Notes carry meaningful labels (Important, Restriction, Tip, and others) and lose their effect if overused; a note must not interrupt a paragraph, and a note longer than a paragraph should become a section. Attention, CAUTION, and DANGER notices are reserved for data loss, injury, and death in rising order.

### Chapter 4: Structure (pages 115 to 142)

The chapter sets out topic-based writing in three types. Task topics open with a short introduction that gives the purpose and context, list prerequisites, give the steps (ideally no more than nine) and point to the next task; they document the simplest or most common way to do something, with alternatives in separate topics or a tip, because minimalism means reducing the reading needed before success. Concept topics begin with a definition, cover one idea, explain benefits and limits, and are written mostly in paragraphs and bulleted lists. Reference topics state facts briefly in a fixed format for readers who already know the technology. Links go mostly at the end of a topic, with inline links reserved for prerequisites and the next task, at most ten per topic, and link text that matches the target's title. The book section covers front and back matter, which matters only for print production. White papers may use the first person when they have named authors, and should open with an abstract written last that names the audience.

### Chapter 5: References (pages 143 to 154)

Avoid footnotes; a short parenthetical phrase usually serves. Every cross-reference should tell readers why to follow it unless the context makes that obvious, and when the referenced material is short and essential, such as a restriction or a parameter description, the guide suggests reproducing it instead of sending the reader away. Refer to other publications by title rather than by chapter or page, since those change. Link labels should be meaningful descriptions, not raw addresses, and the guide distinguishes see or at for reading, go to for interacting, and from for downloading. Example addresses must come from the reserved example domains and documentation address ranges. Link only to stable, authoritative sources, and weigh the cost of links that will break.

### Chapter 6: Numbers and measurements (pages 155 to 184)

Spell out zero through nine and use numerals for 10 and above, with a long list of exceptions that take numerals regardless: units of measurement (including characters), times, percentages, ratios, labels, values, versions, and numbers in compound modifiers. If any number in a series takes a numeral, all do. A number that starts a sentence is spelled out. Do not give a number in both forms. Million may be spelled out, but billion and trillion must be numerals because their values differ between countries. Use commas in numbers of five or more digits, and in four-digit numbers only for consistency; omit them in values the user types. Percentages take numerals and the % sign with no space, except in marketing, which uses the word percent. Ranges take a spaced hyphen and numerals throughout, never between or from with a range; the unit appears after the second number only. When rounding or converting, keep the precision of the original measurement and say how numbers were rounded if it matters. Prefer decimals to fractions. In running text, do not use variable names or comparison symbols as shorthand for words (more than three, not >3).

Dates are written as day, spelled-out month, and four-digit year with no commas (15 May 2011), never all-numeric, because the order of day and month varies. Date ranges use from and through. Times use the 12-hour or 24-hour system but not both, a.m. and p.m. in lowercase with periods, and noon and midnight instead of 12 a.m. or p.m. Measurements give SI units with US customary equivalents in parentheses.

### Chapter 7: Computer interfaces (pages 185 to 232)

**Commands and programming elements.** Put commands in bold monospace in running text and follow the name with the word command, parameter, or option, because names are not translated and the noun tells the reader what the name is. The same applies to keywords, methods, exceptions, and APIs. Never use a command name or keyword as a verb. Tell users to type, enter, or issue a command, and choose one verb for a whole document; do not say run for typing a command at a prompt. Explain each variable in a syntax line immediately after it, optionally with a lowercase where.

**Code and command examples.** Examples support text and do not replace it, so users should not have to extrapolate instructions from an example. Introduce each example with a complete sentence, ending in a colon unless other sentences intervene, and never start a sentence before an example and finish it after. Use monospace, and bold a changed line if needed.

**File names and interface elements.** Use a file name, extension, or directory name as a modifier followed by file or directory (the readme.txt file), not as a bare noun and not after the noun. Describe interface locations with upper left and lower right, and never by position alone, since readers using screen readers cannot see position. Refer to elements by their exact labels without trailing ellipses or colons. A long table assigns verbs and prepositions to each element: click buttons, select and clear check boxes, select from a list, type in a field, and say that windows open instead of appearing.

**Messages.** This section, contributed by Michelle Carey, is the most widely useful part of the chapter. Messages come in four kinds: errors, warnings, information, and confirmation prompts. Errors and warnings need an identifier, message text, an explanation, and a user response. The message text describes the specific problem in one or two full sentences; a generic report that an error occurred is not acceptable. The text never blames the user, and passive voice is the tool for avoiding blame. The explanation says why the problem happened and what follows from it, without repeating the message text. The user response is imperative and specific: name the file and its location, name the command and its parameters, give an example, and list possible fixes in order of likelihood. Telling users to contact support is a last resort, and an instruction to contact an administrator should say what the administrator must do. Message variables stand only for proper nouns, sit at the end of the sentence where possible, and never replace verbs or phrases, since translated word order differs.

### Chapter 8: Writing for diverse audiences (pages 233 to 243)

**Accessibility.** Following Section 508 and W3C recommendations, the guide asks for an accessible format, alt text that states an image's function and is empty for decorative images, accessible alternatives to railroad syntax diagrams, tables with defined headers, and never color or position as the only carrier of meaning.

**International audiences.** This is the densest statement of the guide's controlled-English approach, and most rules restate chapter 1 with translation as the reason. Style: keep sentences to 25 words or fewer; avoid slang, jargon, humor, sarcasm, colloquialisms, idioms, emoticons, and metaphors; be succinct; introduce lists with complete sentences and keep items parallel; use no contractions; do not overuse abbreviations or symbols; avoid negative constructions, rewriting a double negative as a positive and a cannot-without sentence as an instruction; leave out please and thank you, because technical writing needs an authoritative tone; and never write dates in numbers alone. Grammar: active voice and present tense; no needless phrasal verbs; no dangling participles; a clear subject and clear pronoun references; no expletive openers such as there is, there are, and it is important, which hide the subject; noun phrases of no more than three words; keep the optional that in clauses, which the guide calls never wrong and always easier to translate; keep articles and prepositions that add clarity; one part of speech per word; clear coordination (the guide parses one short noun phrase five ways); a repeated to before each infinitive when the infinitives share an object of use; and no omitted verbs in coordinate clauses. Terminology: correct and consistent terms, few new coinages, the simplest word that carries the meaning (large over voluminous), culture-neutral references, no billion or trillion, no domestic and foreign, no Latin abbreviations, varied proper names in examples, no made-up words, and words restricted to one sense: once only for one time, since only for time. Punctuation: no slash for and/or, the serial comma for clear separation of items, no (s) plurals and no em dashes.

### Chapter 9: Glossaries (pages 245 to 254)

A glossary definition is a sentence fragment in the same part of speech as the term, limited to what identifies the concept and distinguishes it from others, optionally followed by one or two explanatory sentences. It does not contain the term itself, does not merely restate it, does not nest a second definition, does not address the reader as you, and does not include task instructions. A term used only once or twice is better explained where it appears than added to a glossary. These rules double as guidance on defining a term inline.

### Chapter 10 and appendix B

Chapter 10 covers index structure, levels, locators, cross-posting, and sorting. Appendix B maps highlighting to DITA elements. Neither affects how prose is written.

### Appendix A: Exceptions for marketing content (pages 274 to 275)

The appendix is useful because it shows what IBM treats as the stricter register. Marketing may use enable to describe what a product does, may drop the serial comma, may use contractions in untranslated English copy, may use em dashes and pause ellipses, may use question headings, may write percent as a word, and may drop the optional that when clarity allows, especially after said. Every one of those freedoms is withheld from technical text.

### Appendix C: Word usage (pages 300 to 380)

The list marks each term as preferred, use with caution or do not use, and most of its several hundred entries settle product spellings and computing terms (one word or two, hyphenated or not). A smaller set gives general usage rules, grouped here by theme.

**Modal verbs.** Must states a requirement. Need to is to be avoided for requirements a product or procedure imposes; the entry calls for must or the imperative, with have to or required to where must is not idiomatic, might have to for conditional requirements, and no you do not need to. It distinguishes need to as an inner necessity from have to as an outside one. Have to is kept for cases where must is too strong, especially in the negative, since not having to do something differs from being forbidden to do it. Should is to be replaced by the imperative, must, or might. May is only for permission; can is for ability and might for possibility. Could is only for the past conditional, and would only for hypotheticals and the future in the past, with you would need replaced by you need.

**Words restricted to one sense.** As is not to mean because or while; since only refers to time; while only refers to a period of time, not although; once means one time, not after; so needs that or therefore as a conjunction; like is not to mean such as; either means one of two, not each; both applies to two items only; if expresses a condition and whether an alternative, with the guide's example showing how an if clause after a verb of telling becomes ambiguous; whether or not only means regardless of whether; due to only follows a form of be, with because of for adverbial use; about does not mean approximately; and comprised of is wrong, with consists of or comprises in its place.

**Plain replacements.** Use replaces utilize, before replaces prior to, to replaces in order to, with replaces in conjunction with, if or when replaces in the event, want replaces desire and wish, according to replaces as per and per, despite replaces in spite of, for example replaces for instance, however or conversely replaces on the other hand, and try again replaces retry. Via is limited to routing. Respectively is to be rewritten out of the sentence, and given in the sense of a particular one usually needs no adjective at all.

**Grant verbs and anthropomorphism.** Allow, enable, let, and permit are not to describe an inanimate object granting a person an ability; the preferred pattern is you can use the product to, or with this product, users can. Appear is replaced by is displayed or opens, reside by is in, and discuss by contains information about. Several verbs are transitive only, so the guide prefers the passive participle after be: the message is displayed, the program is completed, the cord is connected, the light is lit.

**Participles as adjectives.** Corrupted, not corrupt, is the adjective. Desired is banned before a noun in favor of required or the item that you want. Updatable and debuggable become can be updated and can be debugged. Quoted does not mean enclosed in quotation marks, and through is not an adjective meaning finished. The using entry warns that a bare participle phrase is ambiguous between by using and that uses, and extends the warning to other participles.

**Claims, dating, and tone.** New is limited to what's-new sections, because text outlives the newness of what it describes. Best practice is to be avoided in favor of telling readers what to do. User-friendly is banned because good design should be evident from the text. Simply is usually superfluous. Designed to is not to describe what a product actually does, since the plain verb is accurate. Free becomes at no cost for legal reasons. Congratulations is replaced by a plain statement that the reader has finished, since it can sound patronizing. Please and thank you are excluded from technical text. We and us are not to refer to the company, and recommend is avoided, both for legal reasons and because readers cannot tell who is speaking.

**Jargon and loaded words.** Leverage becomes use in technical text. Sanity check, canned, out-of-the-box, overhead, look and feel, black box, and on-ramp are replaced by the specific meaning. Crash becomes fail; abort, kill, and terminate become cancel, end, or stop; impact as a verb only means strike; exploit means make productive use of and is usually replaced by use; legacy is avoided for supported products; fatal is reserved for death; infinite is replaced by an actual number. Inclusive entries replace slave with subordinate, webmistress with webmaster, and first and Christian name with given name, and replace demilitarized zone with DMZ.

**Document references.** Above and below are not used for locations in a document; previous, preceding, following, and later in this section replace them. Following is an adjective, never a noun.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The guide's authority is institutional: two decades of editorial rulings by a standing council at a large technical publisher, applied to documentation translated into dozens of languages. It cites no empirical studies apart from the accessibility standards, and its reasons are short assertions about clarity, translation and tone. Its strongest material is the controlled-English core (chapters 1 and 8 and the word list), which agrees closely with the Microsoft and Google guides and with plain-language practice, and its message-writing guidance, which is more specific than any other source in this folder. Its main limits are age, since product vocabulary, pronoun guidance, and some web conventions date from 2011, and scope, since every rule targets documentation and its one stated alternative register is marketing. Several conventions are IBM's own and differ from US news style: day-month-year dates, spaced hyphens for ranges, the % sign, lowercase after a colon, and gerund task headings.

## What the skill applies

The technical-writing skill takes more from this guide than the writing-style skill does, because the guide's rules were written for the same kinds of text: procedures, messages, and documentation about software. Writing-style wins wherever the two skills disagree. Page numbers are book pages.

**Section 1, instructions and procedures.** Five of the six bullets draw on chapter 3's procedure rules (pp. 84 to 90). A single action written as a sentence, not a one-item list, follows p. 87. Telling the reader where an action happens before describing it follows the guide's rule that location and purpose come before the action (p. 87); the goal-before-action bullet shares that rule with the Google guide, whose wording the skill follows more closely. Putting an explanation after the instruction in the same step, giving branching choices as bullets under one step, and starting an optional step with Optional follow pp. 87 to 88. Stopping at the last real action, with no step that only says the task is done, follows p. 89. Leaving please, thank you, and congratulations out of instructions and messages follows p. 87, p. 237, and the word usage entries for please, thank you, and congratulations (pp. 316, 353, 367). The skill's turn from if you want to X, you can into to X is its own.

**Section 2, voice, person, and tense.** Addressing the reader as you, using the third person for what software does, and skipping let's follow chapter 1 (pp. 33 to 34), where let's appears among the guide's incorrect examples. Using we only for a named team agrees with the we and us entries, which bar both words for the company (pp. 373, 376). Stating a recommendation as an instruction agrees with the recommend entry (p. 357). Present tense for behavior and future tense only for later events follow p. 35, including the guide's example in which will marks something that has not yet happened.

The modal-verb bullet is the closest match to this guide in either skill. Must or the imperative for requirements, may only for permission, avoiding should and need to, and the distinction between don't have to and must not all come from the must, need to, have to, should, and may entries (pp. 333, 344, 346, 347, 362). The skill's can for what is optional or possible and might for what is uncertain also follow the can and might entries (pp. 310, 345). The skill relaxes the guide in one respect: it avoids should and need to only when the reader can't tell their force, where the guide replaces them by default.

The software-behavior bullet follows the anthropomorphism section (pp. 8 to 9): software detects, it does not see or decide. That a window opens and does not appear follows the appear and window entries (pp. 304, 378). The skill's examples are its own.

**Section 3, claims and time.** The guide supports two of the three bullets. Its entry on new (p. 348), which limits the word to what's-new topics because text outlives the newness of what it describes, is a reason for the timeless-documentation bullet. Its entries for designed to, user-friendly, and simply (pp. 321, 362, 374) support claiming only what can be shown and describing steps plainly. The bullets themselves were written from the Google guide, which states them more fully.

**Section 4, code, identifiers, and literals.** Naming the kind of thing after an identifier at first mention, and not using a command as a verb, follow chapter 7 (pp. 186, 196 to 197, 204) and the abbreviation rule against verbs such as FTP the file (p. 2). The guide's reason is that names are not translated, so the noun tells every reader what the name is. The exception for familiar command verbs in chat with engineers is the skill's own. Introducing a code block with a complete sentence that says what it does, and not splitting a sentence around a code block, follow p. 199. The list of common computing words agrees with the guide's word usage list on email, website, web page, online, database, and login as a noun beside log in as a verb.

**Section 5, structure, links, and headings.** Sentence case with no closing punctuation, question headings only in FAQs and troubleshooting topics, and text between a heading and its first subheading follow pp. 73 to 74. Saying why the reader is being sent elsewhere, and stating a short essential fact instead of only linking to it, follow chapter 5 (pp. 144 to 145, 148). Link text that names the destination agrees with the guide's rejection of click here (p. 131) and its advice to label links meaningfully (p. 149). Referring to sections and tables by name, not as above or below, follows the above and below entries (pp. 301, 306). Redefining an abbreviation at its first use in each section of a long document read out of order follows the guide's advice on spelling out abbreviations per major section (p. 3).

**Section 6, writing for every reader.** Avoiding idioms follows chapter 8 (p. 236). Not using a verb as a noun (the install) follows pp. 31 and 239, and repeating to before each infinitive in a series follows p. 240. The note on words that differ between American and British readers is not from this guide.

**Section 7, error messages.** The error-message bullet condenses the messages section of chapter 7 (pp. 221 to 227): name the specific problem instead of reporting that an error occurred, don't blame the user, give the likely cause and a specific fix that names the file, command, or setting, list fixes from most to least likely, and suggest support or an administrator only as a last resort, saying what they must do. The skill's example message is its own. The code-comment bullet does not come from this guide.

**Section 9, revision pass.** Item 1 includes new and simple, which the guide restricts (pp. 348, 362). Item 2, on vague requirements with should, need to, and may, is the revision-pass form of the modal-verb rule above. Item 3 checks for identifiers without a noun for their kind, commands used as verbs, above and below, and link text such as here, all of which the guide rules on.

## What the skill omits

**Gerund task headings.** The guide writes task headings as gerunds (Installing the client program) and rejects imperatives (p. 74). The skill follows the Google guide's imperative headings; see the conflicts section.

**The guide's own date and number conventions.** Day-month-year dates and spaced-hyphen ranges belong to writing-style and were not adopted; see that skill's reference file.

**Highlighting by element type.** The guide assigns bold to interface controls, bold monospace to commands and parameters, monospace to file names and values, and italic to variables (pp. 102 to 110). The skill keeps one rule, code formatting for code and literal values, because agents write mostly in Markdown, which has no bold monospace.

**Notes and notices.** The guide's labeled notes and its Attention, CAUTION, and DANGER notices (pp. 110 to 113) are left out as document design, though the guide's warning that overused notes lose force fits the writing-style rule on bold.

**Topic types and minimalism.** The task, concept, and reference topic structure (chapter 4) is not in the skill. Its advice to document the simplest or most common method and to keep tasks to about nine steps could inform a later revision.

**Messages beyond errors.** The guide's message identifiers, its explanation and user response sections, its rules for message variables, and its guidance on confirmation prompts are left out. They suit products with message catalogs more than the error strings and log lines agents usually write.

**Interface vocabulary and syntax diagrams.** The verbs and prepositions for each GUI element, key names, mouse terms, menu-path notation, syntax diagram conventions, and the reserved example addresses (chapters 5 and 7) are product documentation detail. The skill does not cover UI documentation.

**The strict one-sense list.** The guide restricts since, while, once, and as to one sense in technical text (pp. 241, 304, 350, 362, 377). The writing-style skill states the idea as a preference, and technical-writing does not tighten it.

## Conflicts with other references

These conflicts were checked against the reference files in chat/skills/writing-style/references, which cover the same sources as this folder.

**Task headings (google-developer-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** The guide uses gerunds for task headings. Google uses a bare infinitive verb and discourages an -ing opening because it translates inconsistently. Red Hat uses gerunds for larger units and allows imperatives for tasks, and Microsoft uses infinitive phrases. The skill keeps Google's imperative form, because Google's reason (an -ing opening translates inconsistently) suits text for readers in many languages, and the form matches the skill's existing examples.

**Overview and Introduction headings (red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** The guide recommends Introduction or Overview for a functional overview, in place of pseudo-task headings such as Understanding (p. 74). Red Hat rejects Overview or About used alone, and the skill follows Red Hat in avoiding bare headings. The two guides agree in rejecting the Understanding pattern.

**ISO dates (apple-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** The guide bans all-numeric dates in text and offers a compact form such as 1Dec2007 for tables (pp. 178 to 179). Apple and Red Hat allow ISO year-month-day dates in technical contexts. The skill uses ISO dates in logs, file names, changelogs, and data, and the written-month form in prose, so it agrees with the guide for prose and departs from it for data.

**Username, filename, and internet.** The guide writes user name and file name as two words and capitalizes Internet for the worldwide network. The skill writes username and filename as one word and internet in lowercase, which follows the newer technical guides. The guide's spellings date from 2011.

**Binary units.** The guide distinguishes KB (1,024 bytes) from kB (1,000 bytes) by capitalization. The skill asks writers to say which they mean or to write GiB for the binary unit, which is the IEC convention; the guide does not mention it.

**Contractions (google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** The guide and Red Hat ban contractions in technical writing; Google and Microsoft encourage them. Technical-writing says nothing of its own, so the writing-style rule applies: spelled-out forms in formal documents and in text likely to be translated. The skill's error-message example uses a contraction, which the guide would not.

**Should (google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** The guide replaces should by default (p. 362). Google and Microsoft limit should to recommendations. The skill avoids should only where its force is unclear, which sits between the two positions.
