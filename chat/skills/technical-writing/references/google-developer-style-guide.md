# Google developer documentation style guide

- **Source:** Google, *Google developer documentation style guide*, Google for Developers. A web publication with no edition numbers; Google revises it continuously and logs significant changes on its What's new page, whose latest entry at review time was dated October 16, 2026.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** https://developers.google.com/style (the review used a crawl of 78 pages taken on 2026-11-04)
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** chat/skills/technical-writing/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose and stance

The guide records the house style for Google's developer documentation, written for software developers and other technical practitioners. Its philosophy page is unusually candid about what it is. It says the guide codifies Google's own decisions, claims no objective correctness, and does not try to be an industry standard, a rival to other manuals, or a complete course in basic writing. It explains few of its rules. The stated reasons are that most rules serve the same handful of goals (accessibility, localization, globalization, and ease of understanding), that many rules pick one acceptable option among several for consistency's sake, and that readers usually want a short answer more than an argument. The opening page frames everything as guidelines that a writer can depart from when the departure improves the content, provided the writer then stays consistent.

The guide sets its own place in a reference hierarchy. Project-specific style comes first, then this guide, then outside references: Merriam-Webster for spelling, the 17th edition of *The Chicago Manual of Style* for nontechnical questions, and the Microsoft Writing Style Guide for technical ones, with a caution that some Microsoft guidance applies only to Microsoft products. It mentions the Apple Style Guide and Red Hat's supplementary guide as further reading outside Google style. It also suggests checking established usage in an organization's own documentation or in a broad corpus such as the Google Ngram Viewer.

### Scope and structure

The crawl covered the guide's main sections. A highlights page condenses the most important rules into a short list: a conversational and friendly tone without frivolity, no pre-announcements, descriptive link text, accessible and globally readable writing, second person, active voice, conditions before instructions, sentence-case headings, numbered lists for sequences, serial commas, code font for code, and bold for interface elements. Below that sit pages on general principles (accessibility, excessive claims, future features, global audiences, inclusive language, jargon, prescriptive documentation, third-party content, timeless documentation, and voice and tone), on language and grammar (abbreviations, active voice, anthropomorphism, articles, capitalization, contractions, plurals, possessives, prepositions, tense, pronouns, person, sentence structure, verb forms in reference documentation), on punctuation, on formatting and organization (dates, figures, headings, lists, numbers, paragraphs, procedures, tables, units), on linking, on code (API reference comments, code in text, code samples, command-line syntax, placeholders), on interface elements and HTML, and a long word list of several thousand lines. The What's new page logs changes back to at least 2019.

### Voice, tone, and register

The guide asks for a voice that is conversational, friendly, and respectful, sounding like a knowledgeable friend who understands what the developer is trying to do. It warns writers against copying their speech, since speech is usually looser and wordier than good documentation, and against both extremes of entertainment and dryness. The primary purpose, it says, is to give information to a reader who may be in a hurry. A list of things to avoid includes buzzwords and jargon, cuteness, figurative language, filler phrases such as please note, choppy or long-winded sentences, starting every sentence with the same words, pop-culture references, exclamation marks, phrasing built on let's, words such as simply or easy in a procedure, and internet abbreviations. The tone page closes with a three-column table of examples running from too informal through about right to too formal, which shows by contrast that the target sits close to plain conversational English. A short section on politeness says that please in ordinary instructions overdoes it.

The contractions page follows from this register. It recommends common two-word contractions throughout, and it gives a specific reason for negative ones. A scanning reader can miss a standalone not, while a contracted negative is harder to misread as its positive. It rules out invented contractions and three-word forms.

Exclamation points get a graded rule on the punctuation page. Concept and reference documents never use them, procedures avoid them, blog posts may use them occasionally, and tutorials may use one to mark a milestone. The reasons given are that they can look unprofessional or alarming and translate badly.

### Clarity for a global audience

The longest principle page concerns readers who use translation tools or read English as a second language. Its rules are concrete. Use the simple word: start in place of commence, so in place of consequently, use in place of utilize or leverage, some or many in place of a number of. Write shorter sentences, because translated sentences grow and long ones raise translation cost and review time. Avoid phrasal verbs where a single verb works. Limit noun stacks, with a specific ceiling of two nouns modifying a third. Put modifiers such as only next to the words they modify. Prefer present tense and active voice. Since October 2026 the page also asks writers to limit participles and gerunds, replacing an ambiguous using with by using or that use. Use each word in one sense, and avoid using one word as both noun and verb in close quarters. Keep helper words that conversational English drops (then, that, of, articles) and repeat a word when the repetition removes ambiguity. Clarify pronoun antecedents. Address the reader directly as you, and give context in place of assuming knowledge. Prefer positive constructions where a negative is not needed. Use one term per concept, with the same capitalization each time, because translators and translation memory treat different words as different concepts. Put conditional clauses first. Avoid idioms, slang, humor, seasonal references, and culture-specific references.

The article page adds that articles stay in place even in headings, and the pronoun page adds that a demonstrative such as this should usually be followed by a noun. The accessibility page contributes a specific target of fewer than 26 words per sentence, along with rules to put the distinguishing information first in a paragraph, to keep similar structures parallel, and to avoid double negatives.

### Precision and claims

Several pages push toward literal, checkable statements. The excessive-claims page defines the problem as a statement about performance or cost that the reader cannot verify, a security statement that a breach would falsify, or a subjective or disparaging remark about another company's product. It tells writers to avoid superlatives such as best, simplest, and fastest, and absolutes such as always and never, and to use ensure and guarantee only when something truly is ensured or guaranteed. It asks for a source for any specific performance figure, and it recommends describing what a security feature is designed to do in place of promising what it prevents. Its test looks ahead. A claim must stay true over the life of the document.

The inclusive-language page carries a section on precise, objective language that was generalized in October 2026. It says that words such as simple, easy, and quick express the writer's opinion of a task that may be hard for a newcomer; that careful and thorough are vague and imply the reader is otherwise careless; and that proper and properly are unclear and can sound superior. The word list repeats each point in its own entries, and its entry for just calls the word filler that can usually be deleted, with only, instead, or previously as replacements when a more specific meaning is intended.

The anthropomorphism page is short. Software and hardware should not be given human qualities, because such language is figurative, less precise, and harder to translate. Its examples contrast an object that specifies where to split a string with one that tells a component what to do, and a computer that detects a device with one that sees it.

The prescriptive-documentation page asks writers to recommend a path in place of listing options, and it ties that to the choice of auxiliary verbs. Must marks a required action, can an optional action or a possible outcome, might a possible or uncertain outcome. The guide says to avoid should in general because it leaves the reader unsure whether an action is required or optional. It allows should for recommendations that are widely recognized, such as using a strong password, and suggests we recommend as the usual way to mark advice. It also asks writers to replace a sentence saying a value should be true with whichever of three meanings is intended: a value the reader must set, a value the software sets, or a value the reader changes if it is false. The word list reserves may for policy and legal contexts and asks writers to avoid will and would.

### Tense and timelessness

The present-tense page asks for the present when describing general behavior and keeps the future for something that happens later in a sequence, such as an asynchronous message or a file archived on the next backup run. It also rules out the hypothetical would.

The timeless-documentation page asks writers to document the current version of a product without reference to earlier or later versions. It lists the words that anchor text to a moment (as of this writing, currently, does not yet, eventually, existing, in the future, latest, new, now, old, at present, soon) and groups them by why they fail: some disclose plans, some are implied by the document's existence, some date quickly, and some assume the reader knew an earlier version. It explicitly allows such words in time-stamped content such as blog posts, press releases, and release notes, and it allows soon in a procedure to describe a state change that follows a step. The separate future-features page forbids pre-announcing anything without legal approval.

### Person and voice

The guide asks writers to address the reader as you, to use the imperative for instructions, and to use the third person for what software or an end user does. It reserves user for the users of the software the reader builds. It permits we for the organization behind a document when the antecedent is clear, and its word list says not to use let's at all if that can be avoided. The page on pronouns endorses singular they and rules out he/she constructions.

Active voice is the default because passive constructions hide who is supposed to act, which in technical text can be the reader, the server, an end user, or a visitor. The page lists three cases where the passive is fine: when the object matters more than the action, when the actor should be played down, and when the reader does not need to know who acted.

### Procedures and sentence order

The sentence-structure page states a rule that the guide applies in many places: put the circumstance, condition, or goal before the instruction, so a reader to whom it does not apply can skip it. The procedures page builds on it. A procedure is a numbered list, one action per step with small sequential menu choices allowed in one step, introduced by a complete sentence and never by a fragment that the steps complete. Each step starts with an imperative verb. The location of an action comes before the action, and a goal comes before the action unless that phrasing would make a required step look optional, in which case a colon form is used. A result follows its action in the same paragraph. Optional steps begin with a label. The page also asks for the shortest accessible route when several exist, for one method instead of alternatives, for no keyboard shortcuts and no please, and for introductions to commands that say what the command does in place of telling the reader to run the following command.

### Headings, lists, paragraphs, and links

Headings use sentence case with no closing period. Task headings start with a bare infinitive and concept headings use a noun phrase; an -ing verb as the first word is discouraged because it translates inconsistently and takes space. Headings should be unique, hierarchical, free of numbers marking sequence, free of links, and followed by content.

Lists come in three types: numbered for sequences, bulleted for nonsequential items, and description lists for term and definition pairs. A list never holds a single item, except a one-step procedure. It is introduced by a complete sentence, its items share one grammatical structure, and item capitals and end punctuation follow fixed rules. Description lists separate a term from its description with a colon or period and never with a dash. Inline lists use the serial comma and avoid trailing etc.

Paragraphs should each hold one idea, with the main point first. A paragraph longer than five or six sentences is flagged as a likely sign of too much content, though a longer paragraph about a single idea is acceptable.

The linking pages ask for short, unique, descriptive link text that makes sense out of context, either the title of the destination or a phrase describing it. Click here, this document, and bare URLs are ruled out, partly because screen-reader users jump from link to link. The pages also ask writers to choose links sparingly, since each one is a decision and an exit for the reader, and to give a short explanation on the page when a few sentences would do.

### Code in text and API comments

Code font marks anything a reader types or reads verbatim, including class and method names, file names and paths, commands, environment variables, literal values, and placeholders. The guide's page on the grammar of code elements says not to use a code element as an English noun or verb and not to inflect it. Writers add a plain noun after the element and inflect that noun, so a method gets called, a request gets sent, and a file gets named with a following noun.

The API reference page covers documentation comments that generate reference pages. Every public class, member, method, parameter, return value, and exception gets a description. The first sentence of a class description states its purpose in terms that the name and signature do not already reveal, and it avoids repeating the class name. Method descriptions use the present tense and start with the verb of the action (Gets, Sets, Checks whether, Creates). Boolean parameters state what happens when true and when false. Deprecation notes name the replacement. The guide does not cover ordinary implementation comments, commit messages, or pull requests.

### Inclusive and accessible language

The inclusive-language page treats much of its guidance as a case of avoiding figurative language. It lists ableist terms (crazy, insane, blind to, cripple, dumb, sanity check) and gives literal replacements, rules out gendered terms such as man-hours, asks for person-first language with the caveat that some communities prefer identity-first language, and rules out euphemisms such as differently abled. It also asks writers to avoid socially charged technical terms such as blacklist and native feature, and it gives procedures for writing around terms such as master and slave when they are fixed in code. One item bears directly on this skill. The page asks writers to avoid referring to people as native or non-native English speakers, and to consider whether a document needs to raise the point at all. The jargon page defines jargon as specialized and often figurative group language, including overloaded words such as solution and workload, and gives a decision sequence: write around the term, replace it with a more specific term, or define it at first use.

### Mechanics

On mechanics the guide is mostly conventional American technical style. It uses the serial comma. It spells out zero through nine and uses numerals from 10, with numerals for versions, technical quantities, prices, percentages, measurements, and any number below 10 that shares a sentence with a larger one. It writes percentages with the % sign and no space, except at the start of a sentence. It uses the unspaced em dash for a break in a sentence and does not use the en dash at all; ranges take a hyphen. After a colon it lowercases the next word unless that word is a proper noun, a heading, a quotation, or text after a label such as Note. It writes that for restrictive clauses and which, after a comma, for nonrestrictive ones. It bans e.g., i.e., via, vice versa, and and/or in most contexts, says to use after where once means after and because where since means because, and treats data as a singular mass noun. Its word list replaces allows you to and enables you to with lets you and asks for has in place of with to express ownership.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The guide is institutional judgment, comparable to the news manuals behind the writing-style skill in kind though narrower in purpose. It cites almost no evidence. The few figures it gives are borrowed or practical: a World Health Organization estimate that about 15 percent of people have an accessibility need, the 26-word sentence target, the five-to-six-sentence paragraph signal, and the 4.5:1 contrast ratio. Its claim that inconsistent terminology raises translation costs was softened in July 2026, which suggests the editors recognize that some reasons are plausible more than proven. The strength of the guide lies elsewhere. It is current, it is maintained by working technical editors, its rules are concrete enough to apply mechanically, and almost every rule comes with a recommended and a not-recommended example. Its reasons, where it gives them, are checkable on their face: a reader skipping an inapplicable step, a screen reader jumping between links, a translator facing two names for one thing.

The findings generalize unevenly. The rules on procedures, link text, code font, API comments, and tense are specific to technical documentation and transfer well to an agent's instructions, comments, and README files. The tone rules assume a company speaking to its customers, and the global-audience rules assume translation pipelines that an agent's chat reply never passes through, though they still help any reader who skims or reads in a second language. The inclusive-language rules overlap heavily with the news manuals.

## What the skill applies

Google's guide shaped more of the technical-writing skill than any other reference. The skill began as section 9 of the writing-style skill, and most of that section's bullets were written from this guide; when section 9 became its own skill, those bullets stayed and rules from the IBM, Red Hat, and Oxford references were added around them. The entries below follow the skill's sections in order and say, for each bullet, whether Google is the source, a supporting voice, or absent.

**Section 1, bullet 1 (imperative steps, one action each, numbered when order matters).** The procedures page asks for a numbered list, one action per step, and an imperative verb at the start of each step. Its rule for single-step procedures, which allows an ordinary sentence, supports the skill's statement that a single action is a sentence and not a one-item list. Google also allows a one-step procedure as a single bullet under an introduction; the skill does not take that option.

**Section 1, bullet 2 (goal or condition before the action).** Google is the direct source. Its sentence-structure page tells writers to put the circumstance, condition, or goal ahead of the instruction so that a reader to whom it does not apply can skip it, and the procedures page repeats the rule for steps with a document-creation example. The skill's cache example follows that example in shape. The rewrite of an if-you-want sentence into a clause starting with to fits the pattern of Google's examples, but Google states no rule about want; ibm-style-guide.md calls that rewrite the skill's own, and red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md traces it to Red Hat's want entries. The skill leaves out Google's colon form, which Google uses when a to clause could make a required step look optional.

**Section 1, bullet 3 (where the action happens).** The procedures page asks writers to name the place of an action, such as a tool, console page, or field, before the action itself, and to restate that place at the start of each procedure when several procedures share it. The skill keeps the first part. Writing-style's old section 9 had dropped this rule; technical-writing restored it, with IBM and Microsoft as further sources.

**Section 1, bullet 4 (explanation after the instruction; choices; optional steps).** Google's guidance on steps with results or justifications puts the action first and the result or reason second, in the same paragraph, which is the skill's first sentence. Its optional-step rule, a label of Optional and a colon at the start of the step in place of a parenthesized label, is the skill's last clause. The middle clause, which gives a choice of paths in one step with the alternatives as bullets under it, does not come from Google, which prefers to document a single method and to put any others under separate headings or tabs. The conflicts section covers that difference.

**Section 1, bullet 5 (stop at the last real action).** Google has no rule against a closing step that only announces completion. Its advice to keep a procedure to as few steps as possible points the same way. The bullet comes from IBM.

**Section 1, bullet 6 (please, thank you, and congratulations).** Google's procedures and tone pages drop please from instructions, so that part has Google support. Google says nothing against thank you, and its punctuation page allows a congratulatory sentence with an exclamation point to mark a milestone in a tutorial. The congratulations clause therefore comes from IBM and runs against Google's example.

**Section 2, bullet 1 (you, third person, let's, we).** This bullet condenses Google's page on second and first person and its word-list entries for let's and we. Google permits we for the organization behind a document when the reader can tell who that is; the skill narrows the permission to a named team or organization.

**Section 2, bullet 2 (recommendations).** Google supports the direction and differs on the wording. It asks writers to recommend one path instead of listing options, and it suggests we recommend as the usual way to mark advice. The skill's preference for a plain instruction fits Google's prescriptive stance. Its exclusion of we suggest, and its requirement to name who recommends, come mainly from Red Hat. Under the skill, Google's we recommend is acceptable only when we names a team.

**Section 2, bullet 3 (present tense).** The bullet restates Google's present-tense page, including the exception for something that happens later in a sequence. The skill's example of a server returning an error follows the shape of Google's server example.

**Section 2, bullet 4 (must, can, might, may, and should).** Google's prescriptive-documentation page supplies the three-way mapping of must, can, and might to required, optional or possible, and uncertain, and it supplies the reason to avoid should: the reader cannot tell whether the action is required. Google's allowance of should for widely recognized advice fits inside the skill's narrower objection, which applies only where the force is unclear. On may the two part slightly. Google reserves may for policy and legal text, and the skill keeps may for permission, which is closer to IBM, Red Hat, and Microsoft. The clauses on need to and on the difference between don't have to and must not do not come from Google.

**Section 2, bullet 5 (software by what it does).** Google's anthropomorphism page supplies the principle and the contrast between a device that detects and one that sees. The skill's parser example blends Google's examples of a string delimiter and a detected device. The example of a tool that reports an error comes from Red Hat, and the window example comes from IBM. Google's own example sentences say that dialogs and query results appear, so the window example runs against Google's usage, as the conflicts section records.

**Section 3, bullet 1 (timeless documentation).** Google's timeless-documentation page is the source. It asks writers to document the current version without words that tie the text to a moment, and it exempts time-stamped content such as release notes and blog posts. The skill extends the exemption to commit messages and changelogs, since those records exist to describe a change. IBM's entry on new adds a second reason.

**Section 3, bullet 2 (claim only what you can show).** The bullet is Google's excessive-claims page stated as a positive rule: describe a product by what it does, and give a source for any performance figure. Two parts are the skill's own. It widened the scope from products to results, so the rule covers an agent reporting its own work, and it accepts a measurement in place of a source, which the Linux kernel's patch guide also asks for.

**Section 3, bullet 3 (describe steps plainly).** The precise-language section of Google's inclusive-documentation page and its word-list entries for simple, easy, quick, and just are the source. Google's reason is that a task simple for an expert may be hard for a newcomer; the skill states the cost to that newcomer. IBM and Red Hat support the same rule.

**Section 4, bullet 1 (code formatting and exact names).** The list of things to put in code formatting summarizes Google's code-in-text table. The clause about using identifiers only as the exact names they are compresses Google's rules on the grammar of code elements: no inflecting a code element and no using it as an English verb or noun. The last sentence, on copying code and output from the source, comes from New Hart's Rules, as oxford-new-harts-rules.md records.

**Section 4, bullet 2 (name the kind of thing after an identifier).** Google's grammar page asks writers to put a plain noun after a code element and to inflect the noun, so a constant's value is defined in a named file. Google's rule is the substance of the skill's first sentence. The skill asks for the noun at first mention, while Google applies its rule wherever the name would otherwise be inflected or used as a part of speech. The ban on starting or ending a sentence with a bare identifier comes from Red Hat. Google's headings page adds that a code item in a heading takes a descriptive noun, which the skill does not state.

**Section 4, bullet 3 (introducing a code block).** Google's procedures page asks writers not to introduce a command with a bare instruction to run the following command and to say what the command does instead. Google gives the reason behind the skill's rule. Google's recommended lead-ins are imperative sentences naming the action and ending in a colon, which satisfy the skill's requirement of a complete sentence. The rule against starting a sentence before a code block and finishing it after comes from IBM and Red Hat; the nearest Google rule bans introducing a list with a fragment that the list completes.

**Section 4, bullet 4 (ISO dates).** Google's dates page writes dates in prose with the full month name and asks for ISO 8601 order whenever a date is written in numerals only. Both halves of the skill's bullet agree with it. The skill's choice of where numeric dates belong (logs, file names, changelogs, and data) is its own.

**Section 4, bullet 5 (gigabytes and gibibytes).** Google's units page has a table that separates decimal units such as GB from binary units such as GiB, added in a change that the What's new log records. It supports the skill's instruction to say which value is meant or to write GiB.

**Section 4, bullet 6 (common forms of computing words).** Google's word list agrees with the skill on email, lowercase internet, username, filename (the guide's own page title uses the closed form), and login as a noun beside log in as a verb. It departs on web page. Google's compound-noun guidance recommends the closed webpage, and the skill writes two words.

**Section 5, bullet 1 (headings).** Sentence case, no closing period, a bare verb at the start of task headings, and a noun phrase for concept headings all come from Google's headings and capitalization pages. Google's rule against empty headings, with an example of a section that opens with a sentence before its first subsection, supports the skill's last clause. Google has no rule against bare headings such as Overview, and its own example heading for a concept section ends in overview; it says nothing about question headings. Those two clauses come from Red Hat and IBM.

**Section 5, bullet 2 (link text).** Google's link-text page is a main source. It asks for short, descriptive link text that makes sense out of context and uses the destination's title or a description of it, and it rules out click here, this document, and bare addresses, partly because screen-reader users jump from link to link. The clause asking writers to say why they are sending the reader elsewhere comes from IBM.

**Section 5, bullet 3 (put what the reader needs in the text).** Google's cross-references page treats links as pointers to nonessential material and asks writers to give help on the page where they can: define a term, explain a concept briefly, or give a couple of steps instead of linking. It also asks writers to choose links selectively, since each one is a decision and a chance to lose the reader. Both points support the skill's bullet. The limit of a couple of links per paragraph comes from Red Hat.

**Section 5, bullet 4 (references by name; abbreviations per section).** Google's procedures page rules out directional words such as above and below, for accessibility and localization, and recommends relative terms such as preceding and following. The skill's examples (the previous section, the table of limits) fit that advice. The clause on redefining abbreviations in each section of a long document comes from New Hart's Rules and IBM; Google's nearest rule asks writers to define an abbreviation used in a heading in the first paragraph after it.

**Section 6 (writing for every reader).** Google's global-audience page supports the rule against idioms and figurative phrases, and its warning against using one word as both noun and verb in close quarters is related to the skill's ban on a verb used as a noun. Google has no rule about repeating to before each infinitive and gives no list of words that differ between American and British English; those come from IBM and the Oxford dictionary.

**Section 7, bullet 1 (code comments).** Google contributes part of this bullet. Its API reference page asks that the first sentence of a class description give information that the name and signature do not, which is the same idea as saying what the code cannot. The rest of the bullet (the reason for a choice, a constraint, or a trap, and the ban on restating the next line) does not come from Google, which does not cover implementation comments.

**Section 7, bullet 2 (error messages).** Nothing in this bullet comes from Google. The crawl reviewed for this file contains no page on error messages.

**Section 8 (commits and pull requests).** Nothing here comes from Google. The only related point is the timeless page's exemption for release notes, which supports section 3's statement that change records can say what changed.

**Section 9 (revision pass).** Item 1 holds three word lists drawn from Google: best, fastest, always, never, ensure, and guarantee from the excessive-claims page; simple, easy, quick, and just from the precise-language section and the word list; and now, new, currently, soon, and at present, a selection from the timeless page's longer list. These lists sat in writing-style's revision pass as item 13 before the split. They were moved out of the drafting rules because constraints given while drafting tend to make a model plan a thinner answer, a finding recorded in writing-style's references for the one-token-away-from-collapse and taboo-negative-constraints studies. Item 2 enforces the modal-verb bullet, whose core mapping is Google's. Item 3 enforces the link-text and directional-word rules, both of which Google states.

**Example.** The skill's commit-message example draws nothing from Google.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out most of Google's procedural detail. Lettered sub-steps, angle brackets for menu paths, the order of parts in a complex step (action, command, placeholder explanation, output), the rule to fold a press of the Enter key into its step, and the ban on keyboard shortcuts all assume interface documentation that an agent rarely writes. The skill also leaves out Google's colon form for a goal that might make a required step look optional, and its advice to restate the location at the start of every procedure on a page.

It leaves out Google's rule to document one method and to move alternatives to separate pages, headings, or tabs. The skill handles a branch inside a procedure with bullets under one step instead, following IBM.

It leaves out the conventions for API reference comments: method descriptions that start with a verb such as Gets or Sets, Boolean parameters described for both values, and deprecation notes that name the replacement. The skill's comment bullet concerns comments that explain code to the next maintainer, not comments that generate reference pages. A later revision could add the reference conventions for docstrings.

It leaves out placeholders, command-line syntax, code-sample formatting, HTML, alt text, interface elements, bold for button names, and the long word list, for the reasons given in writing-style's copy of this file: they serve published web documentation and Google's own vocabulary.

It leaves out Google's ban on an -ing word at the start of a heading, its rule that headings carry no links or numbers, and its use of the phrase the following sections to introduce a group of subsections. The skill's verb rule for task headings produces the same result as the -ing ban for tasks.

It leaves out Google's encouragement of contractions in documentation. Technical-writing has no rule of its own on contractions, so writing-style section 7 governs: contractions in conversation, spelled-out forms in formal documents and in text likely to be translated.

Numbers, percentages, colons, and dashes in prose belong to writing-style, and Google's positions on them are discussed in writing-style's copy of this file.

## Conflicts with other references

AGENTS.md ranks the sources: the news manuals, Chicago, and the New Oxford Style Manual first, then the technical guides from Google, Microsoft, Apple, IBM, and Red Hat and the git and kernel patch guides. The owner's choices (no em dashes, the serial comma, and the revision-pass bans) override every source, and writing-style wins wherever technical-writing disagrees with it. The files in this folder read the list of technical guides differently. red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md treats the order as a ranking, with Google ahead of Red Hat, and ibm-style-guide.md says that no ranking settles disputes among the technical guides. The entries below give the skill's resolution either way.

**Alternatives inside a procedure (ibm-style-guide.md).** Google documents one method and moves any others to separate sections or tabs. IBM describes a choice in one step and lists the alternatives as bullets under it, and the skill follows IBM. The two rules cover different cases. Google's concerns several complete methods for the same task, and IBM's concerns a branch point inside one procedure, so a writer can follow both.

**Congratulations (ibm-style-guide.md).** Google allows a congratulatory sentence to mark a milestone in a tutorial. IBM replaces congratulations with a plain statement that the reader has finished, and the skill follows IBM. Google's example also uses an exclamation point, which writing-style restricts to quotations, so the example fails under both skills.

**Appear (ibm-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, apple-style-guide.md).** The skill's example says that a window opens and does not appear, following IBM. Google's procedure examples say that a dialog appears and that query results appear, and Microsoft and Apple use the same verb. The skill keeps IBM's preference; Google states no rule on the word, so the conflict is between IBM's rule and Google's usage.

**Recommendations (red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, apple-style-guide.md).** Google suggests we recommend, Microsoft allows it in place of the impersonal form, Red Hat names the company, and Apple prefers the impersonal it's recommended. The skill states a recommendation as an instruction or names who recommends it and rules out the impersonal form, so it sides with Google, Microsoft, and Red Hat against Apple, with the limit that we must name a team.

**May (ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, apple-style-guide.md).** Google reserves may for policy and legal contexts, Microsoft avoids it, IBM and Red Hat keep it for permission, and Apple uses it for permission and possibility. The skill keeps may for permission only. Google's narrower use falls inside the skill's rule.

**Should (ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** IBM and Red Hat replace should by default. Google allows it for widely recognized advice and avoids it elsewhere. The skill objects to should only where the reader can't tell whether it means required or recommended, which is closer to Google.

**Webpage and web page (oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md, oxford-new-harts-rules.md, ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, apple-style-guide.md).** Google, Microsoft, and Apple write webpage as one word. The Oxford dictionary, New Hart's Rules, IBM, and Red Hat write web page as two, and the skill follows them; the Oxford sources outrank the technical guides. Google's guidance prefers closed compounds when usage is trending that way, so the skill's form may date faster than the others on its list.

**Filename, username, and internet (oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md, ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Google agrees with the skill and the Oxford dictionary on filename and username, against IBM's two-word forms. Google writes internet in lowercase, which the skill follows against both Oxford sources and IBM. oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md says that none of the reference files its author read states the lowercase rule; Google's word list states it, as does Microsoft's.

**Binary units (oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md, oxford-new-harts-rules.md, ibm-style-guide.md).** oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md says that no technical reference it read discusses decimal against binary units. Google does, with a table that pairs GB with GiB and the other decimal units with their binary counterparts, and it agrees with the skill and with Hart's account of the IEC prefixes. IBM's capitalization convention (KB against kB) is the outlier.

**Task headings (ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** IBM uses gerunds for task headings, Red Hat uses title case and gerunds for larger units, and Microsoft suggests infinitive phrases. The skill follows Google's bare verb, as both ibm-style-guide.md and red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md record.

**Overview headings (red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, ibm-style-guide.md).** Red Hat rejects Overview used alone as a heading, and IBM recommends it for a functional overview. Google's example heading ends in overview after a descriptive noun phrase, which the skill's rule against bare headings allows.

**Contractions (ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Google and Microsoft encourage contractions, and IBM and Red Hat ban them in technical text. Writing-style section 7 settles the point for both skills.

**House choices.** Google requires the serial comma, which matches the owner's choice. It uses the unspaced em dash, which the owner's ban overrides.
