# Microsoft Writing Style Guide

- **Source:** Microsoft Corporation, Microsoft Writing Style Guide, published on Microsoft Learn. The guide is a continuously revised website with no edition number; its welcome page shows a 2024 copyright line, and the pages read for this file were last updated between June 2022 and November 2026.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/welcome/ (a local crawl of 980 pages, including the A to Z word list, was read on 2026-11-04)
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/technical-writing/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

### Purpose, audience, and status

The guide is Microsoft's house manual for anyone who writes about technology for the company, from UI strings and error messages to web pages, documentation, marketing, and white papers. Its welcome page says it is meant to be usable by people whose job title is not writer, and that framing shapes the whole guide. The guide gives short, firm rules with paired examples (a preferred form set against a form to replace) and seldom explains its reasoning at length. For grammar and punctuation questions it does not settle, it sends the reader to The Chicago Manual of Style, and since August 2024 it uses Merriam-Webster as its dictionary.

The guide is a living website. A What's new page logs dated changes back to 2019, and each page shows its own last-updated date. That matters for a reference file, because pages written at different times disagree in places. The guide's core voice and word-choice pages mostly date from June 2022 and have not changed since, while the A to Z entries, the punctuation pages, and the procedure pages have been revised through 2026. The most recent changes before this review were dated October 30 and November 2, 2026, and concerned terms such as dialog box, window, and privileges, the prepositions page, and the page on describing interactions with UI.

### The brand voice

The guide opens with a statement of voice that governs the rest. Microsoft describes its voice with three paired traits (warm and relaxed, crisp and clear, and ready to lend a hand) and says the voice stays constant while tone shifts with the situation, from serious to light. The brand voice page (last updated October 2022) turns this into three style tips. The first is to get to the point fast: lead with the main point, put the most important thing where it will be seen, and give people just enough to decide. The second is to talk like a person, with short everyday words, contractions, and sentence-style capitalization, but without jargon or acronyms. The third is that simpler is better: break content up, use steps and layers, and cut every word that adds nothing. The same page says short sentences and sentence fragments are easier to scan.

The Top 10 tips page (last updated July 2026) restates the voice as 10 rules. Five concern word choice and rhythm: use bigger ideas and fewer words, write the way you speak and read text aloud, use contractions, lead with what matters and front-load keywords, and be brief. Four concern mechanics: default to sentence case, leave periods and colons off headings and titles, use the serial comma, and put no spaces around dashes. The tenth is to revise weak writing by starting statements with a verb, cutting unnecessary uses of `you can`, and avoiding `there is`, `there are`, and `there were`. Several of the examples on this page are marketing copy, and one shrinks a full instruction to contact an account representative into a two-word question and a two-word command. That example shows how far the voice leans toward compression and fragments in promotional text.

### Word choice

The word-choice section is the part of the guide that bears most directly on the writing-style skill. Its overview page asks writers to choose words carefully and to use the same word whenever they mean the same thing.

The page on simple words and concise sentences (last updated June 2022) asks for simple words with precise meanings and for cutting words that add no substance, with a caution to use judgment so the result does not sound abrupt. It then tells writers to choose simple verbs without modifiers and, when possible, avoid weak or vague verbs, naming `be`, `have`, `make`, and `do`. Its replacement table pairs `use` with `utilize` and `make use of`, `remove` with `extract` and `eliminate`, and `tell` with `inform`. A second table replaces multiword phrases with single words: `to` for `in order to`, `also` for `in addition`, and `connect` for `establish connectivity`. The page also asks writers to prefer words with one clear meaning (`because` over `since`, which also has a time sense). It asks them to drop adverbs such as `quite`, `very`, `quickly`, `easily`, and `effectively` unless they matter, to keep one term per concept, and to take care with words that work as both noun and verb. The word-choice checklist adds one more pair, `try` in place of `attempt to`.

Other pages extend the same principles. Writers should not invent words from existing ones, give technical meanings to ordinary words, or turn verbs into nouns and nouns into verbs. The examples include using `affect` in place of `impact` as a verb and treating `ask` as a noun. Technical terms are allowed when they are the clearest option, but writers should define them in context, keep them consistent across products, and replace them with everyday words where possible. The jargon page tells writers to avoid business and marketing jargon, with `leverage` meaning take advantage of as its example. It gives a rough test: a term that appears in general-interest newspapers or magazines may suit some audiences, and one that appears in the technical press is probably fine for a technical audience. The guide asks for US spelling, avoids Latin abbreviations such as `e.g.` and `i.e.`, and avoids foreign phrases.

The contractions page (June 2022) encourages common contractions to create a friendly, informal tone, with three limits. Do not mix a contraction and its spelled-out form in the same UI, never contract a noun with a verb, and avoid awkward forms such as the contractions of there would and it will. The checklist for word choice also forbids words that give devices and products human traits, on the ground that devices do not think or feel.

### Grammar

The grammar section is short and asks for the simple grammar most people learned as children. Its rules are present tense for most content, the indicative mood for statements, the imperative for instructions with the subjunctive avoided, and active voice whenever possible. The checklist suggests treating `will`, `was`, and verbs ending in -ed as signs that text has left the present tense. The verbs page lists three uses for the passive: avoiding text that blames or condescends to the customer, especially in error messages; avoiding an awkward construction; and putting weight on the receiver of the action.

The person page (June 2022) tells writers to address the reader as `you` and to omit `you can` whenever the sentence works without it. It discourages the first-person plural because `we` can sound like a looming corporation. It allows `we recommend` when the alternative is the impersonal `it is recommended`, and it allows `we` where Microsoft must be clear that it is the speaker, as in privacy settings. It also contains a 2022 addition about AI-generated content. Such content should signal that a judgment was made on the reader's behalf and might be wrong, either through past tense and an explicit `for you` or through wording that conveys uncertainty. The nouns and pronouns page covers proper nouns, plurals of abbreviations, and pronouns; it allows singular they and asks writers to use the pronouns real people use.

The remaining grammar pages deal with ambiguity. A word ending in -ing can be a verb, a noun, or an adjective, so a heading built on one can mean two things. More than two prepositional phrases in a chain are hard to read. Modifiers such as only belong next to the word they modify.

### Global communications

This section assumes that much of the audience reads English as a second language and that some content will be machine translated or localized. Its writing-tips page (last updated June 2023) is the clearest single statement of global English in the guide. It asks for short, simple sentences, with lists and tables in place of complex sentences. It asks writers to keep `that` and `who` even when they are optional, because they show sentence structure, and to keep articles, which help both readers and translation software find the nouns. It also asks writers to avoid idioms, colloquialisms, and culture-specific references and to avoid stacks of modifiers before a noun. Other requests are active voice and the indicative mood, modifiers kept near what they modify, and no more than three phrases or clauses linked with coordinating conjunctions, preferably two.

Its additional tips for machine translation ask for conventional grammar, standard subject-verb-object order, one word per concept, and limited use of sentence fragments, which translate poorly. They also cover past participles used as modifiers. One of the fixes they offer is to add a form of be so that the role of the -ed word is clear, which is a case where the guide recommends adding the verb it elsewhere calls weak. Other pages in the section cover dates (spell out the month, since the order of day and month varies), currencies, time zones, and seasons (avoid them, or name the hemisphere). They also cover forms that collect names and addresses, culturally neutral examples and images, and web pages built for slow or costly connections.

### Bias-free communication and accessibility

The bias-free page (April 2024) gives gender-neutral replacements for common terms: `chair` or `moderator` for `chairman`, and `staff` or `workforce` for `manpower`, among others. It lists ways to write around generic he and she (second person, plurals, articles, role nouns) and allows singular they when a rewrite fails. It asks writers to use a real person's own pronouns, to vary names and settings in examples, and to avoid generalizations about groups even when they are flattering. It also asks them to avoid terms with racial or military connotations, with `master`, `slave`, and `hang` among the replaced terms. It asks writers to focus on people over disabilities, to avoid words that imply pity, and to mention a disability only when it is relevant. A separate page on militaristic language, added in 2024, supplies replacements for violent terms in security content.

The accessibility pages and the accessibility term collection (June 2025) prefer people-first language by default while accepting identity-first language where a community prefers it, and they list labels to avoid, among them lame, crippled, and handicapped. The writing-for-all-abilities page restates several general rules in accessibility terms. Writers should lead with what matters, keep paragraphs short and sentences simple, and aim for one verb per sentence. They should also keep similar items parallel, write link text that makes sense out of context, and avoid directional words such as `above` and `below` as the only pointer to a location. The alt text page (April 2026) asks for alt text that conveys an image's purpose in at most 150 characters.

### Scannable content, headings, lists, and tables

The guide treats scanning as the normal way people read web and product content. The scannable content page (June 2023) cites unnamed studies for the claim that readers in left-to-right languages scan in an F shape and give most attention to the upper left, though it names no study. It asks writers to lead with what matters, to write short headings, sentences, and paragraphs, to get to the point and then stop, and to keep paragraphs to about three to seven lines.

The headings page (June 2022) asks for headings that are short, specific, and in sentence case, with no closing period, and parallel at each level. It suggests noun phrases for concept headings and offers infinitive phrases such as a to-plus-verb form for task headings, while its example set also includes imperative headings. It warns against two headings in a row with no text between them and says one heading level is usually enough for a page or two.

The lists page (February 2026) sets a range of two to seven items and asks for items of the same structure. It distinguishes bulleted lists (no order) from numbered ones (sequence or priority), adds a format for term-and-definition lists, and sets punctuation rules for list items. The tables page (April 2023) says a table has two or more rows and columns of data and should not be used for a simple list of similar items. It asks for parallel entries in each column and specific column headers, and it forbids leaving cells blank or filling them with a dash. Responsive-content guidance (April 2026) adds that paragraphs should fit on a mobile phone screen and headings on one line.

### Procedures and instructions

The procedure pages begin with a design principle: the best procedure is one the reader does not need, because the UI is clear. When a procedure is needed, the guide asks for numbered steps and one instruction per step (short steps in the same place may be combined). It also asks for complete sentences, imperative verbs, and a heading or introduction that says what the procedure accomplishes without repeating the heading. Each step should tell the reader where the action happens before saying what to do, as in a step that names the tab before the command. The examples introduce procedures with a to-plus-verb phrase that states the goal.

The page on describing interactions with UI (updated November 2, 2026) supplies input-neutral verbs so instructions work for mouse, touch, keyboard, and voice. The verbs are `open`, `close`, `go to`, `select`, `clear`, `choose`, `enter`, `move`, `turn on`, and `turn off`. The page discourages `click`, `swipe`, and `press`. The formatting pages specify bold for UI labels, code style for commands, parameters, file paths in code, and other programming elements, and italic for placeholders and for new terms being defined.

### Developer content

The developer section (mostly June 2022, with the formatting table revised in April 2026) says the brand voice still applies to developers but that writers can assume knowledge of programming basics. It describes the expected sections of reference documentation and asks that descriptions say what an element does without repeating its name, and that parameter descriptions add more than the parameter's name and type. Its guidance on code examples asks for short, reusable, tested, secure examples with an introduction and expected output, and for comments that explain details without restating the obvious. It also warns that comments pulled automatically from source code need review, since developers may omit what customers need or include internal details. The formatting table, revised in April 2026, adds that AI prompts quoted in text go in quotation marks.

### Punctuation

The punctuation section asks for few marks. A sentence with more than a comma or two needs rewriting, and a semicolon signals that a sentence should be split or turned into a list. Its specific rules include these:

- The serial comma is required in a series of three or more.
- Em dashes are allowed for a break in thought or an aside, set with no spaces, and the 2025 dash page warns against overuse. En dashes mark ranges in tables and UI, and running text uses from and through for ranges, with to for ranges of times.
- The colon page (February 2026) discourages colons that introduce an amplification inside a sentence, suggesting a rewrite, a comma, or an em dash in their place. It says the word after a colon in a sentence is lowercase unless it is a proper noun or starts a quotation. In titles and headings the word after a colon is capitalized.
- Exclamation points and questions should be rare; customers come for answers.
- Slashes should not stand in for or.

### Numbers

The numbers page (June 2022) spells out zero through nine and uses numerals for 10 and above. It uses numerals for measurements, times of day, percentages, and anything the reader must type, and it adds a rule that one numeral in a category makes every number in that category a numeral. It forbids starting a sentence with a numeral. On percentages the guide contradicts itself across pages of different dates. The 2022 numbers page writes a numeral followed by the word percent in its examples. The percent entry in the A to Z list (November 2023) and the numbers checklist (April 2025) both call for the % sign with numerals in place of the word. The What's new log records the 2023 and 2025 changes, so the sign is the current rule and the numbers page is out of date.

### The A to Z word list

Most of the 980 pages are single-term entries, many of them about Microsoft product names or UI vocabulary. A smaller set gives general usage rules. Those that bear on the skill follow. The `can` entry suggests deleting the word because it states ability where the sentence could state the action, and it adds `might` for possibility and a ban on `may` because it can read as permission. The `should vs. must` entry (June 2025) limits `should` to optional recommendations and `must` to requirements, and asks for `might` when a statement cannot be definite. `Ensure` is defined as make sure or guarantee, and `simply` is banned as a way of saying a task is easy. The `accessible` entry warns that what is easy for the writer may not be easy for the reader. The guide avoids `enable`, `allow`, and `let` when they describe what Microsoft makes possible, preferring wording centered on what the customer does. The `if vs. whether` entry rules out `whether or not`. The `that vs. which` entry matches the newspaper rule and adds a global tip to keep `that` even where it could be dropped. Other entries cover `less vs. fewer` and `more than vs. over`. `Once` and `while` are each limited to their time sense, `impact` is allowed only as a noun, `leverage` is banned as a verb, `initiate` is banned in the sense of starting a program, and `issue` is banned as a verb and as a synonym for problem. The guide limits `please` and `sorry` to cases where the customer is inconvenienced or the product is at fault, and it discourages `we` in general.

The AI and bot term collection (June 2022) asks writers to talk about what the customer can do with the technology and to avoid discussing the technology itself where possible. The prompt entry (2024) gives terminology for AI prompting in product UI.

### Chatbots

A short 2022 section on chatbots and virtual agents asks bots to identify themselves as bots, to state what they can and cannot do, and to keep prompts short (it suggests checking reading grade level). It also asks them to confirm the user's intent before acting where a misunderstanding could cause harm and to close a resolved conversation with a wrap-up that asks whether the user needs anything else. It frames the bot as working for the customer.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The guide is a corporate house style. Its authority comes from Microsoft's scale and long practice in technical writing, and its rules rest on accumulated editorial judgment. It cites almost no published evidence. The F-pattern claim refers to unnamed studies. The chatbot section mentions unnamed research on whether users know they are talking to a bot, and the global-English pages point to two books on international English (by John R. Kohl and Edmond H. Weiss) without summarizing their evidence. Its strongest material is practical: the paired replacement tables, the global-English rules, and the procedure conventions are specific enough to apply mechanically, and they agree with the other technical guides and with plain-language practice generally.

Two features limit how far the guide generalizes. First, its voice is a brand voice, tuned to sell and to make products feel friendly, so some rules (contractions everywhere, fragments, optimism, a fun tone now and then) serve marketing and UI text more than reports or analysis. Second, pages revised at different times sometimes disagree. The percent rule is one example. The weak-verbs rule is another: its call to avoid be and have conflicts with the guide's own example sentences, which use both verbs freely, and with its machine-translation advice to add a form of be. A reader should check page dates before treating any single rule as current house style.

## What the skill applies

Microsoft's guide is a supporting source for the technical-writing skill. It is the main source for none of the skill's bullets, but it agrees with many of them. On procedures, global readers, and error messages it adds a second or third voice to the rules that Google, IBM, and Red Hat state more fully. The entries below follow the skill's sections in order.

**Section 1, bullet 1 (imperative steps, one action each, numbered when order matters).** The step-by-step page asks for a numbered list, a separate step for each instruction, complete sentences, and an imperative verb at the start of each step. It allows short steps in the same place to be combined, a looser rule than the skill's one action per step. Its single-step guidance, that a single step may not need list formatting, supports the skill's statement that a single action is a sentence.

**Section 1, bullet 2 (goal before the action).** Microsoft supports the direction of the rule more than its exact form. Its examples introduce a procedure with a to phrase that states the goal, and its headings page offers infinitive phrases for task headings. It states no rule about the order of goal and action inside a step. The bullet's source is the Google guide.

**Section 1, bullet 3 (where the action happens).** Microsoft states this rule directly. The step-by-step page asks writers to make sure the reader knows where an action takes place before describing the action, with examples that name a tab or a field first. It suggests an introductory step that names the application or location when confusion is likely. It is a second source beside Google and IBM.

**Section 1, bullet 4 (explanation after the instruction; choices; optional steps).** The review found no Microsoft rule on optional steps or on branches inside a procedure. The bullet rests on Google and IBM.

**Section 1, bullet 5 (stop at the last real action).** The step-by-step page asks writers to include the actions that complete a procedure, such as selecting an OK or Apply button. Such a step is a real action, so the rule agrees with the skill's, though Microsoft says nothing against a closing step that only announces completion.

**Section 1, bullet 6 (`please`, `thank you`, and `congratulations`).** Microsoft limits `please` and `sorry` to cases where the customer is inconvenienced or the product is at fault, and its `thanks` entry limits `thanks` in the same way. Both entries point toward the skill's rule, which is stricter: it omits `please` and `thank you` from instructions and messages altogether, following IBM.

**Section 2, bullet 1 (you, third person, let's, we).** The person page and the we entry support addressing the reader as you and discouraging we. Microsoft allows we where the company must be clear that it is the speaker, which is close to the skill's exception for a named team or organization. Microsoft does not support the skill's ban on let's; its Top 10 tips list let's among the contractions to use.

**Section 2, bullet 2 (recommendations).** The person page prefers we recommend to the impersonal it is recommended. The preference supports the skill's rule against the impersonal form and its preference for naming who recommends. The skill's further exclusion of we suggest comes from Red Hat.

**Section 2, bullet 3 (present tense).** The grammar section asks for present tense in most content and treats will and was as signs that text has left it. It does not state the skill's exception for later events in a sequence, which comes from Google.

**Section 2, bullet 4 (must, can, might, may, and should).** Microsoft's `should vs. must` entry limits `should` to optional recommendations and must to requirements and asks for might where a statement can't be definite. Its can entry treats can as ability and suggests deleting it where the sentence could state the action, and its ban on may rests on the word's reading as permission. The skill keeps may for permission, which follows Microsoft's reasoning in the other direction: Microsoft drops the word because it reads as permission, and the skill keeps it only for that reading. The need to clause and the pair don't have to and must not come from IBM and Red Hat.

**Section 2, bullet 5 (software by what it does).** The word-choice checklist's rule that devices do not think or feel is a direct source for the principle. The skill's examples come from Google, Red Hat, and IBM. Microsoft's own procedure examples describe a bar that appears, so the window example runs against Microsoft's usage (see the conflicts section).

**Section 3, bullet 1 (timeless documentation).** Microsoft supplies no rule here. The closest it comes is its `later` entry, which warns that saying a feature works in a version or later can imply future support.

**Section 3, bullet 2 (claim only what you can show).** Microsoft supplies no rule here. Its `ensure` entry defines the word without discouraging it.

**Section 3, bullet 3 (describe steps plainly).** The simply entry, which bans the word as a way of calling a task easy, and the accessible entry's warning that what is easy for the writer may not be easy for the reader support the bullet. The main source is Google.

**Section 4, bullet 1 (code formatting and exact names).** The formatting table for developer text puts commands, parameters, file paths in code, and other programming elements in code style, which supports the bullet's first sentence. The code-examples page asks writers to compile and test every example. Testing is a different safeguard from the skill's rule to copy code and output from the source, but both aim at code that works when the reader pastes it.

**Section 4, bullet 2 (name the kind of thing after an identifier).** This file does not trace the bullet to Microsoft. IBM and Red Hat are its sources, and I did not search Microsoft's formatting pages for a matching rule.

**Section 4, bullet 3 (introducing a code block).** The code-examples page asks for an introduction that describes the scenario and explains what the code may not make clear. The requirement supports the idea of a lead-in that says what the code does, though Microsoft sets no rule on its grammar.

**Section 4, bullet 4 (dates).** The global-communications pages ask writers to spell out the month, because the order of day and month varies between countries. The rule supports the skill's written-month form in prose. A search of the crawl found no Microsoft guidance on ISO dates, so the bullet's other half rests on Google, Apple, Red Hat, and Hart's.

**Section 4, bullet 5 (gigabytes and gibibytes).** Microsoft's word list has entries for the binary units, including gibibyte with the abbreviation GiB, added in a change logged on the What's new page. It supports the skill's instruction to write GiB for the binary unit.

**Section 4, bullet 6 (common forms of computing words).** Microsoft agrees with the skill on email, website, database, and lowercase internet, which its entry states as a rule. It departs on three forms: it writes file name as two words, writes webpage as one, and avoids log in and login in favor of sign in except where the interface uses the older term. The conflicts section covers all three.

**Section 5, bullet 1 (headings).** The headings page supports sentence case, no closing period, noun phrases for concept headings, and text between a heading and its first subheading, since it warns that two headings in a row may signal a problem of organization. It differs from the skill on task headings, where it suggests infinitive phrases, and on question headings, which it allows in general content.

**Section 5, bullet 2 (link text).** The URL page's rule to use a page title or a description in place of a generic phrase such as click here, and the accessibility rule that link text must make sense out of context, support the bullet. The click here example appears in the guide.

**Section 5, bullet 3 (put what the reader needs in the text).** Microsoft is not a source for this bullet.

**Section 5, bullet 4 (references by name).** The page on writing for all abilities asks writers not to rely on directional words such as above and below as the only pointer to a location, which supports the skill's rule. The clause on redefining abbreviations in each section does not come from Microsoft.

**Section 6 (writing for every reader).** Microsoft is a second source here. Its global-English tips ask writers to avoid idioms and colloquialisms. Its word-choice pages tell writers not to turn verbs into nouns, with `ask` used as a noun among the examples, which matches the skill's ban on `the install`. The review found no Microsoft rule on repeating to before each infinitive. Microsoft's advice to avoid seasons or name the hemisphere is a relative of the skill's rule on words that mean different things to American and British readers, but the skill's examples come from the Oxford references.

**Section 7, bullet 1 (code comments).** The code-examples page asks writers to explain details in comments without stating the obvious, which supports the rule that comments say what the code can't and don't restate the next line.

**Section 7, bullet 2 (error messages).** Microsoft supports two parts of the bullet. Its verbs page lists avoiding blame in error messages as a reason to use the passive, which supports the rule against blaming the user, though the skill gets there by naming the problem instead of the person. Its sorry entry tells writers to apologize only for problems the product caused and to say plainly what went wrong otherwise, which fits the skill's demand for a specific statement of the problem. The rest of the bullet (name the problem, give the likely cause and a specific fix, most likely fix first, support as a last resort) comes from IBM.

**Section 8 (commits and pull requests).** Microsoft has no counterpart. `git-submitting-patches.md` and `linux-kernel-submitting-patches.md` cover the sources.

**Section 9 (revision pass).** Item 1's simple, easy, and just have partial support in the simply and accessible entries. Item 2 enforces the modal-verb bullet, which Microsoft's `should vs. must` entry supports. Item 3's link text and above-or-below checks have Microsoft support.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out Microsoft's interface conventions: input-neutral verbs such as select and enter, the prepositions for interface elements, bold for interface labels, angle brackets for menu sequences, key names, and the guidance on alternative input methods. Agents rarely document graphical interfaces, and the skill does not cover them.

It leaves out Microsoft's formatting for error messages quoted in text, which puts them in quotation marks with sentence-style capitalization. Writing-style section 8 and the skill's section 4 put a literal that someone might copy in code formatting instead.

It leaves out Microsoft's preference for sign in over log in, its infinitive task headings, and its permission for question headings in general content, for the reasons given under conflicts.

It leaves out the brand voice, fragments for scanning, the chatbot guidance, the AI-content pronoun rule, search optimization, and the A to Z vocabulary for Microsoft products, for the reasons given in writing-style's copy of this file.

It leaves out the rule against starting a sentence with a numeral and Microsoft's colon and percent rules, which belong to writing-style.

## Conflicts with other references

Each entry below records the form the skill chose and the reason for it. The owner's choices (no em dashes, the serial comma, and the revision-pass bans) override every source, and writing-style wins where technical-writing disagrees with it. The skill sets no order among the technical guides.

**Sign in and log in (google-developer-style-guide.md, apple-style-guide.md, oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, ibm-style-guide.md).** Microsoft avoids log in and login unless the interface uses them and writes sign in and sign out instead. Google, Apple, Red Hat, and the Oxford dictionary all give login as the noun and log in as the verb, and the skill follows them. Apple also uses sign in, for internet accounts, so the two terms can mean different things in some products. The skill's rule concerns spelling. Where an interface says sign in, writing-style's rule to call a thing by one name keeps the writer on the interface's term.

**File name and filename (oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, apple-style-guide.md, ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Microsoft writes file name as two words, as IBM and Red Hat do, and uses the closed form only for programming terms such as a property name. Google, Apple, and the Oxford dictionary write filename, and the skill follows them; the closed form also matches the one-word username the skill uses.

**Webpage and web page (oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md, oxford-new-harts-rules.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, apple-style-guide.md).** Microsoft writes webpage as one word, as Google and Apple do. The Oxford sources, IBM, and Red Hat write web page, and the skill follows Oxford.

**Task and question headings (google-developer-style-guide.md, ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Microsoft suggests infinitive phrases for task headings, such as a heading that starts with to and a verb, and allows question marks in headings where meaning needs them. The skill starts task headings with a bare verb, after Google, and keeps question headings for FAQs and troubleshooting pages, after IBM. Microsoft's caution against filler text placed only to separate two headings is compatible with the skill's rule. Red Hat states the same idea from the other side: if no useful text fits between two headings, one heading is probably unnecessary.

**Let's (google-developer-style-guide.md).** Microsoft's Top 10 tips list let's among the contractions to use. Google and the skill avoid it. Microsoft's own person page, which discourages the first-person plural, is closer to the skill than the tip is.

**Appear (ibm-style-guide.md).** The skill's example says a window opens and does not appear, following IBM's appear and window entries. Microsoft's examples use appear for things that show up onscreen. Microsoft states no rule on the word, so the skill's IBM-derived rule governs.

**Thanks (ibm-style-guide.md).** Microsoft allows thanks when the customer has been inconvenienced or has gone out of their way. The skill drops thank you from instructions and messages, following IBM.

**Error messages in quotation marks (oxford-new-harts-rules.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Microsoft encloses an error message referred to in text in quotation marks. The skill's code-formatting rule for literals, and writing-style section 8's rule for anything a reader might copy, put it in code formatting instead, so no punctuation attaches to it. oxford-new-harts-rules.md and red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md reach the same result for quoted code.

**Should and may (ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, google-developer-style-guide.md).** Microsoft and Google keep should for recommendations. IBM and Red Hat avoid it by default. The skill avoids should only where its force is unclear. On may, Microsoft avoids the word, while IBM and Red Hat keep it for permission; the skill keeps it for permission only.

**First person (red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md, apple-style-guide.md).** Red Hat and Apple forbid we. Microsoft allows it where the company must be clear that it is speaking, and the skill's exception for a named team is closest to Microsoft's.

**Contractions (ibm-style-guide.md, red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Microsoft encourages contractions in all content; IBM and Red Hat ban them in technical writing. Writing-style section 7 settles the question for both skills.

**House choices.** Microsoft requires the serial comma, which matches the owner's choice, and allows the unspaced em dash, which the owner's ban overrides.
