# The New York Times Manual of Style and Usage (2015 revised edition)

- **Source:** Philip B. Corbett, ed. (revising the 1999 edition by Allan M. Siegal and William G. Connolly), *The New York Times Manual of Style and Usage*, revised and updated edition. New York: Three Rivers Press, 2015. E-book original, eISBN 978-1-101-90322-3.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** Three Rivers Press (Crown Publishing Group, Penguin Random House), 2015 e-book edition; the 1999 predecessor was published by Times Books.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-04
- **Used by:** chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose and scope

The manual is the in-house stylebook of a daily newspaper, offered to general readers. It has a short preface by the editor, Philip Corbett, followed by roughly 18,000 lines of alphabetical entries. The entries mix several kinds of guidance. Some settle spelling, capitalization, hyphenation and abbreviation. Some rule on usage, such as when to prefer one word over another. Some set journalistic standards on anonymous sources, corrections, fairness, obscenity and the description of people. Many entries are narrow reference material: county spellings in New York, military ranks, how to render Russian names, sports scoring, unit abbreviations. Most of that is irrelevant to anyone who is not a newspaper copy editor, and a reader of the whole book will find that perhaps a tenth of it bears on prose quality in general.

The 2015 edition is an update, not a rewrite. The preface says the Siegal and Connolly guidance stays in force, and that the changes concern the web (email without a hyphen, website as one word, guidance on links and social media), a few shifts in social usage, and the removal of outdated entries. It reports several changes meant to cut confusion on deadline. Numbers under 10 are now spelled out even when they sit in a series with larger numbers, and a complete sentence after a colon is always capitalized. A few words once labeled too colloquial, such as ad and the verbs host and debut, were cleared for use. New warnings were added for errors the editors kept catching, among them eponymous, anniversary, forbid versus prohibit, and may versus might.

### The philosophy in the preface

The preface is the most useful part for anyone who writes in a general register. It is short, and it states the goals plainly. The writers are journalists, and are not scholars, poets, entertainers or advocates. The aim is prose that is clear, precise and literate, and that carries important information to busy readers. Readers should not be misled by error, distracted by sloppiness, or annoyed by pedantry, polemic, slang, jargon or heedless incivility. The preface adds that the only time a reader should notice the writing is when, now and then, the reader stops to admire it. The tone should be thoughtful and civil, with room for humor, personality and surprise, but the underlying business is serious and the language should show that.

Two further commitments matter. The manual says usage should follow what educated speakers widely accept, which means the paper does not chase new words or buzzwords, and also avoids a reflexive traditionalism that would make it seem fusty. Language changes and the editors say they reckon with change carefully. The second commitment is that the rules are to be applied with thought. The preface quotes an earlier edition to the effect that a rulebook should encourage thinking, and that a single rule, common sense, would nearly do. This is the manual's real method. Almost every entry gives a reason along with a ruling, and many entries concede exceptions for headlines, quotations, dialogue or light features. The reasons are usually about the reader: what a word suggests, what a construction forces the reader to reconstruct, what a figure implies.

### Families of guidance relevant to writing quality

#### Plain words over fancy ones

A large group of entries tell the writer to prefer the ordinary word. Utilize is called a fancy word for use. Prior to is stilted, and the manual says to use before. Attempt as a verb is often stilted, and try is more conversational. Following is almost always more cumbersome than after, and the following day is stiffer than the next day. Initiate usually means open, start or begin. Capability gives way to ability or capacity, which are shorter and less stilted. Implement as a verb is called pompously bureaucratic, and conversational substitutes such as carry out and put in effect are offered. Attorney is more pretentious than lawyer and also less precise. Complimentary in the sense of free is sales talk; journalism prefers free. Lessen, level (as in on an informal level), individual as a synonym for person, parley, beset, decry and fortnight all get the same treatment: a word found mostly in newspapers or in formal registers is replaced by the word people say. The same instinct condemns hopefully as a sentence adverb only mildly, since grammarians accept it but some readers bristle, and the manual warns that the stiff alternatives (it is hoped, one hopes) are worse than the word they replace.

The test the manual applies, again and again, is conversational naturalness. A word is stilted if it appears mostly in print and rarely in speech. That test is more useful than a list, because it generalizes: when a word feels like it was put on for the occasion, find the plain one.

#### Precision of meaning

A second large group fixes the exact sense of words that have drifted. Impact as a verb means to strike with force, and in the sense of affect it is called technical jargon. Literally should not stand in for figuratively, and when it is used correctly it is often unneeded anyway. Unique means having no equal, takes no modifier such as very, and a writer who has a rare real case should say in what respect. Massive is an overworked adjective best kept for physical bulk, so a building or mountain can be massive and a budget or a fire needs a fresher description. Key as a modifier before a noun is tolerable though clichéd, but used after the noun with no article (the lobbyists were key) it should be resisted in favor of crucial or central. Epic is hyperbole to bestow sparingly. Tragedy is for calamity and not for a minor incident that is merely sad. Dilemma means a choice between bad options, not any problem. Irony in its looser sense of a mere odd coincidence is trite. Fortuitous means by chance and does not mean lucky. Enormity belongs to wickedness, enormousness to size. Presently should mean soon, and now is more direct for the other sense. Comparisons such as three times larger are unreliable, and the manual gives the arithmetic: three times larger is actually four times as large, and five times smaller cannot exist, so write seven times as large or one-fifth as much.

This family shows the manual's habit of explaining why. A word is protected because the distinction it carries is lost when the word is used loosely, and the cost lands on the reader who has to guess what was meant.

#### Clichés, vogue words, jargon and euphemism

The manual keeps a running attack on stock expressions. It labels as trite or clichéd such items as defining moment, all-time (as in records), big time, they won big, bard for Shakespeare, canine used as a noun for dog merely for variety, lion's share (which also means the whole share, not simply the largest), nitty-gritty, on hold outside telephone use, and the posh-glitzy vocabulary of society pages, for which it advises concrete detail instead. It treats journalese as a separate vice: mark in the sense of signify, climax as a verb, boost wages or boost taxes, in as many weeks, helmeted police, flag-draped coffin, and starting a sentence with a dropped article. For the mark entry the manual gives a plain rewrite: say the election is the third in seven years, and drop the verb that dresses the fact up. In as many is faulted on its logic: the phrase pretends to repeat a number it never stated.

Jargon gets equal attention. Grow used as a transitive verb for expanding a business is business jargon. Reference as a verb, input as a word for opinion, interface, outage, online for factories, in-house, catchment area, first responder, the Hill, and three clergy or hiring faculty are each flagged as the slang of some trade. The manual says do not put quotation marks around slang or jargon, because the marks convey condescension; the context must justify the word or it should go. Slang more generally is described as an in-group badge, welcome only when used unmistakably for effect, and otherwise producing the spectacle of an adult trying to pass as a teenager. Colloquial words are allowed in features and light contexts but only if they are not themselves trite.

On euphemism the manual takes a firm line: euphemisms are devices to hide harsh facts and rarely belong in the paper. People die and do not pass on. The obscenity entry says the paper writes frankly about sex, science, health and crime in clinical rather than coarse vocabulary, and explicitly warns against evasiveness. Gaming for gambling, challenged for disabled, and undocumented (with caution) are all flagged on that basis.

#### Concision and structure at the sentence level

The concision entries are brief and specific. One of the reasons is wordy and becomes a reason. Between $1.2 million and $1.6 million is wordy, and the manual suggests $1.2 million to $1.6 million. Absolutely necessary, new record, 10-year anniversary, PIN number, consensus of opinion, general consensus and 10:30 a.m. this morning are redundancies. Anywhere before a range is superfluous. The now defunct magazine produces phrasing that sounds like a literal translation, so the entry gives the magazine, now defunct. And/or is stilted, and apples or oranges, or both, replaces it.

Two sentence-level rules concern order and balance. Definitions should come before the term, so that the explanation slips by unnoticed when the reader meets the term; the manual's example puts the descriptive phrase ahead of the composers' names rather than after. Constructions of the not only, but also type, and either/or and neither/nor, need parallel elements after each half, and the entry shows how the symmetry breaks when a word is misplaced. Articles are repeated before each noun in a series unless the nouns form a single idea. Only is placed next to the word it modifies, with the manual's three-sentence demonstration of how the meaning changes. The conjunction use of like introducing a full clause is rejected in favor of as or the way. Singular indefinite pronouns take he or she on later reference, never they, and the manual advises rephrasing into a plural or removing the pronoun so the reader sees no sign of the writer's struggle. The a, an, the entry bans the habit of dropping a leading article from a sentence, and tells writers to recast sentences if several in a row begin with the same article, to avoid monotony. Fewer goes with countable things and less with bulk amounts, including a sum of money treated as a single quantity. That and which are kept distinct: that for clauses necessary to the sentence, which with commas for clauses that add extra information. Compare to likens, and compare with contrasts. Further is for degree and farther for distance.

#### Numbers, statistics and evidence

The numbers entry spells out one through nine and uses figures for 10 and above, even in a series that mixes the two. It lists a long set of exceptions: ages, percentages, money, dates, times, measurements, scores, decimals, headlines and tables. Percent is one word in text, with a figure before it, and the symbol is allowed in headlines, tables and charts. The percent entry separates percent from percentage point with a worked example: a rate going from 10 percent to 11 percent rises one percentage point but 10 percent. No decline can exceed 100 percent. The decimals entry warns against false precision, and its example is a company whose income went from $545 million to $685 million; calling that a 25.7 percent increase suggests an exactness the rounded inputs cannot support. The polls entry requires the story to say how the sample was chosen and to give the margin of sampling error in plain terms, noting that differences smaller than the margin are statistically insignificant, and to round results to whole points because tenths imply impossible precision. It also restricts the words poll and survey to scientific soundings and requires a caution on street interviews. Round numbers in the thousands take numerals.

The manual contains no entry I found that tells writers to attach a comparison to every statistic or to give absolute numbers beside relative ones. Those practices are a general discipline of data journalism, and they are not part of this book as far as my reading shows.

#### Attribution, anonymity and fairness

The anonymity entry treats an unnamed source as a last resort. If the paper must conceal a name, it should tell readers as much as possible short of breaking the promise: how the source knows the information, whether the source has a stake, and why the person will not speak openly. The entry warns against rote phrases that say little, such as that someone was not authorized to speak, and calls blind attribution a tease. It ranks descriptions by specificity: a diplomat of a named country who took part in the meeting beats a Western diplomat, which beats a diplomat, and a vague familiar-with-the-matter phrase is so loose it could describe the reporter. The sources entry adds that a source said conveys no more than somebody said. Anonymity should not shield a press officer paid to be accountable, should not cover a trivial remark, and should not be a cloak for personal attacks, since vivid language from an unnamed speaker gives an unfair edge.

The fairness entry says the news report takes no sides and plays no favorites in what it covers or omits. A development announced by an institution should normally be reported before critics' objections. Innuendo is forbidden: an accusation that would not be acceptable if stated outright cannot be implied by sly juxtaposition. Word choices should not tilt: if one politician is firm, an opponent should not be rigid, and worshipful tone is as partial as hostile tone. Divisive subjects call for extra care. The editors' notes entry deals with lapses of fairness or balance, which the manual says can be as grave as factual errors; the example is failing to seek comment from someone accused in print. The corrections entry commits to fixing all factual errors promptly and in a way that makes sense to readers who never saw the original.

The quotations entry limits editing of quotations and bans bracketed additions and ellipses that distract from the speaker's meaning, preferring paraphrase. Attribution should arrive before a quotation runs past a phrase or short sentence, and a long quotation should not hang from an abrupt tag. The tense entry says to find the governing verb of an attribution. None of this is about the word said in particular; I found no standalone entry that ranks said above other attribution verbs, though the manual's examples use it throughout.

#### Describing people

Several entries set out how to write about people. Race, ethnicity, disability, age, family status and similar traits are mentioned only when pertinent and when the pertinence is clear to the reader. The race entry gives a contrast: a hate-crime victim's race is germane, while the race of a convicted person is not unless the case has racial overtones, which should then be explained. The disability entry prefers a specific description to a general label and treats disabled as an adjective, not the disabled as a noun. The men and women entry says copy treats the sexes equally and avoids stereotypes, and that thoughtful writing avoids offense without drawing attention to the pitfalls. It tells writers not to call a woman a grandmother unless an equivalent would be used for a man, and not to describe a woman's looks or clothes reflexively; specifics should be supplied for both sexes. The illegal immigrant entry notes that the term is viewed as loaded by some, advises describing the specific circumstances instead, and cautions that the alternative undocumented has a flavor of euphemism, so the guidance is to explain actions without taking sides. Lifestyle is called shopworn and, in phrases like gay lifestyle, it implies that all gay people live alike. Community in phrases like the black community implies unanimity.

#### Punctuation and form

The comma entry omits the serial comma in a simple series and keeps it when confusion would result (the manual's example is a pair of items that include and, such as gin and dry vermouth, set beside another clause). The dash entry is the most distinctive on punctuation: the dash is often misused for the comma, and it is overused; a sentence with more than two dashes confuses the reader because asides cannot be told from the main thread. The dash is one em wide, set with spaces around it in the newspaper's columns, with an en dash reserved for minus signs. The manual allows the dash for a series set inside the sentence, for abrupt breaks, and before namely, and it forbids a dash beside a comma, semicolon or colon. In headlines it advises avoiding dashes as ungainly, and offering the colon, semicolon or parentheses instead. The colon entry says to capitalize a complete sentence after a colon. The exclamation point entry says to use it rarely, only for shouted or deeply emotional quotations, since overuse loses impact, as advertising shows. Contractions are spelled out in straightforward news copy and allowed in quotations, light copy and headlines. The enumeration entry prefers first, second, third to firstly and secondly, and the bullets guidance asks for a complete introductory sentence so readers need not look back to finish the thought. Headlines should read as a simple sentence with the small words restored, and must carry the news by their words alone because online they often appear apart from images.

### Strength and generality of the guidance

The manual is not evidence in the sense that a study is. It records the editorial judgment of professional editors over decades, and it is persuasive mainly through its reasons. Many reasons are checkable on their face: the arithmetic of times larger, the logic of as many, the lost distinction in impact. Others rest on taste, such as the objection to hopefully or to the stilted-versus-conversational contrast, and they reflect the usage of educated American readers of one paper in a particular period. The 2015 date also shows in places. The pronoun guidance rejects singular they on later reference, which many current style authorities have since relaxed. The dash entry reflects print column width, a constraint that does not apply to screen text. The numerous reference entries are tied to a newsroom's coverage. For general prose the transferable parts are the preface's goals, the plain-word and precision entries, the cliché and euphemism entries, the numbers and statistics cautions, the attribution and fairness standards, and the punctuation discipline on dashes and exclamation points.

One further point: the manual contains no discussion of machine-written text, which did not exist as a concern in 2015. Nothing in it identifies the habits that make language-model prose recognizable. It applies to those habits only by analogy, where a rule against stock phrases or empty intensity happens to catch them.

## What the skill applies

The manual is the highest-precedence style source for the skill, and its preface supplies the skill's whole premise. The opening of SKILL.md describes a good newspaper as the model, with clear, precise, literate prose, a thoughtful and civil tone, room for personality, and a reader who should notice the writing only when it is good. Those phrases follow the preface's statement of goals nearly point for point. The skill's closing instruction in its second paragraph, to break a rule when it makes a sentence worse, comes from the preface's insistence on common sense and wise exceptions.

**Section 2, Words.** The first rule lists plain substitutes: use for utilize, before for prior to, try for attempt, after for following, start for initiate, help for facilitate. Four of those pairs come directly from the manual's entries on utilize, prior to, attempt, following and initiate. The rule's stated reason, that fancy words add length and make the writer sound less sure, extends the manual's reasoning about stilted and pretentious wording. The rule about exact senses (impact, literally, unique, massive, key) reproduces the manual's rulings on those five words, including the point that unique takes no modifier and the point about key as a free-standing adjective. The cliché rule draws on the manual's repeated charge that stock phrases are trite, and its example list includes defining moment's close relatives rather than copying the manual's list. The jargon rule borrows the manual's treatment of jargon as the shorthand of a trade, and its advice to translate or define comes from the manual's attitude toward specialist terms for a general audience. The euphemism rule is the manual's: people die, and the skill adds fail and lose money in the same spirit. The rule on calling a thing by one name takes the manual's complaint about canine used for variety and generalizes it. The rule against coining acronyms follows the manual's abbreviation guidance, which tells writers to avoid unfamiliar or specialized short forms.

**Section 3, Sentences.** The padding rule (one of the reasons becomes a reason) is the manual's entry on one of the, nearly verbatim in idea. The advice to keep a series parallel, and to give either/or and not only/but also the same kind of element after each half, comes from the manual's entries on those constructions. The rule on punctuation for asides and the instruction to drop a mark that does not clarify trace to the manual's comments on dashes as an overused stand-in for commas. The exclamation rule (only inside quotations) restates the manual's exclamation point entry. The mild variety rule, vary how consecutive sentences begin, takes its wording from the manual's note about repeating the same article at the start of consecutive sentences. The ban on trailing commentary clauses, the empty openers rule and the active-voice default come from other sources and the manual says little on them.

**Section 4, Numbers and evidence.** The percent versus percentage point rule restates the manual's worked example about interest rates. The rule on small bases and raw numbers leans on the manual's false-precision warning and on its poll guidance, though the specific advice to show raw counts is the skill's own. The attribution rule (say where facts come from, attribute what you did not verify) draws on the manual's anonymity and sources entries, which insist that readers be told how a claim is known. The false-balance and hedging rules come from other references; the manual's fairness entry supports the opposite caution, against implied accusation and tilted word choice, which is why the skill states claims directly and attributes them. The instructions to give every number a comparison, to pair relative with absolute figures, and to adjust money for inflation are not in the manual and come from elsewhere.

**Section 5, People and fairness.** The rule to mention race, gender, age, disability or religion only when relevant is the manual's pertinence test, applied across several entries. The advice to use labels groups use for themselves and to lead with the person fits the manual's disability and ethnicity entries. The advice to avoid metaphors from disability or illness is an extension; the manual warns against disparaging terms for disabilities and against challenged as a euphemism. The gender-neutral nouns rule follows the manual's men and women entry, including its point that the best solutions spare the reader any sign of the writer's struggle. The singular they allowance in the skill departs from the manual and is covered below.

**Section 6, Structure and format.** The list rule that items be introduced by a complete phrase or sentence draws on the manual's bullet guidance. The point about parallel list items applies the manual's parallelism entries to lists.

**Section 7, Tone.** The directions to be direct and civil, and the allowance for conversational register with contractions in conversation and spelled-out forms in formal documents, rest on the preface and the contractions entry.

**Section 8, Mechanics.** This section is almost entirely the manual. The one-through-nine rule, with figures for measurements, money, ages and percentages, follows the numbers entry. The percent-in-text and symbol-in-tables rule follows the percent entry and its allowance of the symbol in tables and charts. The serial comma rule (omit in a simple series, add for ambiguity) follows the comma entry. The capital after a colon follows the colon entry and the preface's note that the rule is now unconditional for complete sentences. That versus which and fewer versus less follow their entries.

**Section 10, Revision pass.** Item 1 (replace em dashes) is stricter than the manual, as covered below, but its concern with dashes as a stand-in for commas begins with the manual's observation that the mark is overused and misused. Item 11 and other items on announcements, intensifiers and hedges owe little to the manual.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out nearly all of the reference material: the capitalization of titles, the spelling of place and personal names, military ranks, sports scoring, units, abbreviations by organization, courtesy titles, datelines and the like. None of that bears on how an agent writes a chat reply or a report.

It omits the manual's headline rules, which concern copy fitting, bank lines and print columns. It omits newsroom ethics procedures such as the correction process, the structure of an editors' note, dateline integrity and the handling of obscenity. The skill's tone section captures the civility but not the apparatus.

It omits the manual's long list of individual word rulings that are about accuracy of fact in a news setting, such as the difference between a county and a township, or between allegedly and a charge. A few usage rulings are left out because they are disputed or dated: the preference against hopefully, the rejection of like as a conjunction, the ban on singular they, and the instruction to write he or she as a last resort. The skill also leaves out the manual's rule on quotations with ellipses and brackets, because agent prose rarely quotes speakers in the way a news story does.

The skill does not adopt the manual's list of trite words wholesale. It keeps a short cliché list in section 2 and adds items that are common in business and technical writing, because the manual's examples come from sports, society and political reporting. The specific phrases the manual condemns are not repeated in the skill, so the skill does not claim authority for items the manual does not list.

The manual's rule on polls (sampling method, margin of error) is only partly used. The skill tells the writer to prefer counts to percentages on small bases, but it does not require a margin-of-error sentence, since most agent output does not report surveys.

## Conflicts with other references

**Dashes.** The manual permits the em dash with spaces on both sides and warns only against overuse, which it sets at more than two dashes in a sentence, and against using it as a replacement for commas. It allows en dashes only as minus signs. The skill bans em dashes outright. That is a deliberate choice by the user and is stricter than the manual. The skill's revision pass also covers spaced hyphens and en dashes used as dashes. The grounds for the ban sit in freeburg-em-dashes.md, which accounts for why models overuse the mark, and the manual's own caution about overuse supports it from the human side. The ban does not contradict the manual so much as tighten its warning to zero. Where the manual says to use a colon, semicolon or parentheses for headline asides, the skill adopts those same alternatives for body text.

**Singular they.** The manual's entry on anybody, anyone, everybody and similar words requires he or she on later reference and calls a plural pronoun with a singular antecedent not tolerable, and its men and women entry reaches the same position. The skill in section 5 allows singular they for an unknown person and respects the pronouns a specific person uses. This follows later style guidance and current usage. I did not open ap-stylebook.md or chicago-manual-of-style.md to check what each says about it, so I do not claim here which of them supports the skill, only that the skill departs from the 2015 manual on this point. The precedence rule for manuals would favor the NYT, but the skill chooses current usage on this one point because the manual's text is dated and because the preface itself says rules should bend to widely accepted usage.

**Voice.** The manual contains no rule on active versus passive voice that I found, apart from its dislike of nominalized, bureaucratic phrasing like implement and the criticism of agentless wording in a few entries on police-blotter phrases such as allegedly. The skill's active-voice default therefore rests on its own justification and on rhodes-active-passive-voice.md and reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md, which I did not reread here. There is no real conflict with the manual.

**Not X, but Y.** The manual has no entry on correction framing. The only nearby ruling is the not only, but also parallelism rule, which the skill keeps as a rule for parallel form. The skill's ban on contrast framing is the user's explicit choice, and the manual's not only/but also entry is about balance within a legitimate pair, so the two do not clash. The skill still permits a legitimate balanced pair when the reader plausibly holds the mistaken view, which is compatible with the manual.

**Recap and summaries.** The manual says nothing against recap endings. Its stress on busy readers supports the user's choice to cut redundant summaries.

**Banned vocabulary.** The skill's list of overused machine vocabulary in section 10 comes from kobak-excess-vocabulary.md, juzek-ward-lexical-overrepresentation.md and wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md. The manual does not list any of those words, but it argues for the same remedy: prefer plain words and be wary of showy ones. It does list a few words the skill also lists in the plain-word section (utilize, initiate, implement in effect), where agreement is full.

**Other style manuals.** On the serial comma, percent, the one-through-nine numbers rule and the capital after a colon, the skill follows this manual. Where the AP and Chicago differ, the stated precedence rule of NYT first resolves it. I did not check those files for their exact positions in this review.

## Review notes

I read the preface in full and the opening entries. I then searched the extracted text (about 18,800 lines) with terms for cliché, trite, journalese, jargon, stilted, pretentious, redundant, wordy, overworked and euphemism, and read the entries those searches turned up, along with the entries named in the assignment: dash, colon, comma, exclamation point, numbers, percent, contractions, either/or and not only entries, the article entry on dropped articles and monotony, hopefully, impact, unique, utilize, literally, key, massive, one of the, in as many, canine, following, prior to, attempt, fewer and less, that and which, the anonymity, sources, fairness, men and women, race, disability, illegal immigrant, polls, decimals, comparisons, definitions, headlines, quotations and slang entries. I did not read every entry: the book is long, and most of it is reference material outside this task's scope. Statements that the manual lacks an entry (on active voice, on attribution verbs, on pairing a number with a comparison, on hedging) mean I searched and did not find one; a miss in a book this size is possible.

The text I read is an extraction of the e-book, so page numbers and any formatting such as italics are not available, and I cite entries by headword only. The extraction renders dashes with spaces, so I took the spaced dash as the manual's practice.

I did not reread the other reference files in this folder for this review. At the time of writing only freeburg-em-dashes.md and juzek-ward-lexical-overrepresentation.md existed in the folder, and I looked only at the opening of the first. The conflict statements about other files rest on the brief's description of them and on the SKILL.md text, and I marked the ones I could not verify.

Every example in the "What the source covers" section is a paraphrase or a description of an entry. I used no quotation marks around source text.
