# The Chicago Manual of Style, 18th Edition

- **Source:** University of Chicago Press, *The Chicago Manual of Style*, 18th edition (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2024). Principal reviser: Russell David Harper. Chapter 5 (Grammar and Usage) is by Bryan A. Garner; chapter 4 (Rights, Permissions, and Copyright Administration) is by William S. Strong.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** Print edition, about 1,100 numbered pages plus front matter, glossary, bibliography, and index (the scanned copy reviewed here runs 1,208 PDF pages). An online edition (CMOS Online) exists at chicagomanualofstyle.org; the manual mentions it and a companion Q and A page and blog, which this review did not consult.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

### Purpose, audience, and philosophy

The Chicago Manual of Style is the standard reference for American book publishers, scholarly journals, and the editors who work for them. The 18th edition appeared in 2024, seven years after the 17th, and its preface calls it the most extensive revision in a generation. The preface frames the manual's job as balancing two things: editorial logic that has stood up over time, and what the editors call attention to real-world usage, now checkable against dictionaries and searchable corpora of published text. It illustrates the balance with a story. Editors in 1969 lowercased Roman and Arabic when they modify numerals, against a tradition going back to 1906. Usage never followed, so the 18th edition reverses that change and capitalizes the words again. The preface calls this a rare concession to actual usage, which tells the reader how the editors weigh the two forces. A rule gives way when evidence shows writers refusing it.

The preface opens with a line from the first edition (1906) saying the rules are meant for the average case and must be applied with some elasticity. It closes by naming clarity, consistency, and attention to detail as the principles that do not go out of style. That framing recurs throughout the chapters I read. Each rule includes a stated reason. The manual often tells editors when to leave an apparent error alone, for example a comma splice that works in conversational prose, a missing comma in a casual register, or a stylistic choice by a fiction writer. The manual presents itself as a tool for judgment more than a statute. It still issues firm preferences, and it states them in plain declarative language, which makes it easy to extract rules from it.

### Scope and structure

The book has three parts. Part I, Publishing and Editing, covers the parts of a book and a journal, manuscript preparation and editing, proofreading, illustrations and tables, and copyright and permissions. Part II, Style and Usage, is the section a writer of ordinary prose uses. It covers grammar and usage (chapter 5), punctuation (6), spelling, distinctive treatment of words and compounds (7), and names, terms, and titles of works (8). It also covers numbers (9), abbreviations (10), languages other than English (11), and quotations and dialogue (12). Part III covers source citation (chapters 13 and 14, with two systems, notes and bibliography as the primary one, and author-date) and indexing (15). A glossary and a bibliography of further resources close the book.

The preface lists what is new. The editors now endorse capitalizing Black, White, and Indigenous when they refer to people, and they accept singular they for anyone whose gender is unknown, irrelevant, or concealed, as well as for nonbinary people. Chapter 5 gained a thoroughly revised section on inclusive language, developed with an advisory board. New material covers Indigenous languages and sources, Korean romanization, fiction and dialogue formatting, accessibility (alt text for images, accessible ebooks and tables), and a reorganized citation section. The math chapter was dropped after usage data from the online edition showed readers rarely consulted it. Terminology changed from headline style and sentence style to title case and sentence case. Near the end of the revision, the editors added material on artificial intelligence: how copyright law bears on it, how to credit images created with it, and how to cite text generated by a chatbot. They say they are taking notes for the next edition. Several rules also changed in ways that matter for the writing-style skill, noted below: capitalization after a colon, the comma before a conjunction joining two imperative clauses, and the placement of years at the start of a sentence.

The acknowledgments name an outside advisory board of trade-publishing, journal, accessibility, linguistics, and conscious-language professionals, and thank the linguist Geoffrey Pullum for extensive comment on the grammar coverage.

### Chapter 5: Grammar and Usage

Garner's chapter begins with a position on a long argument. The introduction describes prescriptive grammar (how people should write) and descriptive grammar (how they do). It notes that each camp used to caricature the other and says the better prescriptivists now treat their advice as a description of Standard English as shown in published work. Descriptivists, for their part, have come to accept that Standard English needs some norms. The chapter then limits itself to Standard Written English, defined as the form taught in schools and used across dialect communities, and says that judging a usage nonstandard means only that it falls outside that standard. It adds that using the standard does not require rejecting one's native dialect, since people shift between varieties by context. The chapter deliberately uses older, widely known grammatical terms over newer technical ones, and it admits that grammarians disagree on the number of parts of speech (it follows the traditional eight).

Most of the chapter is a reference grammar of nouns, pronouns, adjectives, verbs, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and interjections, and I read only parts of it. The passages most relevant to prose style are these.

On voice, the verbs section defines active and passive and says that, as a matter of style, passive voice is often inferior to active. It gives a pair as illustration: a sentence saying the matter will be given careful consideration is weaker than one saying we will consider the matter carefully. It then adds that the choice may depend on whose point of view the writer wants, since a sentence about the cat catching the mouse takes the cat's side and the reverse takes the mouse's. It cautions that the mere presence of a form of to be does not mean a sentence is passive. A separate passage on dangling participles notes that a passive main clause can leave a participle with no logical subject, and the fix it recommends is to switch to active voice.

On syntax, the chapter stresses that English relies on word order, so that the subject-verb-object pattern governs meaning and departures from it signal emphasis or a question. It lists the seven basic clause patterns. The negation section is pro-economy. It shows pairs in which a negating word such as nothing, no one, never, or nowhere replaces a not plus an any-word and shortens the sentence. It says double negatives usually cancel into a mild positive and tend to produce ambiguity. The section on expletives defines the structural words it and there, which have no meaning of their own, and explains that they shift emphasis to the predicate. It warns against stacking several expletive or ordinary its close together, because the reader stumbles over which is which. The treatment is descriptive and mild; it does not tell writers to avoid the construction. The section on parallel structure is firmer. Every element of a series must match in form and grammatical function. A preposition must be repeated in each element unless all share one. Correlative pairs such as `either/or` and `not only/but also` must join matching elements, and a single auxiliary verb before a series must fit every verb phrase in it. Each rule is shown with a faulty and a corrected sentence, and the explanations are short and mechanical.

### Word usage and the glossary

Section 5.253 onward turns from grammar to usage, which it defines as the collective habits of a language's native speakers. Its central distinction (5.254, under the heading of good usage versus common usage) is that a word's presence in a dictionary does not make it fit for Standard Written English. Dictionaries record what speakers do, and lexicographers mostly disclaim any intent to guide writers. Editors therefore need a usage guide such as Fowler's or Garner's own. The glossary that follows runs about fifty pages, from `a lot` to `your` versus `you're`, with a short discussion of each problem word or pair.

The entries show a consistent method. Garner states the traditional sense, notes where common usage has drifted, and gives a verdict on a graded scale: use freely, use with care, resist, avoid, or treat as an error. He also marks changes in acceptability over time. Entries I read that bear on plain, exact prose:

- `use` and `utilize`: `use` is usually the best choice for simplicity; `utilize` is mostly an overblown alternative, though occasionally right when the meaning is to use to best effect.
- `prior to` and `previous to`: make it `before` (or `until`, for `prior to`). `following` as a stand-in for `after`: avoid it, because it can create a false reading. `in order to`: often reducible to `to` when the rhythm survives. `in regard to`: prefer a one-word substitute such as `about`. `due to the fact that`: use `because`. `in the process of`: almost always deletable. `in actual fact`: redundant.
- Unique: reserve it for one of a kind, avoid it in the sense of special or unusual, and avoid putting degree words such as very or more in front of it.
- Whether: use it alone without tacking on or not, which is needed only when the meaning is regardless of whether. While: acceptable for although or whereas in a conversational tone, but ambiguous because it can mean either time or contrast; when real ambiguity exists, choose the other word.
- Such: when used to replace this or that, as in such building was condemned, it is a symptom of legalese and no more precise than the, this, or that. Using it with a mass or plural noun to mean of that type is fine.
- Transpire: its older meaning is to become known; in the newer sense of happen or occur it still carries a faint odor of jargon and pretension, though Garner says that odor is disappearing. The entry is more tolerant than a flat ban.
- Space as a figurative noun: a voguish equivalent of area, to be avoided. Area itself is often a meaningless filler, as in the area of partnering skills, and the entry suggests deleting the area of.
- Impact: resist it as a verb, try affect or influence, because it is hyperbolic and traditionalists still call it a solecism, though it is gaining ground; avoid `impactful` as jargon. Literally: it should not be used loosely in figurative senses, and guides that accept the loose use should be disregarded.
- Presently: ambiguous, so write now or soon. Majority: prefers countable things; use most when it fits. That versus which: in edited American prose, that is restrictive and which is nonrestrictive, usually with a comma; the entry defends the distinction as a useful terminological counterpart to punctuation, while noting British writers rarely observe it. Less versus fewer: less for singular mass nouns or amounts, fewer for plural count nouns. Hopefully and important versus importantly: both treated as established sentence adverbs though some careful writers avoid them.

Two broader points come out of the glossary. First, most verdicts are advice about register: a word can be acceptable in speech and weaker in formal prose. Second, the entries lean toward the shorter and plainer word whenever the longer one adds nothing, a lean that is easy to see in the `prior to`, `in order to`, `due to the fact that`, and `utilize` entries. I did not find glossary entries for massive or for key as a free-standing adjective in my reading, and the skill's rules on those words do not come from this manual as far as I can tell.

### Inclusive language and minimizing bias (5.255 to 5.267)

The revised section is the manual's most visible change in tone. It opens by saying that writers should make conscious choices about language because biased wording can reflect unconsidered etymologies or assumptions about what is normal, while granting that eliminating all bias is unrealistic. The justification it gives rests heavily on credibility. Biased language that is not central to the meaning distracts or offends readers and lowers respect for the writer. It shifts attention from the ideas to the work's political or social subtext, and it can activate readers' own biases so they stop following the argument. The editors say they keep no list of banned terms, because standards evolve, and they apply the reasoning case by case.

Specific guidance: avoid irrelevant references to race, ethnicity, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, disability, age, weight, appearance, nationality, religion, or social standing. Use the label that the individual or group prefers, research what is current, and recognize that labels that look interchangeable may refer to different people. Emphasize the person and, when a characteristic matters, use an adjective instead of a noun for groups (the Vietnamese students, not the Vietnamese). The manual works through an example of a sentence that praises someone as a Black female graduate. It shows how the modifier pair can imply unintended implications of comparison, and contrasts that sentence with one where the same descriptors are plainly relevant. On disability, it presents person-first language (an editor who is deaf) and identity-first language (an autistic illustrator) as two legitimate approaches. It says person-first is often recommended but some readers find it stigmatizing, and that the choice depends on the audience and, when known, the person's own preference. It warns that euphemisms such as `differently abled` obscure meaning and suggest shame, and it recommends `wheelchair user` over `confined to a wheelchair`. It says figurative language built on disability (examples given are `deaf as a post`, `a lame excuse`, and `willfully blind`) will predictably offend some readers and should be replaced by plain alternatives.

On gender, the section favors dropping gendered suffixes (`author` over `authoress`). It says the suffix person rarely works well as a replacement for man (`chair` and `anchor` beat `chairperson` and `anchorperson`). It offers neutral replacements for terms such as `manpower` and `mankind`. It notes that woman used as a modifier can sound dismissive. For pronouns, it lists nine techniques for avoiding a gendered singular pronoun. They are to omit the pronoun, repeat the noun, use a plural antecedent, substitute an article, use the pronoun one, use who, use the imperative, use singular they, or revise the sentence. It observes that several of these also tighten the sentence. Singular they is accepted for indefinite pronouns and for an antecedent whose gender is unknown, irrelevant, or confidential. It is also accepted as the pronoun some individuals use, which should be respected. The section ends with a short summing-up passage on stability and renewal: usage is more stable than it feels, and good usage in context takes wide knowledge, judgment, and some literary taste.

### Chapter 6: Punctuation

The chapter opens by stating its governing idea: punctuation exists to promote ease of reading by clarifying relationships within and between sentences, so it should follow consistent principles lest subjectivity obscure meaning. How strictly the rules apply depends on context, with formal prose handled differently from conversational tone, fiction, or academic writing. For commas in particular, the manual distinguishes a closely punctuated style from an open style and tells editors to account for register and authorial voice while serving the reader.

Rules relevant to the writing-style skill:

- The serial comma (6.19) is strongly recommended. The manual says it prevents ambiguity and shows that it should be used even when the last pair is joined by and. It also shows the rare cases where it cannot rescue meaning and the sentence has to be reworded. It is omitted before an ampersand in a company name.
- Comma splices (6.23) are errors in formal prose and may be fixed with a conjunction, a semicolon, a period, parentheses, or a dash. The manual says an occasional splice can work in conversational or creative writing and that editors should judge whether a correction is a real improvement.
- Restrictive and nonrestrictive clauses (6.29): that with no comma for restrictive clauses, which with commas for nonrestrictive ones. The manual says many writers preserve the distinction and notes British writers often do not.
- Colons (6.65 to 6.71). A colon introduces an element or series illustrating what came before, and between clauses it works like a semicolon but puts weight on the second clause. The 18th edition changes the capitalization rule: when a colon introduces one or more complete sentences, the first word after it is capitalized; when a fragment follows, it is lowercased. The manual says the change is meant to help the reader see that a full sentence follows. It also gives a test for misuse: to merit a colon, the words before it must form a grammatically complete sentence, so a colon between a verb and its object is wrong.
- Exclamation points (6.75) mark an outcry or an emphatic or ironic comment and should be used sparingly to be effective. A period can work as well for a rhetorical question. The syntax chapter repeats the point for informal writing, noting that an exclamation point can signal enthusiasm in email and texts but may be read as sarcasm.
- Hyphens and dashes (6.79 to 6.100). The manual distinguishes the hyphen, en dash, em dash, and longer dashes. The en dash is a specialized hyphen for ranges, but for parallel construction the words to or through must be used when from precedes the first element, and `and` when between does. The em dash (6.91) is the most flexible dash. It sets off an amplifying or explanatory element and can substitute for parentheses, commas, or a colon, especially for an abrupt break in thought, and can even replace a period or semicolon. Two limits apply: it should never appear within another dash-set element, and apart from dialogue there should be no more than two in a sentence. The manual notes that contemporary British practice uses a spaced en dash instead, and that some non-British publishers follow it. In Chicago's own usage the em dash is unspaced.
- Lists (6.138 to 6.143). Items in a list should be parallel. Numbers or letters belong only when they serve a purpose such as order, chronology, importance, or later reference. Short, simple lists are usually better run into the text, especially when the introduction and items form one sentence; vertical lists suit long, multilevel, or prominent lists. A vertical list should be introduced by a complete sentence and a colon. If a list mixes complete sentences and fragments, the manual says to recast the items consistently or work them into the text. The manual adds that when items are long or consist of several sentences, a numbered series of ordinary paragraphs may serve better than a list.

### Chapter 9: Numbers

The overview says the choice between numerals and words should depend on size, exactness, kind of entity, and context, and that consistency sometimes yields to readability. Chicago's general rule (9.2) spells out whole numbers from zero through one hundred and certain round multiples, and uses numerals above that (thirty-two children, 103 years, 5,893 people). An alternative rule (9.3), which the manual says many scientific and journalistic publications follow, spells out only single-digit numbers and uses numerals for the rest. A number that starts a sentence is spelled out (9.5), though the 18th edition now lets a year start a sentence while recommending rewording. Within a category of related numbers in one paragraph, treatment should be consistent (9.7). Numerals are always used with abbreviated units of measure, before the word percent, in the numbered parts of a document, with symbols, in scores, and some other fixed contexts (9.8).

Percentages (9.20) are expressed in numerals except at the beginning of a sentence. In nontechnical contexts the word percent is generally used; scientific and statistical copy more commonly uses the symbol. The manual notes that percent is an adverb and is not interchangeable with the noun percentage, and that less may be used with percentages, a departure from earlier editions. Money (9.22) follows the same split: isolated amounts are spelled out up to one hundred, as in fifteen dollars, with a symbol and numeral for others. Physical quantities in general contexts follow the general rule (`seventy-five miles an hour`, `fifteen minutes`), with numerals allowed for conveniently numeric quantities such as a 40-watt bulb or when an abbreviated unit appears.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The manual's authority is institutional. It rests on more than a century of editorial practice, a large advisory network, correspondence from readers, and in the 18th edition explicit consultation of usage data. It does not report experiments on comprehension, readability, or reader preference. Its rules about readers (that the serial comma prevents ambiguity, that a capital after a colon signals a full sentence, that passive voice is often inferior) are asserted with examples rather than tested. A few rules, such as the colon change, show the editors revising a rule on stated reader-comprehension grounds, but no data is cited. The manual is American, aimed at publishing and scholarship, and its default register is formal, so its rules about the comma, the number threshold, and the semicolon carry that bias. Garner's glossary is a single author's judgment, informed by corpus counts that he mentions, with the usual strengths (consistency, explicit reasons) and weakness (some verdicts are taste). For a skill that tells an agent how to write for a general reader, the most useful features are the stated reasons attached to each rule and the graded verdicts. The verdicts let a writer see which rules are firm and which are soft.

## What the skill applies

The skill takes the following from this source, and in each case the source shaped the wording or the reasoning of a particular rule.

**Section 2 (Words).** The first bullet's replacement pairs draw on Garner's glossary entries. The pairs `use` over `utilize`, `before` over `prior to`, `about` over `with regard to`, and `whether` without `or not` all match entries I read. The skill's parenthetical on `whether or not` (keep it only when the meaning is regardless of whether) follows the glossary's own exception. The skill pairs `happen` with `transpire`, which goes slightly beyond the glossary. Garner is tolerant of `transpire` in its newer sense but admits it still sounds pretentious, and the skill takes the pretension point as decisive for a general reader. The glossary's note that a figurative `space` is a voguish stand-in for `area` is the source of the skill's rule to call a market a market. The unique rule (one of a kind, specify the respect, takes no very) is the glossary's own advice. The line on impact (for influence, say affect) follows the glossary's resistance to impact as a verb, though the skill is stricter than Garner, who calls the usage disputed but gaining ground. The literally rule follows the glossary closely. The skill's point on translating jargon takes backing from entries such as `impactful`, which Garner labels jargon, and from the glossary's repeated preference for the shorter, plainer word. The key rule and the massive rule in that same bullet did not trace to anything I found in this manual.

**Section 3 (Sentences).** Three rules draw on chapter 5 and 6. The skill's keep-a-series-parallel rule comes directly from the parallel-structure section, which requires matching grammatical elements and gives correlatives their own paragraph. That includes the specific mention of `either/or`, `neither/nor`, `both/and`, and `not only/but also`. The cut-empty-openers rule (`there are three reasons` becomes `this fails for three reasons`; `it is important that` becomes `must`) is supported by the expletives section, which explains that it and there have no meaning of their own and shift emphasis to the predicate. The negation section's examples of shortening also support it. Chicago itself is tolerant of expletives, so the skill's rule is a strengthening. The manual's warning about clashing its in one passage reinforces why the skill treats the construction as costly. The default to active voice follows chapter 5's statement that passive is often inferior. The skill's carve-out (use passive when the actor is unknown or beside the point) is consistent with Garner's remark that voice choice can depend on point of view. The rule that punctuation exists to show how parts of a sentence relate restates Chicago 6.1, as does the corollary that a mark that does not clarify should go. The skill's rule on exclamation points rests on Chicago's guidance to use them sparingly; the skill goes further by permitting them only in quotations.

**Section 4 (Numbers and evidence).** The chapters I read do not cover the percent versus percentage-point distinction, so this section owes the manual little. Its note that percent is an adverb and percentage a noun is the only link.

**Section 5 (People and fairness).** This section closely tracks 5.255 to 5.267. Mention a trait only when relevant matches the manual's call to avoid irrelevant references and its credibility argument, which the skill compresses into the clause that irrelevant mentions distract and cost the writer credibility. The rule to use the labels people and groups use for themselves is 5.259. The rule to lead with the person and use the characteristic as an adjective is 5.260's person-not-characteristic advice. Deaf employees in place of the deaf follows the manual's pattern of adjective over noun. The ban on metaphors built on disability or illness follows 5.262. It uses two of the manual's own examples of what to avoid (`a lame excuse`, and `blind to the risk` as a close paraphrase of the `willfully blind` example). The gender-neutral nouns rule (chair, firefighter, staff, people) mirrors 5.263, and the singular they sentence reflects the manual's pronoun options and its instruction to respect pronouns an individual uses.

**Section 6 (Structure and format).** The default to paragraphs and the requirement that list items be parallel and introduced by a complete sentence match 6.138 to 6.142. The manual's observation that short lists are better run into the text and that mixed complete and incomplete items should be recast supports the skill's wording on when a list is warranted.

**Section 8 (Mechanics).** Four rules rest on or agree with this manual. The owner chose the serial comma in version 2.0.0, which agrees with Chicago, Oxford, Google, Microsoft, Apple, IBM, and Red Hat and departs from NYT and AP. Chicago 6.19 is the closest manual statement of the rule the skill now uses. The colon-capitalization rule (capitalize the first word when a complete sentence follows) is Chicago 6.67 in the 18th edition, and it also agrees with NYT and AP. The rule on that and which (that restrictive, no commas; which nonrestrictive, with commas) is 6.29 and the glossary entry on that and which. The rule on fewer and less follows the glossary, though Chicago now permits less with percentages in 9.20 and the skill does not mention the exception. The numbers and percent rules take their general shape from chapter 9 (percent as a word in nontechnical text, the symbol in tables and charts) but depart from the general numbers rule, as described in the next sections.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out the great majority of the manual, which is correct because the manual's purpose differs from the skill's. The whole of Part I (book parts, manuscript preparation, proofreading marks, illustrations and tables, and rights and permissions) and Part III (citation and indexing) concern publishing production and scholarly apparatus. So do the chapters on names and titles, spelling and compounds, abbreviations, and non-English languages. An agent's chat prose, reports, and emails rarely need any of it. The skill is not a house style guide. It keeps only a few general rules on hyphenation, quotation placement, and italics, and none on title case or the capitalization of specific terms.

Within chapters 5, 6, and 9, the skill omits these parts:

- The reference grammar. Case, pronoun classes, adjective degrees, subjunctive mood, and most of the verb conjugation material matter for editors adjudicating errors, and a capable model already writes grammatical English. The skill spends its words on habits the model is prone to, not on grammar it has mastered.
- Most of the glossary. Hundreds of entries distinguish confusables (adduce, deduce, induce; abjure, adjure), settle spelling, or concern legal terms. Those entries do not drive reader trust or clarity in general prose.
- Detailed comma rules (introductory phrases, dates, addresses, quotations, antithetical elements, and spousal commas). The skill relies on the general principle that punctuation clarifies, and it would add length to a skill whose revision pass is already long.
- The dash rules beyond the em dash ban (the en dash, since ranges use to).
- The em dash rules. Chicago permits em dashes with limits; the skill bans them by the user's explicit choice.
- Chicago's numbers rule for spelling out through one hundred, discussed below, plus most of the numeric special cases, such as addresses, Roman numerals, large numbers in scientific notation, and currency codes.
- The glossary's tolerances. Garner often says a usage is acceptable though disputed (`hopefully`, `more importantly`, `transpire` in the sense of happen). The skill keeps its rules shorter and flatter than the manual's graded verdicts, because a skill that lists hedged verdicts would be longer and harder to apply.

The source's evidence quality also limits what the skill borrows. The manual offers authority and reasoning but no experimental evidence, so the skill treats its rules as sound defaults and not as findings. Where studies exist in other reference files (on voice, hedging, and vocabulary), they weigh more for the claim they address.

## Conflicts with other references

**NYT Manual of Style (nyt-manual-of-style.md) and AP Stylebook (ap-stylebook.md).** This manual conflicts with the other two in four places.

On the serial comma, Chicago strongly recommends it in every series of three or more, including cases where the last pair is joined by and, because it prevents ambiguity. NYT and AP omit it in simple series. The owner chose the serial comma in version 2.0.0, which agrees with Chicago, Oxford, Google, Microsoft, Apple, IBM, and Red Hat and departs from NYT and AP. The owner's explicit choices override every source, so on this point the skill follows Chicago.

On numbers, Chicago's general rule spells out zero through one hundred and round multiples (`sixty-five miles`, `fifteen dollars`, `thirty-two children`), which means it spells out far more than NYT and AP. Chicago does describe an alternative rule that spells out only single-digit numbers and says many scientific and journalistic publications use it. The skill's rule (words for one through nine, figures for 10 and above) is that alternative. Its extra rule that measurements, money, ages, percentages, and data always take figures goes beyond what Chicago's general rule does for money and ordinary physical quantities. Chicago's list of contexts that always take numerals (9.8) covers percentages, abbreviated units, and several others. The skill follows NYT and AP, which agree with each other, because agent replies and reports sit closer to the journalistic and scientific writing that Chicago says uses the alternative rule than to book prose. Chicago's number-starting-a-sentence rule is not in the skill.

On colons, the earlier assumption that Chicago lowercases after a colon does not hold for the 18th edition. Chicago now capitalizes the first word when a complete sentence follows, whether one sentence or several, and lowercases it when a fragment follows. NYT and AP also capitalize a complete sentence after a colon, so all three sources and the skill agree. The 17th edition capitalized only when the colon introduced two or more sentences, so a reader who uses older Chicago advice would see a conflict that no longer exists.

On percent, Chicago agrees with NYT: use the word in nontechnical text and the symbol in scientific and statistical copy. AP now uses the symbol with figures. The skill writes percent in prose and uses the symbol in tables, which fits both NYT and Chicago.

On dashes, Chicago uses the unspaced em dash and endorses it as the most flexible dash, with a limit of two per sentence outside dialogue and a rule against nesting. NYT allows the spaced em dash with similar limits, and AP uses spaced dashes. Chicago uses the en dash for ranges (with to or through required after from and `and` after between). The skill bans the em dash and writes ranges with to, a stricter choice made on the user's instruction. Chicago acknowledges the British spaced en dash as a variant, which the skill also does not use.

**freeburg-em-dashes.md.** Chicago presents the em dash as a normal, flexible mark of good prose, with limits, and Freeburg's data indicates human writers also use em dashes at nontrivial rates. Both sources therefore argue against treating the em dash as a reliable marker of machine writing. The skill bans it anyway because the user asked, not because either source shows the mark is inherently wrong, and it limits its argument to readers' present recognition of the pattern.

**rhodes-active-passive-voice.md and reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md.** Chicago says passive voice is often inferior to active and recommends active to cure danglers. Rhodes reports no comprehension difference by voice, and Reinhart finds that instruction-tuned models produce agentless passives at roughly half the human rate. Those three sit awkwardly together. The skill resolves the tension by justifying active voice on accountability (it names who acted) and not on comprehension. It permits the passive when the actor is unknown or beside the point, which Chicago's point-of-view remark also allows. It does not claim that readers understand active sentences faster.

**boggia-artificial-epanorthosis.md.** Chicago's negation section treats negation as a normal resource and prefers fewer words (nothing for not anything), and its coverage says nothing against the correction-framing pattern. No conflict arises, but the manual offers no support for the skill's ban on not-X-but-Y constructions, which comes from the user's choice and from Boggia's findings.

**one-token-away-from-collapse.md, taboo-negative-constraints.md, and anthropic-prompting-docs.md.** Garner's glossary is a list of words to avoid, and it names them in the entries. The two studies found that a model obeys bans like these when they are in its prompt but writes a thinner answer, and the prompting docs prefer positive instructions. An earlier version of this entry cited a claim that naming the words primes them; that claim was dropped. The skill takes the glossary's content but keeps its word lists in the post-draft revision pass and phrases the drafting sections around the plain word to use instead.
