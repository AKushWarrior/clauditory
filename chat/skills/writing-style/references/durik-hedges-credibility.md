## What the source covers

### Purpose and framing

The paper asks whether hedges weaken persuasive writing, and it argues that the question is too coarse. A hedge is a word or phrase that marks a claim as less than certain, such as sort of, kind of, probably, possibly, may, likely, or seem to. Earlier work on powerless speech had treated hedges as a sign of a weak speaker and predicted that any hedge would cost the writer credibility and persuasive force. Scientific and academic writing, though, uses hedges constantly and is expected to, because accuracy often requires admitting what the evidence does not settle. The authors therefore set up two competing expectations. One says that hedges as a category lower credibility. The other says that the effect depends on where the hedge appears in the argument and on what kind of hedge it is. They also used dual-process accounts of persuasion (the elaboration likelihood model and the heuristic-systematic model). Those accounts allow a hedge to act as a surface cue for some readers and as input to a careful evaluation of the argument for others.

The paper builds on an earlier study by Blankenship and Holtgraves (2005) and reuses that study's editorial and most of its hedge words. Two experiments, each with college students, test one factor apiece: placement in the first and type in the second.

### Experiment 1 on where the hedge goes

The first experiment used 150 students at a Midwestern university. About 40 percent came from an introductory psychology course and about 60 percent from a research methods course, usually taken by juniors. Slightly under half were men (46 percent of those who reported gender). Students took part for course credit, in groups of up to 20, under a cover story that the psychology department was helping a college evaluate editorials.

The material was a short editorial of about 310 to 320 words, written at a twelfth-grade reading level, that urged the participants' own university to require "comprehensive exams" for seniors. The topic was chosen as probably counterattitudinal for students, and the proposal was attributed to the university president. The editorial came in three versions assigned at random. The control version had no hedges. The data-hedge version had six hedges (sort of twice, kind of twice, probably once, possibly once), placed mostly on statements of data, such as figures about students. The interpretation-hedge version used the same six hedges moved onto statements that interpret or predict, such as what the exams would probably do for graduates. Because all six hedges were colloquial in this experiment, placement was varied while type was held constant, though the data condition still left a few hedges on interpretations, so the contrast was not perfectly clean.

Participants rated their attitude toward the exam policy on seven-point scales (four semantic differentials and one agreement item, reliability .90). They also rated the source (four items covering knowledge, trustworthiness, likability, and competence, reliability .81) and the argument (four items on soundness, logic, reasoning, and strength, reliability .91). A manipulation check asked how professional the language was and how many particular hedge words appeared. An open question asked for suggested improvements.

The manipulation check worked: the control editorial was rated most appropriate in language, the interpretation-hedge version next, the data-hedge version lowest, with all three pairs differing (F = 23.24 with 2 and 147 degrees of freedom). On the outcomes, the data-hedge editorial did worse than the other two, while the control and interpretation-hedge versions did not differ from each other. For attitudes the means were 4.73 for the control, 4.47 for interpretation, and 3.84 for data. For source evaluation they were 4.72, 4.55, and 4.00 in that order. For argument quality they were 4.85, 4.74, and 3.87 in that order. All three omnibus tests were reliable at the .01 level (F values of 6.07, 6.57, and 8.57). The same six hedges cost the writer roughly a point on the seven-point scale for argument quality when they were placed on data and almost nothing when they were placed on interpretation.

An exploratory analysis added course as a factor and found an interaction on source evaluation (F = 3.11 with 2 and 144 degrees of freedom, p below .05). No pairwise comparison reached significance on the Tukey test, so the authors relied on effect sizes. Research methods students rated the unhedged source lower than introductory students did (effect size 0.65). Only the research methods students separated data placement from interpretation placement, rating the data-hedged source far lower (effect size 0.82, against 0.04 for introductory students). The authors treated this as unplanned and suggested that sensitivity to appropriate hedging grows with scientific training.

### Experiment 2 on what kind of hedge

The second experiment used 149 introductory psychology students at the same university (mean age 18.7, 66 percent women, mostly White). Every hedge in this experiment was on an interpretation statement, which Experiment 1 had shown to be the safe location. The control editorial had no hedges. The professional-hedge version (296 words) used words common in research articles, drawn from a published list: may twice, probably twice, seem to once, and likely once. The colloquial-hedge version (299 words) used sort of twice, kind of twice, somewhat once, and possibly once, and five of those six came from the earlier study.

The new element was a measure of scientific reasoning, a 10-item classroom test by Lawson (2000), entered as a continuous predictor centered on its mean. The authors expected readers with weaker scientific reasoning to rely on the surface look of the hedges and readers with stronger reasoning to judge whether the hedges fit. The dependent measures repeated Experiment 1's. The paper does not report their reliabilities again, and it does not report the reasoning scores' mean or spread. Analysis was by multiple regression with dummy codes for the two hedge conditions, with reasoning and its products with the dummies added in a second step.

Colloquial hedges lowered the manipulation-check rating of language appropriateness (a drop of about one scale point, from a fitted 5.53 to 4.53) while professional hedges did not. Colloquial hedges also lowered source evaluation (about half a point, t = -2.95) and argument quality (about six-tenths of a point, fitted 5.31 down to 4.68, t = -2.81). The effect on attitudes was marginal (t = -1.74, p = .08, fitted 4.99 down to 4.55). Professional hedges differed from the control on none of these measures, so they did no harm but also gained no credit. The reasoning moderator appeared for source evaluation: the interaction with the colloquial condition was reliable (t = 2.07, p below .05). Readers one standard deviation below the mean on reasoning rated the colloquial-hedge source clearly lower than the control source (a drop of about nine-tenths of a point). Readers one standard deviation above the mean showed no clear difference (about two-tenths of a point, p = .52). Reasoning also had a main effect on source ratings. The authors read this as low-reasoning readers relying on the hedge style as a quick cue to the writer's competence, while high-reasoning readers looked past it. Argument-quality ratings showed the colloquial penalty regardless of reasoning, and the paper does not claim the moderation extended to attitudes.

### Conclusions the authors draw

Hedges do not damage persuasion as a class. They hurt when they weaken a claim that the reader expects to be firm, which here means statements of data, and they hurt when their wording sounds casual even if the placement is appropriate. Careful hedging on interpretation, in the register of research writing, is tolerated. The authors add that their conditions favored careful reading: participants knew the policy would affect their own university the following year and had unlimited time. They expect smaller effects under low motivation or low ability, and they list social context, politeness uses of hedges, and whether a message is easy to refute as open questions.

### Strengths and weaknesses of the evidence

The design is simple and the manipulations are tight: same editorial, same word count within a few words, same number of hedges, with only placement or type varied. The manipulation checks confirmed that readers noticed the differences. Two experiments with two distinct manipulations, and a pattern (data hedges bad, interpretation hedges neutral, colloquial hedges bad, professional hedges neutral) that fits a coherent story, give the result more weight than a single study would.

The limits are real. Both samples are university students reading one editorial on one topic, so nothing is known about other genres, topics, or readers. The editorial was probably counterattitudinal, and the reading conditions encouraged scrutiny, which may inflate how much readers attend to hedges. Cells held about 50 people, so the professional-hedge null results cannot show that professional hedges are harmless, only that no difference was detected. Placement and type were studied in separate experiments with different samples, so the paper cannot say how they combine. In Experiment 1 the course interaction is post hoc and rests on effect sizes after the formal tests failed. In Experiment 2 the reasoning interaction is on one outcome, the reasoning scores are not described, and a related interaction on the manipulation check was only marginal. The manipulation check itself asked readers to count and rate hedges, which may have drawn attention to them. The attitude effects were small and mostly unreliable, so the strongest findings concern perceived credibility and argument quality, not persuasion itself.

### Implications for writers

Three points follow with reasonable confidence. State measured facts plainly, since hedging a number invites the reader to doubt the number. Put uncertainty on the inference, the forecast, or the explanation, where a careful reader expects it. And when a hedge is needed, use the plain, formal modal or verb (may, likely, probably, appears to) rather than casual softeners (sort of, kind of, somewhat). Less certain is the advice for readers who are not students reading an editorial, which the paper cannot support directly.

## What the skill applies

The source supports one rule almost word for word, in section 4 (Numbers and evidence): the bullet that says to state facts and data plainly and to hedge the interpretation that is actually uncertain. The bullet's closing sentence, that hedged data reads as unreliable and a hedged inference reads as honest, compresses Experiment 1. The first half matches the finding that data hedges lowered attitude, source, and argument ratings, and the second half matches the finding that interpretation hedges cost almost nothing. The word "honest" is the skill's gloss. The paper measured perceived competence and argument quality, not honesty.

The source also reinforces the vocabulary rule in section 2 (Words) about plain, exact words, and the "Hedge stacks" item in section 10 (Revision pass). Experiment 2 found that casual softeners such as sort of and kind of lowered credibility. The finding supports cutting them. The revision item names stacked hedges such as "may potentially" and "could possibly," and tells the writer to use one hedge or none. That specific limit comes from the skill's own judgment about padding, not from the paper, whose editorials contained six hedges in about 300 words without penalty when they were professional and well placed. The paper justifies the claim "hedges on interpretation are acceptable" and the claim "casual hedges cost credibility," not the claim "one hedge is the right number."

Finally, the section 4 bullet on false balance and the example under "Reporting a finding" use the same split: the example states the measured cancellation rates flatly and puts the single hedge on the untested cause. That example is the skill's illustration of the Durik pattern.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out the scientific reasoning moderator. It concerns individual differences in the reader, which a writer cannot see or adjust to in an agent setting, and it rests on a single interaction on a single outcome in one sample. The skill also omits the course-level findings from Experiment 1, which were post hoc and not supported by the formal pairwise tests.

It does not adopt the paper's contrast between "professional" and "colloquial" hedges as a named distinction. The practical advice it keeps is narrower: avoid casual softeners and use one plain hedge on a real inference. The paper's professional list (may, probably, likely, seem to) is not reproduced in the skill, because the skill's guidance on hedges is meant to be short and because a list of approved hedges would invite using them all. The skill also does not claim that professional hedges gain extra credibility, since the paper found none.

The persuasion-theory material (elaboration likelihood, heuristic-systematic processing), the discussion of politeness hedges, and the suggestions about message refutability are background for the authors' future work and have no bearing on agent prose. The attitude-change results are omitted because they were weak and because the skill is concerned with clarity and credibility, not with changing minds.

## Conflicts with other references

No direct conflict with the style manuals, which are silent on hedging in this sense. Two sources bear on the same ground. Bakhshi (`bakhshi-rhetorical-miscalibration.md`) concerns how confident machine text sounds relative to its evidence. The file `anthropic-prompting-docs.md` favors telling a model what to do rather than what to avoid. Neither is in tension with Durik as the skill uses it. If Bakhshi's measurements show that models hedge too little or too much in some settings, that would affect how often to apply the hedge rule, not where to put the hedge; Durik addresses placement and wording only. I did not read those files closely while writing this one, so that statement is a judgment from the brief's descriptions and has not been checked against the files.

One real tension is internal to the evidence base. Durik shows that readers tolerate and even expect hedging on interpretation, while the skill's revision pass tells the writer to remove hedge stacks and keep at most one. The skill resolves this by following Durik on placement and the register of the hedge, and by following its own padding concern on quantity, since stacked qualifiers make the sentence longer and blur which part is uncertain. The wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md and the vocabulary studies (Kobak, Juzek and Ward, Liang) say nothing about hedge placement, so there is no conflict with them.
