# Why Does ChatGPT Delve So Much? Sources of Lexical Overrepresentation in Large Language Models

- **Source:** Tom S. Juzek and Zina B. Ward, "Why Does ChatGPT 'Delve' So Much? Exploring the Sources of Lexical Overrepresentation in Large Language Models," Proceedings of the 31st International Conference on Computational Linguistics (COLING 2025). Florida State University. Preprint: arXiv 2412.11385.
- **Type:** peer-reviewed conference paper
- **Where to find it:** ACL Anthology 2025.coling-main.426; preprint at https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11385 (HTML version at https://arxiv.org/html/2412.11385v1)
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-04

## What the source covers

### Purpose and framing

The paper starts from an observation that circulated on social media and in several preprints during 2024: certain words, delve above all, turned up far more often in scientific abstracts from 2023 and 2024 than in earlier years. The common assumption was that researchers using ChatGPT and similar tools caused the jump. Juzek and Ward set two goals. The first is a repeatable method for finding such words, so the list does not depend on someone's impression of what sounds machine-made. The second is to ask why a language model would favor these words in the first place. They call that second question the puzzle of lexical overrepresentation. The authors are linguists and philosophers of science rather than machine learning engineers, and the paper reads as an exploratory study that says plainly what it could not settle.

### Corpus and identification method

The corpus is PubMed's repository of biomedical abstracts, downloaded as a snapshot dated 4 May 2024. It holds about 26.7 million abstracts and more than 5.2 billion tokens, and the authors analyze 1975 through May 2024. Frequency is measured in occurrences per million words (opm) for each inflected form, with no lemmatizing, because part of speech is often unclear for a single token.

The method has three steps. Step one compares each token's opm in 2024 with its opm in 2020, a year chosen because LLMs were not yet in wide use. About 7,300 tokens showed an increase that passed a chi-square test. The two authors then went down the list from the largest percentage increases and removed tokens whose rise had an obvious explanation, such as year numbers, or words tied to the omicron variant or the metaverse. They worked independently, and any disagreement was resolved by keeping the word. They stopped at 50 words whose spike had no evident cause.

Step two checks whether a model actually favors those words. The authors drew 10,000 random PubMed abstracts from 2020 and had ChatGPT-3.5 summarize each in a couple of sentences. They then asked the same model to write a roughly 200-word scientific abstract from each summary. That two-stage prompt imitates a researcher who feeds notes to the chatbot, which was the likely workflow when the models could not take a full paper as input. The model returned 9,953 abstracts (it declined a few, probably over topic sensitivity). Words used significantly more often in the machine abstracts than in the human originals, again by chi-square, form the model-overuse list.

Step three intersects the two lists. A word that spiked for no obvious reason and is also overused by the model counts as a focal word. The result is 21 focal words, all inflected forms: delves, delved, delving, delve, showcasing, showcases, boasts, underscores, underscore, underscoring, comprehending, intricacies, intricate, surpassing, surpasses, garnered, emphasizing, realm, groundbreaking, advancements and aligns.

### Per-word frequency changes

The appendix gives opm for 2020 and 2024 for each focal word. The changes are large because the 2020 baselines are tiny. Delves rose from 0.21 to 14.38 opm, an increase of roughly 6,700 percent. Delved went from 0.12 to 2.90, and delving from 0.12 to 2.38. Delve itself rose from 0.58 to 8.50 (about 1,375 percent). Showcasing climbed from 0.59 to 8.79. Underscores went from 4.50 to 45.19 and underscore from 7.42 to 36.40. Intricate rose from 6.22 to 44.22, a roughly sevenfold jump. Emphasizing rose from 8.30 to 41.27, and advancements, the mildest case, from 12.49 to 47.17 (about 278 percent).

A second table compares these words across corpora, using the ChatGPT-3.5 abstracts, abstracts generated by a later model (GPT-4o-mini), arXiv abstracts, the Leipzig news corpus, PubMed averaged over all years, and Wikipedia. Delves appears about 183 times per million in the ChatGPT-3.5 text against well under 4 per million in every human corpus. Boasts, delving, showcases and intricacies show similar gaps. Common baseline words such as the, of and data behave much the same across corpora, which gives the comparison a sanity check. With GPT-4o-mini the picture shifts in places: boasts stops being overused, delve is still overused but less so, and underscore and its forms become far more frequent (over 1,000 opm for underscore). The authors list several possible reasons, including the shared GPT-3.5 summaries used as input, deliberate intervention by the developer, and reactions among the people who rate model outputs. They say overuse remains a feature of current ChatGPT versions and conclude it is likely to persist.

### Hypotheses about causes

The paper lays out seven candidate sources and stresses that they are not mutually exclusive: the initial training data, the fine-tuning data, model architecture, choice of algorithms (tokenization is the example given), context priming by the scientific-writing genre, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), and other settings such as temperature and top-k sampling. The authors note that most of what would discriminate among these is secret, even for open-weight models, since training data, fine-tuning steps and the identity of human raters are undisclosed. The study therefore uses indirect probes.

Training data. Because the focal words are rare in arXiv, news, Wikipedia and earlier PubMed text, it is unlikely that the model simply reproduces their frequency in its pretraining data. The authors also checked the International Corpus of English, whose subcorpora run to roughly a million words each, to test a popular claim that the words come from Nigerian English via outsourced raters. They found no variety of English in which the focal words stand out, so that account gets no support. The corpus is small, and the authors call the analysis initial.

Architecture and algorithms. Building and varying a model is beyond their resources. As a substitute they compared Llama 2 base with Llama 2 chat, which share architecture and most algorithms but differ in that the chat version gets fine-tuning and RLHF. They computed per-word entropy (how surprised the model is by each word) on the 10,000 human abstracts and on the matching ChatGPT-3.5 abstracts. The base model found human text slightly less surprising (1.616 for human, 1.633 for machine text). The chat model reversed this: 1.051 for human text and 0.886 for machine text, so it was markedly less surprised by the machine-written abstracts. Repeating the test with Llama 3 and 3.1 8-billion-parameter models gave a similar direction, though the Llama 3.1 chat gap was small and the Llama 3 chat gap was nearly flat (1.174 against 1.165). Since the architecture is constant across each pair, the authors take the pattern as evidence against architecture and for fine-tuning or RLHF. They state the limits: the entropy gap may reflect other features of machine text, and Llama only approximates ChatGPT.

### The human-participant study

To test the RLHF idea directly, the authors ran an online study. They used ChatGPT-3.5 to rewrite short (70 to 100 word) 2020 abstracts two ways: once instructed to include four of the focal words (regenerated until at least three appeared), and once told to avoid a blocklist of the 21 focal words plus 21 more found by the same method. That produced 200 pairs. More than half of the focal-word abstracts used delve in the first sentence, so the authors built 30 critical pairs, 15 delve-initial and 15 others, matched on length, and added 30 distractor pairs where both abstracts came from the no-focal-word prompt.

They recruited 201 Prolific participants located in India (140 men, 61 women, mean age 31.3), as a stand-in for the outsourced raters that RLHF often uses, and paid about $15 an hour. Each person judged 20 pairs, picking the abstract they preferred, with attention and language checks included. After exclusions for failed checks, speeding and incomplete sessions, 1,822 ratings remained (1,215 distractor, 607 critical, about 20 per critical item). The authors say the exclusion rate surprised them.

Results: across all critical items together, participants leaned slightly toward the no-focal-word abstract, but the difference from the distractor baseline was not significant (p = 0.174). After splitting by item type, delve-initial pairs differed significantly from the distractors (p = 0.023), with participants preferring the abstract without focal words. For the other critical pairs there was a slight, nonsignificant lean toward the focal-word abstract (p = 0.651). The two item types differed from each other (Wald test, p = 0.03). The authors read this as sensitivity to the word delve, probably because their sample skewed young and technical and had absorbed the online conversation about it, and they say a larger follow-up is needed. They also concede that forcing the model to include given words makes some abstracts worse, for instance by inserting a word about exceeding results into a text that had no such claim.

### Conclusions

The authors report no evidence for architecture, algorithm choices or training data as primary causes, while suspecting these factors still matter. RLHF stays a live explanation, backed by the Llama comparison but not by the human study. They speculate that rushed, low-paid raters may use particular words as a quick proxy for quality, so the words get rewarded without anyone judging content. They add that scientific writing probably played a small part in the feedback data, and the habits likely spilled over from rating academic prose generally. They note that most focal words were already rising before ChatGPT's release, which suggests models may be accelerating an existing drift, and that public awareness may feed back into future rating. They also warn about training on model-written text, and call for more transparency about how human feedback is collected.

### Strengths and weaknesses of the evidence

The identification method is the strongest part. It is explicit, reproducible, and uses two independent filters, so a word makes the list only if it both rose in real abstracts and is overproduced by a model. The scale of the PubMed corpus is large and the frequency gaps are big enough that sampling noise is not a worry.

The weaknesses are real, and the authors state most of them. The data are biomedical abstracts, one genre, and the machine text comes from one model family, mostly ChatGPT-3.5 with one spot check on GPT-4o-mini. The exclusion of spikes judged explainable depends on two authors' judgment. The cut at 50 words is arbitrary. The inflected-form approach splits one word into several entries. The Llama comparison is a proxy, and entropy differences cannot be traced to specific words. The human study was underpowered, post hoc in its split into delve-initial and other items, relied on forced insertion of words, and had many exclusions. Its finding is best read as a hint about readers' reaction to one conspicuous word. It is too weak to count as a test of RLHF. The paper does not test decoding settings such as temperature at all; it lists them only as untested factors.

### Implications for writers

For a writer, the paper supports a few practical points. A handful of words became markers of machine text in a short window, and readers noticed. The marker effect can be strong enough that an abstract opening with delve was disfavored by raters, even though raters did not penalize the other focal words. The words are not wrong in themselves, since they have older human uses, and the paper's own examples show they were typically attached to claims that did not need them. Finally, because frequency lists change as models are retrained and as developers react, any word list is a snapshot, which the GPT-4o-mini results illustrate.

## What the skill applies

Section 10, item 8 of SKILL.md (overused vocabulary) takes its core list from the focal words. Delve, underscore, showcase, intricate, realm, garner, surpass, boast and groundbreaking all appear there in base form, and the paper's evidence is what justifies treating them as machine markers. The skill lists base forms rather than the paper's inflected tokens because a writer needs to catch delving and delved as well as delve. Other words in that item (for example pivotal, crucial, comprehensive, robust, tapestry) are not on the focal list and come from other references, so this paper supports only part of the list.

The paper's choice to rely on measured frequency rather than impression shaped how the skill phrases that item: it names words that rose sharply in both human-published text and model output, and it limits itself to a modest set rather than a long list of everything that sounds formal.

The skill's instruction in item 8 to keep one of these words only when it is the most precise available, which is rare, follows from two things in the paper. The words have ordinary meanings (surpass and emphasize do describe real claims), and the authors' own observation that inserting a focal word into an abstract that makes no corresponding claim yields a worse abstract. The skill therefore tests fit to the claim, and it does not forbid the word outright.

The opening paragraph of the skill, which says readers now recognize default machine prose and trust it less, draws support from the delve-initial result. Raters disfavored abstracts whose first sentence used delve, in a sample the authors believe was tuned in to the discourse around AI wording. The result is small and exploratory, so the skill uses it only as one motivation among several and does not cite a size of effect.

Section 1 (open with the answer) and the stock-opener entry in item 6 are consistent with the finding that the first-sentence position is where readers reacted most, though the paper tested only the delve opener and the skill's rule rests mostly on other references.

The decision to keep word lists only in the revision pass (How to use this skill, step 2) is not a product of this paper; it comes from the priming reference. The paper's contribution is to give the list content.

## What the skill omits

The skill omits the causal account. Whether RLHF, rater incentives, or fine-tuning produce the words matters to model developers, but a writing agent cannot act on it, and the paper itself leaves the question open. The skill also omits the Llama entropy results, the ICE analysis of English varieties and the speculation about outsourced raters in the Global South, none of which bears on how to write a sentence. The raters' nationality claim in particular is a hypothesis the paper tested only weakly, and repeating it in agent instructions would add an unsupported social inference.

The skill does not reproduce the full 21-word list of inflected forms, the opm figures or the percentage increases. Percent increases from tiny baselines (the delves figure is a rise from 0.21 to 14.38 opm) are the kind of number the skill's own section 4 warns about, so the skill gives no such numbers. The skill also leaves out comprehending, advancements, aligns and emphasizing. Advancements, aligns and emphasizing are ordinary words with high baseline use (advancements started above 12 opm in 2020) and banning them would remove common vocabulary for modest gain; comprehending is rare in general writing and unlikely to be reached for by an agent.

The method for identifying focal words is not carried into the skill, since the skill is a style guide and not a detection tool. The paper's suggestion that the method be repeated for other genres and models is a research agenda, and the skill instead relies on the broader and different measures in the other references.

## Conflicts with other references

The paper does not contradict the other sources, but it uses a different measure from kobak-excess-vocabulary.md and liang-ai-modified-peer-reviews.md. Juzek and Ward intersect a human-corpus spike with a model-overuse test on PubMed abstracts; Kobak and colleagues estimate excess word frequency against a projected baseline, and Liang and colleagues estimate the share of text modified by a model. The word lists overlap (delve, underscore, intricate, showcase) but the numbers are not comparable, and the skill does not mix them. Where a popular source such as wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md lists words that do not appear in the measured lists, the skill's item 8 keeps only words with at least some measured support or a clear reason from another reference.

There is mild tension with semantic-gravity-wells.md and with anthropic-prompting-docs.md. Those favor positive instruction and warn that naming a banned word primes it. This paper offers the vocabulary that tempts a ban list. The skill resolves the tension by confining the list to the post-draft revision pass. A second, smaller tension concerns the human-study result: raters disliked only the delve opener and showed a slight, nonsignificant preference for abstracts containing the other focal words. That sits uneasily with a blanket ban on all of them, and the skill handles it through the keep-if-most-precise exception in item 8.

No conflict arises with the style manuals (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md); they address usage and mechanics, and none of them speaks to model-favored vocabulary. The dash and not-X-but-Y bans have no basis in this paper, which does not study punctuation or rhetorical frames.
