# Semantic Gravity Wells: Why Negative Constraints Backfire

- **Source:** Rana, Shailesh (independent researcher). Semantic Gravity Wells: Why Negative Constraints Backfire. arXiv preprint 2601.08070, version 1, submitted 12 January 2026. Single author as listed on the arXiv abstract page.
- **Type:** preprint (not peer-reviewed)
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08070 (full text at https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08070v1). The author links a public code repository with prompts and raw outputs.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-04
- **Used by:** chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose and framing

The paper asks why a language model so often breaks a negative instruction that forbids a specific word, and it answers with a mechanism rather than a list of failure examples. Its central idea is a contest between two forces. Semantic pressure is the pull toward whatever word the model would naturally produce in context. Constraint pressure is the push away from that word created by the instruction. The paper argues that semantic pressure usually wins, that the outcome is predictable from how strong the natural pull is, and that naming the forbidden word inside the instruction can make the failure more likely by planting the word in the prompt.

The author, an independent researcher with no stated institutional affiliation, studies a single open-weight model so that the internals can be inspected. The work is a preprint. It has not been through peer review, and the paper itself reports no journal or conference venue.

### Model, data and conditions

The model is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, a 28-layer transformer, run on one A100 GPU with 80 GB of memory. The dataset has 2,500 prompts, 500 in each of five categories: idioms, factual questions, common sense, creative completions, and out-of-distribution prompts (surreal or counterfactual scenarios). Every prompt has one target word that is the expected one-word answer. The categories were built differently. Idiom prompts came from a public idioms dataset on Hugging Face, filtered for a unique one-word completion and for familiarity (a model-assigned familiarity score of at least 7). Factual prompts were generated from Wikidata queries for relations with a single value (capitals, currencies, chemical elements) and turned into sentences with templates. Common-sense prompts were generated in batches by GPT-5.2 across ten relation types (such as what a thing is used for, made of or has as a property). Creative and out-of-distribution prompts were also batch-generated by GPT-5.2, with targets chosen to span the full range of baseline probabilities. Every prompt was then scored by GPT-5.2 on four criteria: a single clearly dominant answer, low ambiguity, no leak of the target into the prompt, and naturalness. The paper says it applied a baseline-probability gate of at least 0.20 and balanced the bins to cover the pressure range.

There are two conditions. In the baseline, the prompt tells the model to answer with one word and then gives the question. In the negative-instruction condition, the prompt adds a sentence between those two parts telling the model not to use the target word, with the word written out in quotation marks. The instruction wording is the only variant tested; the paper has no condition that rephrases the constraint, omits the word, or mentions the word without negating it. For the negative condition the author drew 16 stochastic samples per prompt (temperature 1.0, top-p 0.9), for 40,000 generations. A violation is a case-insensitive match of the target at word boundaries, with trailing punctuation accepted, so detection is mechanical and does not depend on a judge. The seed was 42 and the paper says all code, prompts and raw outputs are public.

### The semantic pressure measure

Semantic pressure, written P0, is the total probability the model assigns, under the baseline prompt, to any valid surface form of the target (capitalized, spaced or punctuated variants included). The appendix says each variant is tokenized and scored with a teacher-forced forward pass, and the probabilities of all valid token sequences are summed. It is a property of the question and the model, measured before any constraint is added. The paper stresses that P0 is not itself the violation probability. It is the inclination the constraint has to overcome. The paper also defines P1, the same probability under the negative instruction, and suppression as the drop from P0 to P1. A prompt with P0 near 1 is one where the model is almost certain of the forbidden word. A prompt with P0 near 0 is one where the model rarely reaches for it.

### Behavioral result: violation rises with pressure

Prompts were grouped into five bins by P0. The violation rate was 9.1 percent in the lowest bin (P0 under 0.2, 1,193 prompts) and between 20.1 and 20.8 percent in each of the three middle bins (162, 103 and 126 prompts). It was 46.3 percent in the top bin (P0 of 0.8 or above, 916 prompts; confidence interval 43.5 to 49.1 percent). The middle bins have wide intervals of roughly 5 to 6 points on each side, so the three cannot be told apart. So the top bin has more than five times the violation rate of the bottom bin, and it holds 916 of the 2,500 prompts. Weighting the bin rates by prompt count gives an overall violation rate of about 24.5 percent, and the top bin supplies roughly two thirds of all violations. A logistic fit of violation on P0 has a slope of 2.27 (interval 2.21 to 2.33) and an intercept of negative 2.40, and the author says it accounts for 78 percent of the variance. The paper does not say what units that R-squared is computed over; the figure shows binned points with an isotonic fit alongside the logistic curve. The author reads the narrow slope interval as showing that pressure causes violations, which overreaches, since pressure was measured and not manipulated.

Three points about this evidence deserve care. First, the middle three bins are flat at about 20 percent, so the relationship is closer to a step at the high end than to a smooth ramp. Second, the fitted curve implies roughly 41 percent violation at a P0 of 0.9 (my arithmetic from the reported coefficients), below the 46 percent observed in the top bin, so the fit understates the region that matters most. The paper's text and figure caption give 9 percent at P0 of 0.1 and more than 46 percent at 0.9, which match the observed bins and not the fitted curve at 0.9. Third, the paper reports no per-category results, no prompt-level prediction accuracy, and no breakdown of how the five categories fall across the pressure bins, so it is unclear whether pressure is doing the work or the prompt type is.

### Mechanistic findings

The mechanistic section has four parts.

Suppression exists but is lopsided. Outcomes are labeled per prompt: 1,996 prompts count as successes and 504 as failures, a failure share of 20.2 percent of the 2,500 prompts. The paper does not say how a prompt with 16 samples gets a single label. That 20.2 percent does not match the roughly 24.5 percent overall sample-level violation rate implied by the bins, so the labeling rule must differ from simple majority or any-violation counts, and the reader cannot reconstruct it. At the decision step (the position just before the target would be emitted), the negative instruction lowered the target's probability by 22.8 points on average for successes (interval 21.1 to 24.5) and by 5.2 points for failures (4.1 to 6.3). The ratio is about 4.4 to 1. Because suppression is positive even in failures, the author rejects the idea that the model ignores the instruction. It obeys partly, and not enough.

Attention differs only slightly. Among high-pressure prompts, violating runs attended marginally more to the mention of the target inside the instruction and marginally less to the negation words. The paper defines four measures: the share of attention going to instruction tokens rather than question tokens, attention within the instruction to the negation words, attention within the instruction to the target mention, and a priming index equal to the second minus the first of the last two. At high pressure, failures versus successes were 0.205 against 0.200 on target-mention attention, 0.141 against 0.142 on negation attention, and 0.282 against 0.272 on instruction attention overall. The gaps are in the third decimal place, and the paper reports no interval or test for them. In the pressure-bin figure the target mention gets more attention than the negation in both outcomes, so a positive priming index is the norm and not a mark of failure. The author reads this as the mention acting as a cue, and adds that attention shows routing, not proof that the information was used. I would treat these differences as too small to carry weight on their own.

The logit lens, which reads the model's predicted word probabilities from intermediate layers, shows the target is negligible through about layer 20 and then diverges sharply across layers 21 to 27. The paper describes three regimes: layers 0 to 20, where the target probability is below one in ten thousand in every condition; layers 21 to 27, where the curves split; and the final layer. By the final layer, violating runs have a target probability of 0.66 under the negative instruction, against 0.71 at baseline, so the instruction barely changed the outcome. For compliant runs the figure falls from about 0.30 to about 0.08.

Component decomposition at layer 27 splits the final push into attention and feed-forward contributions. Attention tends to push against the target. The feed-forward network consistently pushes toward it. In compliant runs the feed-forward push is about 0.10 and attention about negative 0.05. In violating runs the feed-forward push is about 0.39 and attention about negative 0.13, so the feed-forward push is nearly four times larger and swamps the opposing attention signal. The paper points out that attention's suppression is actually stronger in failures (more negative), which makes the failure a case of a larger opposing push winning, and not of a missing suppression signal. This sits a little awkwardly beside the earlier finding that total suppression is weaker in failures; the two are reconciled only by the feed-forward term.

### Causal check by activation patching

On 769 prompts with P0 of 0.8 or higher, the author replaced the residual stream at one layer of the negative-instruction run with the baseline run's activations, one layer at a time, and measured the change in final target probability. Patching at layers 0 to 5 lowered it by 10 to 22 points, layers 6 to 12 by about 21, layers 13 to 17 by 13 to 20, and layers 18 to 22 by 1 to 10. Layer 23 showed an effect of about four ten-thousandths, and layers 24 to 27 raised it by 3 to 7 points. Patching baseline activations into early layers lowers the target, which the author reads as those activations carrying information that supports suppression downstream; this is a strange result for a baseline run that contains no constraint, and the paper does not explain it further. The author concludes that layers 23 to 27 cause the override. That conclusion is plausible but not tight: the crossover effect at layer 23 is essentially zero, each patch replaces one layer's full residual stream and so cannot isolate heads or neurons, and single-layer patches do not show that the layers act together.

### Failure taxonomy

The author sorts the 504 failure prompts into two types. A priming failure (87.5 percent, 441 cases) has a positive priming index, meaning more attention to the target mention than to the negation, or has a negative suppression value, meaning the instruction raised the target's probability. An override failure (12.5 percent, 63 cases) has suppression above 0.1 together with large feed-forward contributions in layers 23 to 27. The two counts sum to all failures, so the paper treats the scheme as exhaustive. Given that the priming index is positive for nearly every high-pressure prompt, successes included, the priming label is very broad, and the 87.5 percent figure says more about how the category was defined than about a discovered split. The paper gives one worked example of each. In the priming example (an idiom whose target is the word book) the target-mention attention was 0.30 against 0.11 for the negation, about three times higher. In the override example (the currency of Brazil) suppression was reported as 0.92, the final target probability as 0.82, and the layer 27 feed-forward contribution as 0.34. A suppression of 0.92 together with a final probability of 0.82 would need a baseline probability above 1, so the example's numbers cannot all refer to the same quantity as defined elsewhere in the paper.

### Recommendations the paper draws

From the priming result, the author recommends not naming the forbidden word when avoiding it matters, and gives three untested alternatives: a constraint on a whole category (do not mention any cities), a positive reframing (use only general terms), and a circumlocution that points to the word without writing it. The author says plainly that a systematic comparison is future work. It also recommends estimating semantic pressure before deployment and adding safeguards for high-pressure cases, filtering outputs after generation for safety-critical uses, and monitoring attention patterns as an early warning. The listed future work is cross-model validation, comparing constraint phrasings, head-level patching and safety-relevant constraints. It labels itself the first complete mechanistic account of instruction-following failure, a claim the evidence does not support, since the study covers one model and one narrow task.

### Limits stated by the author, and others visible in the text

The author lists five limits: one model only (larger models of 70 billion parameters or more, mixture-of-experts and state-space designs, and other training methods may differ); analysis of the first token of multi-token targets only; short controlled one-word tasks rather than long or ambiguous outputs; attention treated as routing without knockout experiments; and whole-layer patching. Reading the full text, I add several more. The caption for the bin table says 40,000 samples while its prompt counts add to 2,500, which is a labeling slip, since the 40,000 samples were aggregated into prompt-level bins. The stated gate of at least 0.20 contradicts the 1,193 prompts below 0.2, and the full text does not resolve it. The paper never says how a prompt's 16 samples become one success or failure label. There is no control that mentions the word without negating it, so the study cannot separate priming by the word's presence from a failure to process the negation. The summary language is strong for the evidence: the conclusion calls the account the first complete mechanistic one, describes the failure as understood, and the main text calls the logistic fit extraordinary.

### Related work

The log-probability study of ironic rebound (Mann and colleagues, arXiv 2511.12381, November 2025) is a useful companion. It borrows the psychology idea that being told not to think of something makes it more accessible, and it measures the effect in bits of surprisal across several models. It finds that rebound grows with longer or more semantically loaded distractor text between the negation and the output, that repetition helps suppression, that model family matters more than size, and that a small set of middle-layer attention heads boosts forbidden tokens. That study agrees in direction with this paper (negation can raise the probability of the thing negated) but uses different models, measures and prompts, so the numbers do not transfer between them. I read only the abstract page of that study.

### What this means for writers and prompt authors

For the skill's purposes the transferable claim is narrow. Putting a specific word into an instruction that forbids it can raise the chance the model produces it, and the risk is greatest when the word is the natural completion. What the paper measures is a one-word answer to a short question. Whether the same effect holds across a 600-word draft, where the model has many places to choose a word, is not shown. The paper's own wording of the practical lesson is to avoid explicit target naming, and that is the part the skill borrows. The sensible reading is a precaution, not a law: when you want a style, describe it in positive terms, and keep forbidden-word lists out of the context in which the draft is being composed.

## What the skill applies

The paper shaped one structural decision in SKILL.md, in the "How to use this skill" section. Step 1 tells the drafter to aim at the target style while drafting, and step 2 says the lists of patterns to remove live only in the section 9 revision pass. The stated reason, that reading a list of banned words right before writing tends to put those words in your head, is a direct application of the priming finding. Without the paper, the natural design would put the banned-word list at the top, where it would be read first and sit nearest the start of the draft.

The same logic is why sections 1 to 8 are written as positive instructions wherever the rule allows it. Section 2 says to use the plain word and gives the preferred word first (use, not utilize), and section 3 says to state claims directly. Section 3's wording "Say what is true rather than first describing what isn't" echoes the positive-framing lesson, though that rule's main justification is reader efficiency. Where a rule has to name the thing to avoid, it names it once, in the pair or the revision list, and places the preferred form beside it.

The paper also supports putting the revision pass after drafting. Section 9 asks the model to read a finished draft against the list and rewrite hits. At that point the model is editing existing text, not choosing the next word of a fresh answer, so the pressure pattern the paper describes (a natural completion beating an instruction at the moment of generation) matters less. The paper's recommendation of post-generation filtering for high-stakes cases is the same architecture, with the skill's revision pass standing in for a filter.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out the mechanistic detail: the logit lens, component decomposition, attention fractions and patching results. They explain why the effect occurs inside one 7-billion-parameter model, and an agent writing prose cannot act on them. The skill also leaves out the failure taxonomy and the practitioner advice on attention monitoring and pressure estimation, none of which an agent writing prose can use. It leaves out the pressure measure itself. A writer cannot compute baseline word probabilities for a deployed model, and the paper's suggestion to estimate pressure before deployment is a task for the people building the system, not the one drafting.

It does not adopt the paper's circumlocution advice, because the paper never tested it. The skill does not cite specific rates (9 percent, 46 percent, 87.5 percent) anywhere in its rules, because those rates describe one model on one-word answers and would give false precision if attached to long-form prose.

The skill also does not claim that its two-stage design is proven to work. The paper supports a caution, not a measured benefit for revision-pass lists, and the skill's wording ("tends to") keeps that caution modest.

Finally, the skill ignores the paper's broad claims of a complete mechanistic account. Given a single model, a single token position and an observational design, that claim carries no weight in the rules.

## Conflicts with other references

The closest relation is with anthropic-prompting-docs.md. The Anthropic guidance favors telling the model what to do rather than what to avoid, which agrees with this paper's direction, so there is no conflict there. The paper supplies a mechanism for a recommendation the docs give as practical advice, though the paper's evidence is thinner than the docs' broad framing, and the two should not be treated as independent confirmation.

There is a real tension with the skill's own use of word lists, and with the sources behind those lists: kobak-excess-vocabulary.md, juzek-ward-lexical-overrepresentation.md, liang-ai-modified-peer-reviews.md and wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md. Those files motivate a list of overused words and patterns to remove. This paper suggests that putting such a list in front of a model can prime the listed words. The skill resolves it by quarantining the lists in the section 9 revision pass and not exposing them during drafting. That resolution is a judgment call. The paper tests naming inside the same short prompt as the question, and the skill's revision pass is a separate step after the draft exists, so the paper's setup does not map cleanly onto it. If later testing shows the revision pass itself reintroduces listed words, the lists would need to move into a separate tool or filter.

A smaller tension involves the em dash and "not X, but Y" bans, which the user chose explicitly. Those bans are written as rewrite instructions in section 9, not as drafting-time prohibitions, which fits the paper's caution. No conflict arises with the style manuals (NYT, AP, Chicago), which this paper does not address.

## Review notes

On 2026-11-04 I read the full text of arXiv 2601.08070v1 from a local text copy of the paper, covering the abstract, all eight numbered sections, the appendix (dataset construction, pressure computation and extended tables) and the reference list. An earlier version of this file was written from a fetched summary of the HTML page; this version corrects it against the full text. The figures were available only as extracted axis labels and captions, so figure-only values (such as the attention panels by pressure bin) are described from captions and text. I confirmed the single authorship, the preprint status and the date on the paper's own first page. I did not open the code repository or rerun anything.

Two things the full text settled: the 40,000 versus 2,500 mismatch is a caption slip, because the bins hold prompts. Two things it left open: the 0.20 gate against the 1,193 sub-0.2 prompts, and the rule that turns 16 samples into one success or failure label. The overall violation rate of about 24.5 percent, the failure share of 20.2 percent, the fitted 41 percent at a P0 of 0.9 and the impossibility in the override example are my own arithmetic from the paper's numbers. For the ironic-rebound study (arXiv 2511.12381) I read only the abstract page, so my account of it is brief. My view that the evidence is modest rests on the single model, the one-word task, the missing mention-only control and the observational design. My claim that the skill's revision-pass design is a sound response to the paper is a reading, not something the paper tests.
