# Microsoft Writing Style Guide

- **Source:** Microsoft Corporation, Microsoft Writing Style Guide, published on Microsoft Learn. The guide is a continuously revised website with no edition number; its welcome page shows a 2024 copyright line, and the pages read for this file were last updated between June 2022 and November 2026.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/welcome/ (a local crawl of 980 pages, including the A to Z word list, was read on November 4, 2026)
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

### Purpose, audience, and status

The guide is Microsoft's house manual for anyone who writes about technology for the company, from UI strings and error messages to web pages, documentation, marketing, and white papers. Its welcome page says it is meant to be usable by people whose job title is not writer, and that framing shapes the whole guide. It gives short, firm rules with paired examples (a preferred form set against a form to replace) and seldom explains its reasoning at length. For grammar and punctuation questions it does not settle, it sends the reader to The Chicago Manual of Style. Since August 2024 it has used Merriam-Webster as its dictionary.

The guide is a living website. A What's new page logs dated changes back to 2019, and each page has its own last-updated date. The dates matter for a reference file, because pages written at different times disagree in places. The guide's core voice and word-choice pages mostly date from June 2022 and have not changed since, while the A to Z entries, the punctuation pages, and the procedure pages have been revised through 2026. The most recent changes before this review were dated October 30 and November 2, 2026, and concerned terms such as dialog box, window, and privileges, the prepositions page, and the page on describing interactions with UI.

### The brand voice

The guide opens with a statement of voice that governs the rest. Microsoft describes its voice with three paired traits (warm and relaxed, crisp and clear, and ready to lend a hand) and says the voice is constant while tone shifts with the situation, from serious to light. The brand voice page (last updated October 2022) turns this into three style tips. The first is to get to the point fast: lead with the key point, put the most important thing where it will be seen, and give people just enough to decide. The second is to talk like a person, with short everyday words, contractions, and sentence-style capitalization, but without jargon or acronyms. The third is that simpler is better: break content up, use steps and layers, and cut every word that adds nothing. The same page says short sentences and sentence fragments are easier to scan.

The Top 10 tips page (last updated July 2026) restates the voice as 10 rules. They say to use bigger ideas and fewer words, write the way you speak and read text aloud, and use contractions. They also say to lead with what matters and front-load keywords, be brief, and default to sentence case. The remaining rules say to leave periods and colons off headings and titles, use the serial comma, put no spaces around dashes, and revise weak writing. The last rule means starting statements with a verb, cutting unnecessary uses of `you can`, and avoiding `there is`, `there are`, and `there were`. Several of the examples on this page are marketing copy, and one shrinks a full instruction to contact an account representative into a two-word question and a two-word command. That example shows how far the voice leans toward compression and fragments in promotional text.

### Word choice

The word-choice section is the part of the guide that bears most directly on the skill. Its overview page asks writers to choose words carefully and to use the same word whenever they mean the same thing.

The page on simple words and concise sentences (last updated June 2022) asks for simple words with precise meanings and for cutting words that add no substance, with a caution to use judgment so the result does not sound abrupt. It then tells writers to choose simple verbs without modifiers and, when possible, avoid weak or vague verbs, naming `be`, `have`, `make`, and `do`. Its replacement table pairs `use` with `utilize` and `make use of`, `remove` with `extract` and `eliminate`, and `tell` with `inform`. A second table replaces multiword phrases with single words: `to` for `in order to`, `also` for `in addition`, and `connect` for `establish connectivity`. The page also asks writers to prefer words with one clear meaning (`because` over `since`, which also has a time sense). It asks writers to drop adverbs such as `quite`, `very`, `quickly`, `easily`, and `effectively` unless they matter, to keep one term per concept, and to take care with words that work as both noun and verb. The word-choice checklist adds one more pair, `try` in place of `attempt to`.

Other pages extend the same principles. Writers should not invent words from existing ones, give technical meanings to ordinary words, or turn verbs into nouns and nouns into verbs. The examples include using `affect` in place of `impact` as a verb and treating `ask` as a noun. Technical terms are allowed when they are the clearest option but should be defined in context, kept consistent across products, and replaced by everyday words where possible. The jargon page tells writers to avoid business and marketing jargon, with `leverage` meaning take advantage of as its example. It gives a rough test: a term that appears in general-interest newspapers or magazines may suit some audiences, and one that appears in the technical press is probably fine for a technical audience. The guide asks for US spelling, avoids Latin abbreviations such as `e.g.` and `i.e.`, and avoids foreign phrases.

The contractions page (June 2022) encourages common contractions to create a friendly, informal tone, with three limits. Do not mix a contraction and its spelled-out form in the same UI, never contract a noun with a verb, and avoid awkward forms such as the contractions of `there would` and `it will`. The checklist for word choice also forbids words that give devices and products human traits, on the ground that devices do not think or feel.

### Grammar

The grammar section is short and asks for the simple grammar most people learned as children. Its rules are present tense for most content (the checklist suggests treating `will`, `was`, and verbs ending in -ed as signs that text has left the present tense). It asks for the indicative mood for statements and the imperative for instructions, with the subjunctive avoided. It also asks for active voice whenever possible. The verbs page lists three uses for the passive. They are avoiding text that blames or condescends to the customer, especially in error messages; avoiding an awkward construction; and putting weight on the receiver of the action.

The person page (June 2022) tells writers to address the reader as `you` and to omit `you can` whenever the sentence works without it. It discourages the first-person plural because `we` can sound like a looming corporation. It allows `we recommend` when the alternative is the impersonal `it is recommended`, and it allows `we` where Microsoft must be clear that it is the speaker, as in privacy settings. It also contains a 2022 addition about AI-generated content. Such content should signal that a judgment was made on the reader's behalf and might be wrong, either through past tense and an explicit `for you` or through wording that conveys uncertainty. The nouns and pronouns page covers proper nouns, plurals of abbreviations, and pronouns; it allows singular they and asks writers to use the pronouns real people use.

The remaining grammar pages deal with ambiguity. A word ending in -ing can be a verb, a noun, or an adjective, so a heading built on one can mean two things. More than two prepositional phrases in a chain are hard to read. Modifiers such as only belong next to the word they modify.

### Global communications

This section assumes that much of the audience reads English as a second language and that some content will be machine translated or localized. Its writing-tips page (last updated June 2023) is the clearest single statement of global English in the guide. It asks for short, simple sentences, with lists and tables in place of complex sentences. It asks writers to keep `that` and `who` even when they are optional, because they show sentence structure, and to keep articles, which help both readers and translation software find the nouns. It also asks for avoiding idioms, colloquialisms, and culture-specific references, avoiding stacks of modifiers before a noun, active voice and the indicative mood, and modifiers near what they modify. Finally, it asks writers to link no more than three phrases or clauses with coordinating conjunctions, preferably two.

Its additional tips for machine translation ask for conventional grammar, standard subject-verb-object order, one word per concept, and limited use of sentence fragments, which translate poorly. They also cover past participles used as modifiers. One of the fixes they offer is to add a form of `be` so that the role of the -ed word is clear, which is a case where the guide recommends adding the verb it elsewhere calls weak. Other pages in the section cover dates (spell out the month, since the order of day and month varies), currencies, time zones, and seasons (avoid them, or name the hemisphere). They also cover forms that collect names and addresses, culturally neutral examples and images, and web pages built for slow or costly connections.

### Bias-free communication and accessibility

The bias-free page (April 2024) gives gender-neutral replacements for common terms, such as `chair` or `moderator` for `chairman` and `staff` or `workforce` for `manpower`. It lists ways to write around generic `he` and `she` (second person, plurals, articles, role nouns) and allows singular `they` when a rewrite fails. It asks writers to use a real person's own pronouns, to vary names and settings in examples, and to avoid generalizations about groups even when they are flattering. It also asks them to avoid terms with racial or military connotations, with `master` and `slave` and `hang` among the replaced terms. Writers should focus on people over disabilities, avoid words that imply pity, and mention a disability only when it is relevant. A separate page on militaristic language, added in 2024, supplies replacements for violent terms in security content.

The accessibility pages and the accessibility term collection (June 2025) prefer people-first language by default while accepting identity-first language where a community prefers it, and they list labels to avoid, among them `lame`, `crippled`, and `handicapped`. The writing-for-all-abilities page restates several general rules in accessibility terms. Lead with what matters, keep paragraphs short and sentences simple, aim for one verb per sentence, keep similar items parallel, and write link text that makes sense out of context. Avoid directional words such as `above` and `below` as the only pointer to a location. The alt text page (April 2026) asks for alt text that conveys an image's purpose in at most 150 characters.

### Scannable content, headings, lists, and tables

The guide treats scanning as the normal way people read web and product content. The scannable content page (June 2023) cites unnamed studies for the claim that readers in left-to-right languages scan in an F shape and give most attention to the upper left, though it names no study. It asks writers to lead with what matters, to write short headings, sentences, and paragraphs, to get to the point and then stop, and to keep paragraphs to about three to seven lines.

The headings page (June 2022) asks for headings that are short, specific, and in sentence case, with no closing period, and parallel at each level. It suggests noun phrases for concept headings and offers infinitive phrases such as a to-plus-verb form for task headings, while its example set also includes imperative headings. It warns against two headings in a row with no text between them and says one heading level is usually enough for a page or two.

The lists page (February 2026) sets a range of two to seven items and asks for items of the same structure. It distinguishes bulleted lists (no order) from numbered ones (sequence or priority), adds a format for term-and-definition lists, and sets punctuation rules for list items. The tables page (April 2023) says a table has two or more rows and columns of data and should not be used for a simple list of similar items. It asks for parallel entries in each column and specific column headers, and it forbids leaving cells blank or filling them with a dash. Responsive-content guidance (April 2026) adds that paragraphs should fit on a mobile phone screen and headings on one line.

### Procedures and instructions

The procedure pages begin with a design principle: the best procedure is one the reader does not need, because the UI is clear. When a procedure is needed, the guide asks for numbered steps, one instruction per step (short steps in the same place may be combined), complete sentences, and imperative verbs. A heading or introduction should say what the procedure accomplishes without repeating the heading. Each step should say where the action happens before saying what to do, as in a step that names the tab before the command. The examples introduce procedures with a to-plus-verb phrase that states the goal.

The page on describing interactions with UI (updated November 2, 2026) supplies input-neutral verbs (`open`, `close`, `go to`, `select`, `clear`, `choose`, `enter`, `move`, `turn on`, `turn off`) so instructions work for mouse, touch, keyboard, and voice. It discourages `click`, `swipe`, and `press`. The formatting pages specify bold for UI labels, code style for commands, parameters, file paths in code, and other programming elements, and italic for placeholders and for new terms being defined.

### Developer content

The developer section (mostly June 2022, with the formatting table revised in April 2026) says the brand voice still applies to developers but that writers can assume knowledge of programming basics. It describes the expected sections of reference documentation and asks that descriptions say what an element does without repeating its name, and that parameter descriptions add more than the parameter's name and type. Its guidance on code examples asks for short, reusable, tested, secure examples with an introduction and expected output, and for comments that explain details without restating the obvious. It also warns that comments pulled automatically from source code need review, since developers may omit what customers need or include internal details. The formatting table, revised in April 2026, adds that AI prompts quoted in text go in quotation marks.

### Punctuation

The punctuation section asks for few marks. A sentence with more than a comma or two is a candidate for rewriting, and semicolons are a sign a sentence should be split or turned into a list. Its specific rules include these:

- The serial comma is required in a series of three or more.
- Em dashes are allowed for a break in thought or an aside, set with no spaces, and the 2025 dash page warns against overuse. En dashes mark ranges in tables and UI, and running text uses from and through for ranges, with to for ranges of times.
- The colon page (February 2026) discourages colons that introduce an amplification inside a sentence and suggests a rewrite, a comma, or an em dash in their place. It says the word after a colon in a sentence is lowercase unless it is a proper noun or starts a quotation. In titles and headings the word after a colon is capitalized.
- Exclamation points and questions should be rare; customers come for answers.
- Slashes should not stand in for or.

### Numbers

The numbers page (June 2022) spells out zero through nine and uses numerals for 10 and above. It uses numerals for measurements, times of day, percentages, and anything the reader must type, and it has a rule that one numeral in a category makes every number in that category a numeral. It forbids starting a sentence with a numeral. On percentages the guide contradicts itself across pages of different dates. The 2022 numbers page writes a numeral followed by the word `percent` in its examples. The `percent` entry in the A to Z list (November 2023) and the numbers checklist (April 2025) both call for the % sign with numerals instead. The What's new log records the 2023 and 2025 changes, so the sign is the current rule and the numbers page is out of date.

### The A to Z word list

Most of the 980 pages are single-term entries, many of them about Microsoft product names or UI vocabulary. A smaller set gives general usage rules. Those that bear on the skill are listed here, each by its entry name.

- `can`: the guide suggests deleting it because it states ability where the sentence could state the action. It prefers `might` for possibility and bans `may` because it can read as permission.
- `should vs. must` (June 2025): `should` is limited to optional recommendations and `must` to requirements. The entry asks for `might` when a statement cannot be definite.
- `ensure`: defined as make sure or guarantee.
- `simply`: banned as a way of saying a task is easy.
- `accessible`: the entry warns that what is easy for the writer may not be easy for the reader.
- `enable`, `allow`, and `let`: the guide avoids them when they describe what Microsoft makes possible, in favor of wording centered on what the customer does.
- `if vs. whether`: rules out `whether or not`.
- `that vs. which`: matches the newspaper rule and adds a global tip to keep `that` even where it could be dropped.
- `less vs. fewer` and `more than vs. over`.
- `once` and `while`: each limited to its time sense.
- `impact`: allowed only as a noun.
- `leverage`: banned as a verb.
- `initiate`: banned in the sense of starting a program.
- `issue`: banned as a verb and as a synonym for problem.
- `please` and `sorry`: limited to cases where the customer is inconvenienced or the product is at fault.
- `we`: discouraged in general.

The AI and bot term collection (June 2022) asks writers to talk about what the customer can do with the technology and to avoid discussing the technology itself where possible. The prompt entry (2024) gives terminology for AI prompting in product UI.

### Chatbots

A short 2022 section on chatbots and virtual agents asks bots to identify themselves as bots, to state what they can and cannot do, and to keep prompts short (it suggests checking reading grade level). Bots should confirm the user's intent before acting where a misunderstanding could cause harm. They should close a resolved conversation with a wrap-up that asks whether the user needs anything else. The section frames the bot as working for the customer.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The guide is a corporate house style. Its authority comes from Microsoft's scale and long practice in technical writing, and its rules rest on accumulated editorial judgment. It cites almost no published evidence. The F-pattern claim refers to unnamed studies, and the chatbot section mentions unnamed research on whether users know they are talking to a bot. The global-English pages point to two books on international English (by John R. Kohl and Edmond H. Weiss) without summarizing their evidence. Its strongest material is practical. The paired replacement tables, the global-English rules, and the procedure conventions are specific enough to apply mechanically, and they agree with the other technical guides and with plain-language practice generally.

Two features limit how far the guide generalizes. First, its voice is a brand voice, tuned to sell and to make products feel friendly, so some rules (contractions everywhere, fragments, optimism, a fun tone now and then) serve marketing and UI text more than reports or analysis. Second, pages revised at different times sometimes disagree. The percent rule is one example. The weak-verbs rule is another: its call to avoid be and have fits poorly with the guide's own example sentences, which use both verbs freely, and with its machine-translation advice to add a form of be. A reader should check page dates before treating any single rule as current house style.

## What the skill applies

The guide is a secondary source for the skill. Before version 2.0.0 it supplied part of section 9; those rules moved to the technical-writing skill, and that skill's copy of this file maps them. In writing-style the guide reinforces rules in sections 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 8 that rest mainly on the news manuals and the other references in this folder.

**Section 1: Open with the answer.** The guide's repeated instruction to get to the point and lead with what matters most (brand voice page, Top 10 tips, scannable content, writing for all abilities) agrees with the skill's first section. The skill's third bullet says readers decide in the first lines whether to continue. That matches the guide's claim that content on the first screen is the most likely to be read and that many readers go no further without a reason.

**Section 2: first bullet (the plain word).** Two of the skill's pairs match the guide's tables directly: `use` in place of `utilize` and `try` in place of `attempt`. The guide's `if vs. whether` entry supports the skill's ban on `whether or not`. Its `initiate` entry supports `start` in place of `initiate`, though the guide's entry is narrower (it concerns starting programs). The skill's format, giving the preferred word first and the avoided word second, is the same format the guide uses throughout its replacement tables.

**Section 2: use words in their exact sense.** The guide's `impact` entry (a noun only; `affect` for the verb) supports the skill's `affect` rule, though the guide is looser than the skill, as the omissions section explains.

**Section 2: put the person who acts in the subject.** The `enable`, `allow`, and `let` entries prefer wording centered on what the customer does over wording about what Microsoft makes possible, which supports the bullet and revision item 12. ibm-style-guide.md records the IBM guide as the bullet's main source.

**Section 2: prefer verbs to nouns built from verbs.** The guide's pair of `connect` for `establish connectivity`, and its rule against turning verbs into nouns, push in the same direction. The skill's justification (noun-heavy prose hides the actor) is drawn from other sources.

**Section 2: connecting words.** The simple-words page prefers `because` over `since`, and the `once` and `while` entries limit each word to its time sense. All three agree with the bullet that gives connecting words one job each.

**Section 2: one name throughout; intensifiers; jargon; abbreviations.** The guide's one-term-one-concept rule, which it gives for clarity and again for machine translation, supports the rule to call a thing by one name. Its list of adverbs to drop (`quite`, `very`, and others) supports the intensifier rule. Its jargon page, including the `leverage` example, supports the jargon rule and the presence of `leverage` in item 8 of the revision pass. Its acronym rules (spell out on first use, do not introduce an acronym used only once) are close to the skill's abbreviation bullet, which also asks writers not to coin an abbreviation to save a few words.

**Section 2: Latin abbreviations and slashes.** The guide avoids `e.g.` and `i.e.`, and its punctuation pages say a slash should not stand in for `or`. Both points agree with the skill's bullets on Latin abbreviations and slashes and with revision item 13.

**Section 3: cut empty openers.** The Top 10 tip on avoiding `there is`, `there are`, and `there were` is the clearest technical-guide support for the skill's example of rewriting a there-are opener.

**Section 3: trim padding.** The guide's replacement of `in order to` matches the skill's example.

**Section 3: opening phrases, articles, and that.** The grammar pages place modifiers such as `only` next to the word they modify, which agrees with the clause on `only` in the bullet on opening phrases. The global-English tips keep `that` and `who` even when they are optional and keep articles for readers and translation software. Both rules support the bullet on keeping articles and the conjunction `that`. Before version 2.0.0 these two rules were in section 9's bullet on readers whose first language is not English.

**Section 3: keep a series parallel.** The guide asks for parallel structure in lists, headings, table columns, and comparisons, which supports the skill's parallelism rule and its section 6 list rules.

**Section 3: active voice.** The guide asks for active voice whenever possible and names passive uses. The skill keeps its own justification and exceptions (see the conflicts section).

**Section 5: People and fairness.** The guide's gender-neutral replacement table supports the skill's examples of `chair` and `staff`, and its handling of generic pronouns (rewrite first, then singular `they`) matches the skill's fourth bullet. Its instruction to mention a disability only when relevant supports, for disability, the skill's broader first bullet. Its people-first default supports the second bullet. Its list of offensive labels, which includes `lame` and `crippled`, gives partial support to the skill's rule against disability metaphors. The guide lists these as labels for people and does not discuss figurative uses.

**Section 6: Structure and format.** The guide's list rules (introduce the list, keep items the same structure) support the skill's list bullets. Its range of two to seven items is a little tighter than the skill's two to about nine. Its table rule (do not use a table for a simple list of similar items) is consistent with the skill's rule that tables compare items on shared attributes. Its rules of sentence case and no closing period for headings support the header bullet. Its remark that one heading level is enough for a page or two is consistent with using headers only in documents long enough to need them. Its advice to get to the point and then stop, and its final-review advice to cut anything the reader would not miss, support the rule to stop when the content is done.

**Section 8: Mechanics.** The guide's `that vs. which` entry and its `less vs. fewer` entry match the skill's rules. Its numbers rule agrees with the skill on figures for 10 and above and for measurements and percentages, and both forbid starting a sentence with a figure. Its global-communications advice to spell out the month, because the order of day and month varies, gives the same reason as section 8's date rule. It requires the serial comma, which the skill now uses by the owner's choice.

**Section 2: is, are, and has, and revision item 12.** The guide's relation to these rules is a tension, treated in the conflicts section.

**Former section 9.** The earlier entries mapped the guide to rules that now live in the technical-writing skill. They covered steps, `you` and `we`, present tense, modal verbs, describing steps plainly, software that thinks or wants, code formatting, link text, task and concept headings, idioms for global readers, and code comments. They also covered the words `simple`, `easy`, `quick`, and `just`, which the old revision item 13 removed. Technical-writing's copy of this file covers all of them.

## What the skill omits

**The brand voice as a target register.** Microsoft's voice is built to sell and to reassure, with optimism, occasional fun, and clipped marketing lines. The skill's model is a good newspaper, and its tone rules ban cheerleading and performed enthusiasm. The skill takes the guide's plainness and brevity but leaves out its promotional warmth.

**Fragments for scanning.** The brand voice page endorses fragments, and its marketing examples use them. The skill leaves them out, because its sources rank complete sentences higher for prose and because the guide's own global-English page limits fragments for translation. The skill's revision pass also warns against a repeated colon-and-fragment construction, a habit of machine prose.

**Scanning as the default reading mode.** The guide encourages lists, tables, headings, and run-in headings to break text up, and asks writers to replace complex paragraphs with lists. The skill defaults to paragraphs and treats bullets that should be prose as a defect (revision item 11). The guide writes for web pages and UI that readers skim. The skill writes chat replies and documents that readers usually read through, and the running prose of the news manuals serves those better than a scannable layout.

**Product, UI, and platform rules.** Input-neutral verbs, UI prepositions, bold for UI labels, key names, keyboard shortcuts, the Segoe font, line spacing, and alignment concern Microsoft products or web publishing. So do alt text length, responsive layout, URL formatting, currencies, address forms, and the bulk of the A to Z list. They have no counterpart in agent prose, so the skill leaves them out on scope.

**Chatbot conversation design.** The chatbot section's advice to close a conversation by asking whether the user needs anything else conflicts with the skill's rule to skip offers of further help, which the owner asked for. Its other advice (confirm intent, state limits) concerns bot design more than writing. The skill does not take it up.

**Search-engine optimization.** The guide's keyword and backlink advice serves discoverability on the web and has no place in the skill.

**The AI-content pronoun rule.** The person page's advice that AI-generated content should signal a judgment with past tense and phrases such as `for you` is UI copy guidance, and the skill does not use it. The skill's own rule on uncertainty (section 4) asks writers to hedge the interpretation, which has a related purpose.

**Rules the skill makes stricter.** The guide allows exclamation points sparingly, and the skill allows them only inside quotations. The guide allows `impact` as a noun and uses it as a replacement term in its security vocabulary. The skill's sense rule comes from the news manuals and is narrower.

**Technical rules.** The guide's procedure, person, tense, modal-verb, formatting, link, and code-comment rules, and the question of which section 9 rules had no Microsoft source, now concern the technical-writing skill. Its copy of this file covers them.

## Conflicts with other references

**The weak-verbs rule and the skill's rule for `is`, `are`, and `has` (wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md).** The tension between these two rules is the most delicate one in this file. The simple-words page (June 2022) tells writers to avoid weak or vague verbs and names `be` and `have` among them. The skill's section 2 tells writers to use `is`, `are`, and `has` when they are the accurate verbs. Revision item 12 removes inflated substitutes such as `serves as`, `stands as`, `features`, `offers`, and `boasts`. Both come from the Wikipedia page's account of copula avoidance in machine text, which wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md records. Read side by side, the guide seems to push writers away from the verb the skill asks them to keep.

A close reading of the guide narrows the disagreement. First, the guide's rule is qualified (it says to avoid these verbs whenever you can) and sits beside a call for simple verbs without modifiers. Every replacement in its tables runs from a longer or fancier form to a shorter, plainer one: `use` for `utilize`, `remove` for `extract`, `tell` for `inform`, and `connect` for `establish connectivity`. None replaces `is` with a longer construction. Second, the guide's own examples use `be` and `have` as main verbs throughout, in sentences stating that a document is in a folder, that a product includes apps, and that a page has two extra buttons. Third, its machine-translation tips recommend adding a form of `be` to make a participle's role clear. Fourth, its Top 10 tip on weak writing targets `there is` and `there are` constructions and statements that could start with an action verb. These are empty-opener problems of the kind section 3 already covers. Finally, the jargon page uses the construction the skill flags when it describes jargon as something that `serves as` shorthand. That suggests the guide's editors did not have copula substitution in mind when they wrote the weak-verbs line.

The best reading is that the guide's weak verbs are `be`, `have`, `make`, and `do` used as vague placeholders where a precise action verb exists: `make a change` where `change` would do, `have the ability to` where `can` would do, and `do a search` where `search` would do. That reading agrees with the skill. The skill states its position in two parts: prefer a precise action verb when one exists, and never swap a plain copula for an inflated substitute. The second part is a point the guide does not make. A writer who followed the guide's line literally, avoiding `is` wherever possible, would likely produce the `serves as` and `features` constructions that item 12 removes. Those are the commonest ways to avoid `is` without restructuring the sentence. The skill therefore follows the guide where it pushes toward shorter and more precise verbs. It parts from the guide where a literal reading would push toward longer and vaguer ones. On that reading the section 2 rule gives up nothing the guide asks for, so it stands as written.

**Serial comma (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** The guide requires the serial comma and lists it among its top 10 tips. NYT and AP omit it in a simple series; Chicago requires it. Earlier versions of the skill followed NYT and omitted the comma unless an item contained `and` or ambiguity would result. Version 2.0.0 adopted the serial comma by the owner's choice, so the guide and the skill now agree, against NYT and AP.

**Dashes (freeburg-em-dashes.md and the three news manuals).** The guide uses the em dash, unspaced, as an acceptable alternative to colons, semicolons, and parentheses, warns against overuse, and suggests a dash in place of a colon within a sentence. Its spacing matches Chicago's and differs from NYT's and AP's spaced dashes. The skill bans the em dash outright because the owner asked for that. Freeburg's evidence that human writers also use the mark means the ban rests on that choice and on readers' current association of the dash with machine text. For ranges the guide uses `from` and `through` in text and en dashes in tables. The skill writes ranges with `to` everywhere, which agrees with the guide only for ranges of times.

**Colons (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** The guide lowercases the word after a colon within a sentence unless it is a proper noun or begins a quotation, whether or not a complete sentence follows. NYT, AP, and the 18th edition of Chicago capitalize a complete sentence after a colon, and the skill follows them. The guide also discourages colons that introduce an amplification within a sentence, which the skill does not adopt. The skill lists the colon among the marks that can replace a dash, and section 8 allows it only after a complete clause.

**Contractions (nyt-manual-of-style.md).** The guide encourages contractions in all content as part of its friendly voice. The skill follows the NYT manual's distinction between registers. It uses contractions in conversation and spells them out in formal documents and in text likely to be translated. The skill's rule is stricter for reports and documentation, which the guide would write with contractions.

**Let's.** The Top 10 tips page lists `let's` among the contractions to use. Technical-writing's section 2 tells writers to skip `let's` in technical text, and the writing-style revision pass removes `let's dive in` as a stock opener (item 6). The guide's person page and `we` entry, which discourage the first-person plural, are closer to the skill's position than its contraction tip is. The skill follows the stricter reading.

**Percent (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** The guide's current rule (A to Z entry, 2023; numbers checklist, 2025) is the % sign with numerals, which sides with AP. Its older numbers page writes the word `percent`. NYT and Chicago write `percent` in text, and the skill follows NYT.

**Numbers below 10.** The guide spells out zero through nine, NYT and AP one through nine. The difference is only whether zero is a word, and the skill's rule follows NYT. The guide's category rule, under which one numeral makes every number in its category a numeral, has no counterpart in section 8.

**Active and passive voice (rhodes-active-passive-voice.md, reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md).** The guide prefers active voice without arguing the point at length and lists three uses for the passive, one of which (avoiding blame in error messages) is a tone judgment the skill does not make. Rhodes finds no comprehension difference by voice, and Reinhart finds that instruction-tuned models use agentless passives at about half the human rate, so a blanket active-voice rule targets a habit models do not have. The skill keeps the default and justifies it by accountability (the active names who acted), with passives allowed when the actor is unknown or beside the point. The guide's preference supports the default, but its reasoning is not the skill's.

**Exclamation points.** The guide allows them sparingly; NYT restricts them, and the skill restricts them to quotations. The skill follows the stricter rule.

**Ban lists and positive instructions (one-token-away-from-collapse.md, taboo-negative-constraints.md, anthropic-prompting-docs.md).** The guide's tables name the word to avoid next to the word to use. The two studies found that bans like these, placed in a model's prompt, are obeyed but make the answer thinner, and the prompting docs favor positive instructions. An earlier version of this entry cited a claim that naming the avoided word primes it; that claim was dropped. The skill keeps the guide's ordering (plain word first) in section 2 and holds its longer lists of words to remove in the revision pass, applied after drafting.

**Fragments and scanning.** The guide's brand voice favors fragments and broken-up text for scanning; its global-English page limits fragments. The skill sides with the global-English page and with the news manuals' preference for complete sentences in prose.
