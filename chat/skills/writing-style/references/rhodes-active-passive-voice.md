## What the source covers

### Purpose and framing

The dissertation asks whether the long-standing advice to avoid the passive voice in scientific writing has any empirical support. Rhodes starts from a gap she saw as a technical writing teacher: style manuals and textbooks had grown steadily more hostile to the passive over the preceding thirty years, yet published scientific articles were full of passives and often read perfectly well. She cites two surveys of the advice literature to show how one-sided it was. One reviewer found that all twelve textbooks he looked at disparaged the passive. Another, an annotated bibliography by Warren, covered 47 sources, and 42 of them (89 percent) said something negative, using words such as vague, wordy, weak, awkward or confusing. In Study 1 she also quotes the then-current Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association, a scientific-writing handbook by Day, and others telling writers to prefer the active voice.

Her position is that the evidence behind this advice is thin and mostly irrelevant to scientific reports. The famous experiments from the 1960s and 1970s used short, isolated, concrete sentences, usually reversible ones (the boy chased the girl against the girl was chased by the boy) with the actor named in a by-phrase. Many of those studies were designed to test Chomsky's transformational grammar as a theory of the mind, so the passive was a tool for probing processing, and nobody was trying to learn how to write a lab report. Rhodes argues that such results cannot be carried over to whole documents until someone tests whole documents. The dissertation has three studies, a long literature review before them, and a general discussion after.

### The literature review

The review (the longest chapter, covering roughly the 1960s to the mid-1990s) sorts voice experiments by what they measured: speed of sentence verification, accuracy of verbatim recall, comprehension, and preference for choosing a voice. The picture she draws is of results that depend on context. Active sentences were verified faster in some early studies, but the advantage shrank or disappeared when the sentence was not reversible, when the passive had no by-phrase, when the passive fit the topic of the surrounding passage, or when the question matched the voice of the sentence. People also choose the passive naturally to keep an inanimate or previously mentioned thing in the subject slot. Her synthesis is that the literature does not support the claim that passives make scientific documents hard to read, and that the one consistent lesson is sensitivity to context. She notes that the best-known pro-active result for connected text, Coleman's work in 1964, changed voice together with nominalization and adjectival forms, so it cannot isolate voice, and she criticizes the sentence selection and scoring of Coleman's follow-up. I read the review for its structure and conclusions and did not audit each cited study.

### Study 1: how scientists use the passive

Study 1 counted verbs in 36 empirical reports, 12 each from psychology, botany and chemistry or physics, drawn at random from three mainstream journals per field, all published since 1993, short (five pages or less), and each reporting a single experiment in the Introduction, Method, Results, Discussion format. She counted verbs in main and subordinate clauses (an extension of an earlier speech pathology study by Riley that counted main clauses only) and coded a verb as passive when the grammatical subject received the action. Participles that act as adjectives were treated as active.

About a third of all verbs (32.9 percent on average) were passive. The share rose with how object-oriented the field is: 24.0 percent in psychology, 34.7 percent in botany and 42.7 percent in chemistry and physics. It also varied by section in a consistent order from lowest to highest: Discussion at 21.1 percent, Introduction at 24.9, Results at 32.3 and Method at 63.6. Methods and Results together averaged 46.1 percent against 22.1 percent for Introductions and Discussions. Rhodes reads this as writers using the passive where the topic is the material or the procedure and switching to the active where they interpret and argue.

A second analysis classified 751 passives from 12 of the articles. Nearly all (740, about 98.5 percent) were both truncated, meaning they name no actor, and non-reversible, meaning the subject and object could not plausibly swap. Only 3 of the 751 kept a by-phrase. The it-was-found-that type of passive, which style guides love to cite as typical, made up 11 of 751 (1.5 percent). This matters for the whole project: the passive sentences that psycholinguists tested are nearly absent from the genre they were supposed to inform.

### Study 2: the central experiments

Study 2 is the study the skill relies on, and it contains two experiments.

**Materials.** Rhodes adapted four published psychology and animal-behavior articles (counterfactual thinking and liability judgments; mood and memory; pied flycatcher fathers; feeding methods and stereotyped behavior in zoo bears) into two versions each. The high-active version used active voice for every verb. The high-passive version used the passive for 42 percent of verbs, a level she chose to sit at the top of the range seen in Study 1. Each version ran about 1,540 to 1,750 words and was matched to a twelfth-grade Flesch-Kincaid level. The passive versions were a little longer (on average 1,596 against 1,568 words in the first experiment, and 1,746 against 1,728 in the second) because auxiliary verbs and some by-phrases had to be added to keep the content identical. She adapted rather than used the originals so that she could control wording and keep the articles coherent and cohesive.

**Participants and design.** The first experiment had 267 introductory psychology students and the second had 268, all earning course credit. Each person read one article in each voice, with article and order counterbalanced across four packets, and read at their own pace while timing themselves from a wall clock. After each article they answered 16 five-option multiple-choice questions, which were always worded in the active voice, so any match effect would favor the active versions. They also rated how comprehensible and how interesting each article was. Because every reader saw both voices, the main test of voice was a within-subjects comparison, supplemented with between-subjects analyses of variance to check for voice-by-article interactions.

**Measures.** The outcomes were reading time in minutes, number of correct answers out of 16, and the ratings. To confirm that the questions tested the article and not general knowledge, she gave the questions to readers who had not seen the articles (77 people for the first pair, 33 for the second). They scored at chance, about 3.1 to 3.3 right out of 16 against 3.2 expected by guessing.

**Results of the first experiment.** Comprehension averaged 8.82 correct for active and 8.61 for passive versions, which is a difference of about one percent of the test and was not significant (p = .20). Reading time averaged 8.35 minutes for active and 8.51 for passive (p = .14), based on 225 people because 42 failed to record both clock times. The gap is about 10 seconds. Once adjusted for the slightly greater length of the passive texts, reading rate was essentially identical, 187.8 words per minute against 188.1. Ratings of comprehensibility were the same for both voices (4.90 on a 0 to 8 scale in each case), and liking for the article did not differ. Order and article did matter: the second article was read more slowly, and the mood and memory article produced better scores than the counterfactual one. Rhodes attributes the article difference to the questions or the content, since readers rated the two as equally comprehensible. No interaction between voice and article appeared.

**Results of the second experiment.** The second experiment replicated the design with new articles, and added a change meant to make voice matter more: each question asked about a fact stated exactly once, in either an active or a passive clause. The results again showed no effect of voice on comprehension (8.44 against 8.21 correct, p = .23) or on reading time (9.21 against 9.33 minutes, p = .28; 251 people supplied usable times). Readers also had to say which article sounded more scientific. Forty percent named one article, 20 percent named the other, and 40 percent saw no difference. Among the 160 who did choose, none gave voice as a reason, and the split between preferring the active and the passive version (46 against 54 percent) was not significant.

### Study 3: weak and strong student reports

Study 3 compared lab reports written by psychology majors in two upper-level laboratory courses in spring 1996. Four instructors each picked reports they judged weak, typical or strong, and Rhodes analyzed the weak and strong ones: 20 reports in the tables (the running text says 19 once, which appears to be a slip). She counted passives, report length, number of references and citations, and APA citation errors, and rated the Introduction and Discussion sections on a 0 to 4 scale for eight pieces of content, organization and sentence cohesion. She did the ratings herself first and then trained a second rater who did not know the groups. Their correlations ranged from .57 to .70 on the content and organization ratings, and were low and non-significant on sentence cohesion (.24 and .35).

Strong reports beat weak ones on every content rating, on organization and cohesion, on length (about 131 against 82 verbs) and on APA errors (1.0 against 2.6 per paper). Passive use did not differ: 29.6 percent in strong reports and 28.0 percent in weak ones, and no section differed significantly. The strong reports used slightly more passives in every comparison. The pattern by section matched Study 1, and the overall 28 percent was close to the 24 percent for published psychology articles. Rhodes is careful here. She says weak writers may misuse the passive in less visible ways, such as clumsy constructions or confusing switches between voices, so the result does not prove that voice never affects student writing. She adds that it does cast doubt on the idea that telling students to use the active voice will in general improve their reports.

### The author's interpretation and recommendations

The general discussion draws four conclusions. Passives are common in scientific writing. They are used mostly where the topic is the thing acted on. They produced no detectable cost in speed or comprehension in two controlled experiments. And they did not distinguish good from poor student reports. She argues that these findings remove any cognitive justification for blanket advice against the passive in scientific reports, and that Methods and Results sections in particular benefit from it because it keeps the material, and not the researchers, as the topic of the sentence.

She then gives seven reasons she thinks the passive acquired its reputation. The word passive is ambiguous, and many people (including some textbook authors, in her examples from Flesch, Gunning, Klare and others) mix up passive voice with weak or stative verbs and with nominalization. The passive is bundled in people's minds with bureaucratic prose, although readability research seldom measured voice. The wordiness charge applies only to full passives, which are rare, because a truncated passive can be as short as its active counterpart (we analyzed the samples against the samples were analyzed). Badly built passives stand out. Voice is an easy, concrete target for teachers and manual editors. Critics overestimate how much the voice research proves. And the passive fell out of fashion when positivist ideas of neutral observation language did.

She also writes a clear limit on her own claim: the finding of no difference should be applied only to passages that are otherwise coherent, and it would be as wrong to advise writers to use the passive as to advise them to avoid it. She cites Hake and Williams (1981) to make a related point, that English teachers preferred nominalized prose that readers processed more slowly, so the advice in circulation and the behavior of the people giving it can diverge.

### Strengths and weaknesses of the evidence

The design is strong on several counts. The samples were large for this kind of work. Voice was varied alone, with content, length and reading level held close. Two experiments with different articles gave the same answer. The second experiment made voice relevant to the question being asked. The questions were verified as passage-dependent. The passive condition was deliberately extreme, and the test items were worded to favor the active voice. Study 1 gives real, measured passive rates and a classification of passive types, which is a helpful empirical base that most writing advice lacks.

The limits are also significant, and Rhodes states most of them herself. The readers were first-year psychology students, not scientists. The texts were short adaptations, with tight cohesion, so they may be easier than real articles. The questions tested literal recall of specifics, which is a narrow definition of comprehension, and the multiple-choice format is indirect. Voice was never varied inside a poorly written text. A null result from hypothesis testing cannot show that no difference exists. She argues the power was high enough that any real difference must be small, and the means do lean toward the active version in every comparison. That consistent small lean is easy to overlook: about one percent in comprehension and about ten seconds of reading time, neither distinguishable from chance in her data. Study 3 is the weakest of the three. It has about 20 reports, instructor-chosen groups, one coder who was not blind for the first pass, and weak agreement on cohesion. Finally, all stimuli were English-language scientific reports from the 1990s, and the passives in them were nearly all truncated and non-reversible. The findings say little about full reversible passives in other genres, and Rhodes acknowledges that some of the older experiments probably were right about those.

### How the source sits against other work (labeled as not from Rhodes)

The long-running style advice she opposes is documented in her own text. Strunk and White's line that the active voice is usually more direct and vigorous, and Flesch's claim that the passive weakens sentences, are both quoted there, as are the APA manual and several scientific-writing handbooks of the period. These are secondhand through Rhodes; I did not open those books. Style manuals today still generally favor the active voice for clarity and directness while allowing the passive when the actor is unknown or unimportant; this comes from my general knowledge and from the other reference files in this folder, not from Rhodes.

On other empirical work, Rhodes herself discusses the sentence-level experiments by Miller, Gough, Slobin, Herriot, Coleman, Charrow and Charrow, and others, summarizing them in two tables. These are her summaries, not my readings. Later psycholinguistic work on how readers handle passive sentences exists, but none of it was read for this file, so it is left out. I did not find a replication of her whole-article design in the time available, so her central result rests on her own two experiments.

## What the skill applies

The skill uses Rhodes as the evidence that keeps its voice rule modest. SKILL.md section 3 says to default to the active voice because it names who acted, and to use the passive when the actor is unknown or beside the point. Rhodes shaped the second half of that rule and the reason given for the first half. Because she found no comprehension or speed penalty for 42 percent passives in coherent reports, the skill does not claim that active sentences are faster to read or easier to understand, and it grounds the default in accountability, which is a claim about content (the reader learns who acted) and does not depend on processing speed. Her finding that scientists place the passive where the actor is irrelevant (procedures and results, with truncated, non-reversible forms) supports the exception clause for an unknown or beside-the-point actor. The same exception covers a methods sentence in a report, where the sample or the procedure is the topic.

A second contribution is in section 2, the line about preferring verbs to nouns built from verbs ("we decided" beats "a decision was made"). Rhodes shows that critics often blame voice for problems that come from nominalization and vague verbs, and that the first major pro-active experiment varied both together. The skill keeps its verb-over-noun rule on its own grounds and treats the passive as a separate question. That is why section 3 states the passive rule as a default with a stated exception, and section 2 states the nominalization rule without mentioning voice.

A third contribution is in section 9. The revision pass lists no voice-related hits. It does not tell the reviser to flip passives to actives mechanically, since Rhodes's data and Reinhart's measurements both show that the passive is neither the problem in expert prose nor in model prose. The only voice-adjacent item is item 3, on trailing commentary clauses, and that comes from other sources.

Last, Rhodes's point that the effects of a stylistic device depend on context supports the framing at the top of SKILL.md, that a rule which would make a particular sentence worse should be broken and that judgment beats the rulebook.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out all the corpus numbers from Study 1 (passive rates by field and section). They describe how scientists write, and the skill governs prose for general readers, so those rates do not give any rule. It also leaves out her recommendation to keep the passive in Methods and Results. That advice is specific to scientific reports, and the skill's scope is chat replies, documents, emails and summaries. A methods-style sentence is covered by the general exception for an unimportant actor.

The skill omits her seven-factor account of the passive's reputation and her historical material, because they explain a belief and give a writer no instruction. It leaves out the Study 3 result for student writing. The sample is small, the groups were chosen by instructors, and Rhodes herself declines to draw firm conclusions from it. It also leaves out the cohesion and thematic arguments from the review (passives that keep the same topic across sentences), although they are a good reason to use a passive. The skill's wording handles that case only implicitly, through the clause about the actor being beside the point. Adding an explicit rule about keeping the topic in subject position would go beyond what this source tested and has no counterpart in the manuals the skill follows.

Finally, the skill does not cite Rhodes for any claim about machine prose, because the dissertation predates language models and says nothing about them.

## Conflicts with other references

The main tension is with the style manuals' general pull toward the active voice and with the skill's own default. Rhodes finds no comprehension or speed difference, while the skill still prefers the active voice. The two fit together because the skill's reason is accountability and the preference is a default, not a ban. The NYT manual, per nyt-manual-of-style.md, has no entry on voice, and ap-stylebook.md likewise has no rule prescribing active voice in running text, so no precedence question arises. The skill follows the manuals' silence by keeping the rule short.

A second tension is with reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md, which finds that instruction-tuned models use agentless passives at about half the human rate. That finding means the active-voice default cannot be defended as a correction of a typical machine habit, and the skill does not defend it that way. Rhodes and Reinhart agree in effect that blanket passive-bashing aims at the wrong target: Rhodes because the passive does not hurt readers of coherent expert prose, Reinhart because models do not overuse it. The two sources measure different things (human comprehension of scientific articles against grammatical frequency in model continuations), so neither contradicts the other. wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md takes no position on voice.

I found no conflict with the sources on dashes, numbers, vocabulary or hedging, since Rhodes does not address them.

## Review notes

I read the full scanned PDF of the dissertation from UW ResearchWorks after running optical character recognition on all 177 pages, because the file has no text layer. I read in full the abstract, introduction, Study 1 (method, results and discussion), Study 2 (both experiments and the general discussion of them), Study 3, and the general discussion including the seven-factor account, applications, and future directions. I also read the literature review's closing synthesis and its two summary tables, and the front matter. I did not read each of the roughly 40 pages of the review that discuss individual studies, so my account of the older experiments is what Rhodes says about them. I did not read the appendices (article citations, the full text of the Study 2 articles, the rating sheet, and sample student reports).

Recognition errors affected several tables, which came out garbled in places, especially the table of means by order in Study 2 and the layout of Study 1's Table 3. I used the figures stated in the running text and the abstract, and cross-checked them against each other where possible (for example, the per-field percentages and the section means agree with the overall figure). I did not report numbers from the garbled tables. The scan itself shows the Study 3 sample as 19 papers in one sentence and 20 elsewhere, and I used 20, which matches the group sizes given in the table.

I could not check any of the secondhand claims about style manuals, Coleman, Hake and Williams, or the older psycholinguistic experiments against their originals. I did not locate a published journal version of the dissertation or a replication of the whole-article experiments. My statement about the Ferreira paper is based on a search that confirmed the title and background knowledge of its general finding. A possible doubt about my own reading: Rhodes says the null effect should hold only for otherwise well-written text, and I have reported that limit, but the skill's wider use (all kinds of prose) is an extension beyond what she tested.
