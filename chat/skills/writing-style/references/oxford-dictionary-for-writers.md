# New Oxford Dictionary for Writers and Editors

- **Source:** Angus Stevenson and Lesley Brown, editors, *New Oxford Dictionary for Writers and Editors*, published as the second part of *The New Oxford Style Manual* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2016), which pairs it with *New Hart's Rules*. Chief editorial consultant: Rosemary Roberts. The dictionary descends from F. Howard Collins's *Authors' and Printers' Dictionary* (1905) and the *Oxford Dictionary for Writers and Editors* (1981; second edition, edited by Robert Ritter, 2000).
- **Type:** style manual (A-to-Z spelling, form, and usage dictionary for writers and editors)
- **Where to find it:** Printed pages 428 to about 861 of the combined manual, followed by appendixes (proofreading marks, a glossary of printing terms, lists of office holders, alphabets, symbols, and units). The preface says the text is also available through the Oxford Dictionaries website under a paid subscription.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

### Purpose and lineage

The dictionary is a desk reference for people who handle text for a living: authors, copy editors, proofreaders, students, journalists, report writers, and website editors. Its preface sets it apart from an ordinary dictionary. It does not try to define the common vocabulary of English. It lists the words and names that send a writer to a reference book because their spelling, capitalization, hyphenation, or punctuation is uncertain, and it gives the settled form. The entries range across ordinary words, foreign words and phrases, proper names, abbreviations, trademarks, titles of works, fictional characters, and rare words.

The book has a long history. It began in 1905 as a printers' handbook, ran through eleven editions under that title, was renamed in 1981, and was expanded into a larger format in 2000. The 2016 text returns to a compact handbook. According to the preface, it was compiled fresh from the Oxford English Corpus (about two and a half billion words of current English) and the Oxford Reading Programme, with spellings aligned to Oxford's current dictionaries. The editors present it as one of three companion books, with New Hart's Rules (the house style guide) and the New Oxford Spelling Dictionary. Its claim to authority is therefore corpus evidence plus a century of editorial practice, not experiment or reader testing.

### How the entries work

The guide to the dictionary, which I read in full, explains a compact notation that holds most of the book's advice.

- **Definitions** are deliberately brief and exist only to identify the word. Common words included only for their form get no definition at all.
- **Inflections** appear only when they are irregular or likely to trip a writer. Four kinds get them: plurals of nouns in `-o`, verbs that double or might double a final consonant (benefit, focus), verbs whose inflections differ between British and American English (label, travel), and nouns with a possible Latinate plural (cactus, stadium).
- **Variants and errors** are graded. The main form is the standard British one. An acceptable alternative follows the word also. An archaic or nonstandard form follows the word not and is treated as an error. Cross-references send the reader from a variant to the main entry, from an American spelling to the British headword, and from an unacceptable form to the correct one with the instruction use.
- **The -ize question** gets a direct statement. Where a verb can end in either `-ize` or `-ise`, the dictionary gives `-ize` as the main form and marks `-ise` as a British alternative. It says either is acceptable depending on house style, that `-ize` has been used in English since the sixteenth century, and that it is not an Americanism even though American English uses it consistently.
- **Compounds** get the longest discussion in the guide. The editors say no rule decides whether a compound is spaced, hyphenated, or closed, and that all three forms of a word like air base occur and none is wrong. What matters is consistency within a text or a set of texts, which is why compound forms are a main focus of the book. They describe two trends: modern English increasingly avoids hyphens in noun compounds, and American English tends to close them up into one word where British English tends to leave them as two (endpoint against `end point`). The dictionary records the usual British form.
- **Capitalization** of titles such as king or president is lower case in ordinary use and capitalized as part of a name, marked by a note at each entry. The guide sends the reader to New Hart's Rules for the full treatment.
- **Abbreviations** such as BBC and NATO appear in capitals without points, which the guide calls the usual British style, while noting that American English more often uses points. An acronym written with only an initial capital (Nato) is described as an acceptable house style.
- **Labels** mark regional usage. Brit. means standard in British English but not in American; US and N. Amer. mean the reverse.
- **Italics** mark foreign words not yet fully absorbed into English. As a borrowed word becomes common it loses its italics, and words whose English form has dropped an accent show the original form in brackets. Titles of complete works are italic and short poems roman.
- **Usage labels** in the abbreviation list include disp. (disputed), derog. (derogatory), offens. (offensive), arch. (archaic), dated, and informal. These labels record the book's usage judgments.

### Kinds of advice the entries carry

I counted the notation markers in the OCR text to get a sense of proportion. The numbers are approximate because the two-column layout and scan errors split some entries. The book contains roughly 1,950 entries marked one word, 1,070 marked two words, and 1,030 marked as hyphenated; about 1,500 not-forms (errors or rejected spellings); about 590 cross-references marked cf. that point to a word easily confused with the headword; about 580 `var.` of cross-references; about 360 entries giving a US spelling in brackets and about 290 marked Brit. also `-ise`; about 240 trademarks; about 140 entries that split a compound's form by part of speech or position; and fewer than 40 entries that give an explicit usage verdict with avoid, disp., prefer, or similar words. The book is overwhelmingly a guide to form. Its usage advice is sparse, terse, and concentrated in a small number of entries, which I list below.

**Spelling and form.** Most entries settle a spelling (accommodate with two c's and two m's, supersede not `-cede`, minuscule, liaise) or a plural (agendas, criteria as the plural of criterion, phenomena, `appendices` for books). Others settle an inflection (benefited or `benefitted`, focused or `focussed`, `travelled` with American traveled). Still others settle a past tense (learned with British `learnt`, spelled with British `spelt`, dreamed or dreamt, proved or proven, and dove as a North American past of dive). The entry for gotten says British English uses it only in ill-gotten while it is common in North America.

**Verbs that never take `-ize`.** About two dozen entries mark a verb as ending in `-ise` in every variety of English because the ending is not the Greek suffix. They include advertise, advise, chastise, comprise, compromise, despise, devise, enterprise, excise, exercise, franchise, improvise, revise, supervise, surmise, surprise, and televise. The `-yse` verbs follow British `-yse` with American `-yze` (`analyse`, US analyze).

**American and British forms.** Several hundred entries pair a British headword with its American form. Most are the regular patterns: `colour` and color, `centre` and center, `defence` and defense, `grey` and gray, `catalogue` and catalog, ageing and aging, `acknowledgement` and acknowledgment, and doubled consonants such as `travelling` against American traveling. Some carry a twist. Dialogue has dialog as an American alternative. `Programme` and disc give way to program and disk in American use and in computing. `Licence` and `practise` are British noun and verb forms beside American license and practice, `fulfil` and `enrol` become fulfill and enroll, and judgment is the legal and North American form of `judgement`. Word-division pairs follow the same pattern. Any more is two words in British use and one in American, and on to is two words in British use with onto as the American and mathematical form. `Whilst` is marked as British with a direction to prefer while. A few entries flag words whose meaning differs between the two varieties. Momentarily means briefly in British use and very soon in North American use. First floor is the floor above the ground in Britain and the ground floor in North America. Homely means simple and comfortable in Britain and unattractive in North America. Billion once meant a million times a million in British use, now marked dated.

**Compounds by part of speech and position.** Two recurring formulas give advice that applies far beyond the listed words. First, many phrasal-verb compounds are written as one word or with a hyphen when used as nouns and as two words when used as verbs. Backup, breakdown, buyout, checkout, cutback, and login are closed nouns. `set-up`, start-up, check-in, `check-up`, cover-up, `cut-off`, `break-up`, and `trade-off` are hyphenated British nouns. In every case the verb is two words (back up, set up, log in). Second, many phrase compounds are written open when they stand after the verb and hyphenated when they stand before a noun. Examples are long term (two words as a noun, hyphenated as an adjective), common sense, well known, well off, ill at ease, middle of the road, and first floor. The well entry states the general principle: well with a participial adjective is usually two words after the verb and hyphenated before the noun, so people are well adjusted but well-adjusted people are. The ill entry says ill takes a hyphen with participial adjectives (ill-advised, ill-defined).

**Prefixes and suffixes.** Prefix entries state a default for the whole family. `re-` usually forms solid compounds except before words beginning with e and sometimes r. `pre-` generally forms solid compounds, and `un-` forms solid compounds except before a capital. `under-` is usually solid except in age categories such as the under-fives, `super-` is solid, and `inter-` is mainly solid. `ex-` in the sense of former is generally hyphenated, `self-` is hyphenated with stated exceptions, and half is usually hyphenated in compounds. `Non-` is hyphenated in most compounds, which is a British convention, and `co-` compounds are hyphenated in the entries I checked (`co-author`, `co-worker`, `co-pilot`, `co-opt`). For `-like`, established compounds are one word, while compounds whose first element ends in l (the OCR is unclear on whether one l or two), new coinages, and long first elements take a hyphen.

**Words for computing and the web.** The 2016 text records forms for the vocabulary an agent uses most. Email (with `e-mail` as an alternative), website, `weblog`, webmaster, online, offline, username, filename, database, and smartphone are one word, and login (with logon as an alternative) is one word as a noun. Web page, data set, data processing, `end user`, and user interface are two words. User-friendly, `e-book`, and e-commerce take hyphens, and pop-up is hyphenated as a noun and adjective. URL has the plural URLs, World Wide Web is three capitalized words with the Web as its short form, and `Internet` takes a capital. Units such as gigabyte and terabyte are defined twice, as a power of ten and, strictly, as a power of two.

**Confusable pairs.** The cf. cross-references are the book's main tool for words that are easily mistaken for each other. Most of them distinguish spellings (desert and dessert, dyeing and dying, lightening and lightning, palate, palette, and pallet). A substantial minority distinguish meanings that writers of ordinary prose mix up. Some are affect (have an effect on) and effect (a result, or to bring about); ensure, insure, and assure; and infer and imply. Others are disinterested (impartial) and uninterested; militate (have an effect) and mitigate (make less severe); and flaunt (display) and flout (disregard a rule). More are continual (frequent) and continuous (unbroken); practicable (able to be done) and practical; discreet and discrete; and principal and principle. Still more are complement and compliment; adverse and averse; elicit and illicit; and eminent, immanent, and imminent. Others are stationary and stationery; lay (place) and lie (be horizontal); forgo (go without) and forego (precede, also accepted as a variant of forgo); biannual and biennial; and historic and historical. The book also splits `enquire` and inquire in British use between a request for information and a formal investigation.

**Disputed senses.** Nine entries carry the disp. label, which records a sense that is common but objected to. Two are alibi in the sense of an excuse and dilemma in the sense of any problem, as against a choice between undesirable alternatives. Three more are enormity in the sense of large size, as against extreme seriousness; fulsome in the sense of generous, as against excessively flattering; and protagonist in the sense of a supporter of a cause. The other entries are hopefully, regretfully, and thankfully as sentence adverbs meaning it is hoped that, it is regrettable that, and fortunately, and refute in the sense of deny, as against disprove. The label is descriptive. It warns the writer that some readers object; it does not say the sense is wrong.

**Explicit verdicts.** The entries that tell the writer what to do are few, and I believe I found nearly all of them by searching for avoid, prefer, use, strictly, traditionally, best, and better:

- Comprise ends in `-ise`, and the construction comprise of is to be avoided.
- Due to in the sense of because of has traditionally been condemned as incorrect, and the entry calls owing to or on account of a better choice.
- Bimonthly, biweekly, and biyearly are to be avoided as ambiguous, with every two months and twice a month offered as the clearer alternatives.
- Deceptively can mean a thing and its opposite (a deceptively simple plan, a deceptively spacious room) and is to be avoided where possible.
- `Inflammable` should give way to flammable for clarity, and unthaw is best avoided because its meaning differs between North American and British use.
- `Whilst` is British, and the entry says to prefer while.
- Cannot is preferred to `can not`, except in constructions such as `can not` only.
- `Alright` and `irregardless` are cross-referred as errors to all right and regardless, and the entry for whence says that, strictly, `from whence` should be avoided.
- PIN is glossed with a note that, strictly, PIN number is redundant, and hoi polloi with a note that, strictly, `the hoi polloi` repeats the article.
- `Per cent` (American percent) takes the % symbol, but the entry says to avoid the symbol in running text.
- `E.g.` takes a comma before it but not after it; `i.e.` is preceded by a comma; `viz.` takes a point, is not italic, and is preceded by a comma.
- Decimate receives a tolerant note: only traditionalists insist on its original sense of killing one in ten.
- Centigrade gives way to Celsius, `cp.` to cf., and USA to US.
- Attendee is cross-referred to attender, a British preference that American usage does not share.

**Inclusive and sensitive language.** The dictionary's longest run of explicit advice concerns words for people. Disabled is the standard adjective and is preferred to crippled, defective, or handicapped, and the entry adds that coinages such as differently abled have not replaced it. Handicapped is to be avoided in British English though still accepted in American English. Dumb in the sense of unable to speak, deaf mute, spastic, and cripple are to be avoided, with alternatives such as speech-impaired, `profoundly deaf`, and person with cerebral palsy. Black is the lower-case adjective for people and is not to be used as a noun. Several terms are marked dated and offensive and directed to modern replacements, and Oriental is not to be used of people, with Asian or a specific nationality in its place. Tribe should give way to community or people in contemporary contexts. Native American is the preferred term in the United States, with names of specific peoples better still. For gender, the book prefers firefighter to fireman and gender-neutral compounds in general. It prefers the human race or humankind to man and `mankind`, author and poet to `authoress` and poetess, comedian for all sexes, and head teacher to headmaster. Given name, `forename`, or `first name` is preferred to `Christian name`, with given name described as better in non-Western contexts. Sex should give way to gender for social or cultural differences. Themself is marked avoid, with themselves, himself, or herself offered in its place, which is the book's only remark bearing on singular they that I found.

**Words the skill names.** Most of the words in the skill's plain-word and exact-sense rules have no usage entry in this dictionary. `Utilize` appears only as a spelling entry with the British alternative `utilise`; nothing prefers use. Literally appears only with its abbreviation. Key appears only as the Caribbean island and in compounds such as key ring. Necessary appears only as a spelling (one c, two s's). Data has a real entry: it is treated as plural in specialized scientific fields and as a singular mass noun in non-scientific use, with `datum` as the singular for a single piece of information. Media is treated as singular or plural, and none as singular or plural depending on emphasis. Comprise has the avoid comprise of note above. I found no entries for `prior to`, impact, unique, massive, transpire, whether, fewer, less, that, which, or need. None discusses idiom in general, though the book defines Briticism (an idiom confined to British English, preferred to Britishism), Gallicism, and Graecism. It defines a participle as a verb form used as an adjective.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The book's strength is breadth and a consistent notation. A writer can check the settled form of thousands of words quickly, and the patterns behind individual entries (noun closed and verb open, compound hyphenated only before a noun, a family default for each prefix) generalize well. Its corpus basis means the forms reflect actual edited usage in the 2010s rather than a single editor's taste.

Its limits for this skill are equally clear. It is British. Its headwords, its prefix conventions (`non-` and `co-` hyphenated, `set-up` and start-up hyphenated) and some preferences (attender over attendee) follow British practice, and American forms appear only as variants. It says little about usage. The disp. label and the rare avoid notes are terse, give no reasons beyond ambiguity or tradition, and cover a few dozen words. It does not address style at the level of the sentence, the paragraph, or the register, and it is silent on most of the inflated and misused words that the skill targets. It reports no tests of comprehension or reader preference. As evidence, then, it is a strong source for the form of individual words and for compound patterns, a weak source for usage judgments, and no source at all for the skill's sentence-level rules.

## What the skill applies

The skill takes word forms, compound patterns, and a short list of usage verdicts from this dictionary. New Hart's Rules, the other half of the same manual, backs several of the same rules, and its own reference file covers that half. Section numbers below are those of SKILL.md version 2.0.0.

**Section 2 (Words).**

- The exact-sense bullet gained three sentences from this source. The refute and dilemma sentences follow the disputed labels on those entries (pages 534 and 753): refute in the sense of deny, and dilemma in the sense of any problem. The skill goes further than the dictionary, which only marks the senses as disputed. The skill treats them as wrong and offers dispute as the word to use instead. The NYT manual's dilemma entry agrees. The comprise sentence follows the comprise entry's note to avoid comprise of (page 513). The dictionary gives no replacement, so the skill's "consists of" comes from the IBM guide and from general usage.
- The bullet on words readers take two ways rests on two entries. Biweekly and bimonthly follow the bimonthly entry, which calls them ambiguous and offers every two months and twice a month (page 476). Momentarily follows that entry's split between the British sense of briefly and the North American sense of very soon (page 678). The bullet's third example, table, does not come from this dictionary; I found no usage note on it. The replacements in a minute and postpone are the skill's own.
- The bullet on calling a thing by one name now adds that the writer should also spell it one way, with email and `e-mail` and `dataset` and data set as examples. That sentence follows the guide to the dictionary's point that compounds have no single correct form, so consistency within a text is what counts (page 431). The skill's preferred form, `dataset`, follows American technical usage. The dictionary itself writes data set.
- The `e.g.` and `i.e.` bullet takes the plain-word half of my proposal: for example and that is in running text, with the abbreviations kept for parentheses and tables. That preference comes mainly from New Hart's Rules and the technical guides. This dictionary contributed only the punctuation of the two abbreviations, which the skill leaves out.

**Section 3 (Sentences).** The padding bullet now lists PIN number among words that repeat a meaning. The PIN entry notes that, strictly, the phrase is redundant (page 724). The NYT manual lists the same phrase as a redundancy. The skill's other examples (revert back, past history, the CLI interface) generalize the point. They are not from this source.

**Section 5 (People and fairness).** Two additions trace here. The labels bullet now says `first name` or given name, not `Christian name`, which follows the `Christian name` and `forename` entries (pages 505 and 572). The IBM guide makes the same substitution. The gender-neutral bullet adds humankind, which follows the man and `mankind` entry (page 662); firefighter was already there from Chicago and matches the dictionary's own entry (page 568).

**Section 8 (Mechanics).**

- The American spelling bullet uses the dictionary's paired British and American forms and its explanation that `-ize` is not an Americanism (pages 430 to 431). The `-yze` form follows the `analyse` entry (page 450), traveled follows the travel entry, and judgment and gray follow their entries. The list of verbs that end in `-ise` in every system (advertise, advise, comprise, compromise, devise, exercise, revise, supervise, surprise) comes from the not `-ize` entries, cut to the common ones. Toward and canceled are the skill's additions; I did not find dictionary entries that state them.
- The compound-modifier bullet (hyphen before a noun, open after a verb) follows the well, ill, and long term entries (pages 848, 615, and 654) and the many entries marked hyphen when attributive. Its example pair, well-known and well known, is the dictionary's own pattern. The clause on adverbs ending in `-ly` comes from New Hart's Rules and the IBM guide, not from this dictionary.
- The phrasal-verb bullet (two words for the verb, one word for the noun) follows the roughly 140 entries that give a compound's noun and verb forms, such as backup (page 466), `set-up` (page 782), and login (page 653). The skill writes the nouns closed (setup) where the dictionary hyphenates them (`set-up`), following American usage.
- The prefix bullet follows the family entries for `re-`, `pre-`, `un-`, `under-`, `super-`, `inter-`, `self-`, and `ex-` (pages 558 to 832), which close most prefixes and hyphenate `self-` and `ex-`. The skill writes nonprofit closed against the dictionary's British `non-` entry (page 693). The cases where a hyphen prevents a misreading (re-create, re-sign) come from New Hart's Rules and the IBM guide.
- The data sentence follows the data entry (page 526), taking its general-use half: a singular mass noun outside scientific writing. The Google guide also treats data as singular. Keeping criteria and phenomena plural follows the criterion and phenomenon entries (pages 521 and 720).
- The cannot, all right, and no one sentence follows those entries (pages 493, 447, and 694). The ban on `noone` is the skill's addition. The sentence on roman type for ad hoc, per se, and vice versa follows those entries, each marked not italic (pages 439, 719, and 840), and the guide's account of borrowed words losing their italics (page 432).

**Section 10 (Revision pass).** Item 13 checks for a hyphen in a phrasal verb (to `set-up`), which enforces the phrasal-verb rule above. Item 14 lists confusable pairs. Eight of its ten pairs come from the dictionary's cf. cross-references: affect and effect, ensure and insure, imply and infer, continual and continuous, discrete and discreet, complement and compliment, principal and principle, and practical and practicable. Comprise appears there from the comprise entry, but the dictionary does not pair it with compose. Fewer and less come from the earlier rule and from Chicago, since this dictionary has no entry for either word.

The skill follows the dictionary's own design note too: it keeps the list of pairs in the revision pass and puts a positive rule in the drafting sections.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out most of the dictionary, as it should. The book is a lookup tool for thousands of individual spellings, capitals, italics, and proper names, and an agent writing chat replies and reports needs the patterns, not the entries. The British main forms are left out as well, because section 8 of the skill sets American spelling.

Of my phase 1 proposals, these were cut or cut down:

- Due to in the sense of because of (proposal 9). The dictionary says this use has traditionally been condemned, and the IBM guide agrees, but the skill keeps only the plain-word swap of because for `due to the fact that`, which comes from Chicago. I can't tell why the narrower rule was dropped; a likely reason is that many current American guides accept due to as a preposition.
- The disputed senses of enormity and fulsome. Only refute and dilemma made it into section 2, probably because those two turn up in agent summaries and the other two rarely do.
- Biannual and biennial, and deceptively. The ambiguity bullet keeps biweekly, bimonthly, and momentarily.
- The comma rules for `e.g.`, `i.e.`, and `viz.` The skill moves the abbreviations out of running text, which makes their punctuation a minor point.
- Five confusable pairs: disinterested and uninterested, militate and mitigate, flaunt and flout, forgo and forego, and lay and lie. The revision item keeps the ten pairs most likely in technical and business prose.
- The full `-ise` list (improvise, televise, chastise, and others) and the British-only forms `whilst`, any more, and on to. The skill keeps the common verbs and says hold American spelling.
- The extension of PIN number to ATM machine. The skill uses its own example, the CLI interface, instead.
- The inclusive-language items on tribe, Oriental, and gender versus sex. The skill keeps its general rule on labels people use for themselves, which covers these cases.
- Media and none as singular or plural. The skill's data sentence covers the common case.

The dictionary also contributes nothing to the skill's sentence, structure, tone, or evidence rules, because it has nothing on those subjects. It has no entries for `prior to`, impact, unique, massive, transpire, whether, fewer, less, that, which, or need. The skill's rules on those words rest on other references.

On idiom, the owner chose a general rule on idiomatic word choice (section 2) over word-level rules. This dictionary does not support that bullet. It has no general rule on idiom and says nothing about need, needed, or necessary beyond spelling.

## Conflicts with other references

**New Hart's Rules (oxford-new-harts-rules.md).** The two halves of the manual agree on every point the skill took: `-ize` spelling, the verbs that keep `-ise`, hyphenation by position, closed or hyphenated nouns from phrasal verbs, and dropping italics from familiar borrowed terms. Hart's is the fuller source on hyphens and prefixes and adds the `-ly` rule. Both books write `e.g.` and `i.e.` with no comma after; the skill avoids the question by keeping the abbreviations out of running text.

**NYT Manual of Style (nyt-manual-of-style.md) and AP Stylebook (ap-stylebook.md).** The NYT manual agrees on dilemma and PIN number. On hopefully, which this dictionary labels disputed, the NYT manual objects mildly and the AP Stylebook accepts the modern sense. The skill states no rule on hopefully, so no conflict appears in the text. The dictionary's British forms (`colour`, `centre`, `programme`, `whilst`) give way to the American spelling that section 8 sets.

**Chicago Manual of Style (chicago-manual-of-style.md).** Chicago's glossary and this dictionary share few words. Chicago rules on impact, literally, transpire, and `utilize`, which this dictionary leaves alone. Where the two meet, as on hopefully, the skill sides with Chicago, which treats the word as an established sentence adverb, and so sets no rule against it. The dictionary's entry marking themself as a word to avoid fits poorly with the singular they that Chicago and AP accept. The skill follows them and says to use the pronouns a person uses. It doesn't mention themself.

**Apple Style Guide (apple-style-guide.md).** Apple tells writers to avoid comprise altogether because readers misuse it. The skill follows this dictionary and the IBM guide instead and keeps comprise in its exact sense, with the whole comprising its parts. The exact sense is standard, and the skill's revision item catches the misuse Apple worries about, so the skill keeps the word and guards against the error.

**IBM Style Guide (ibm-style-guide.md).** IBM agrees on `comprised of`, given name, and due to after a form of be. On prefixes, IBM closes reenter where this dictionary hyphenates `re-` before e. The skill gives neither example, so the difference doesn't surface.

**Google developer style guide (google-developer-style-guide.md) and Red Hat style guide (red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md).** Google's singular data matches the skill. Red Hat's setup and set up and login and log in entries match the phrasal-verb rule and use the American closed nouns over the dictionary's British `set-up`.

## Review notes

What I read: the preface, the guide to the dictionary, and the list of abbreviations in full (printed pages 428 to 434). For the A-to-Z text I ran systematic searches rather than reading every entry. They covered the usage markers (avoid, disp., prefer, use, strictly, traditionally, better, best, ambiguous, offensive), every cf. cross-reference outside proper names, and the compound formulas (one word, two words, hyphen, hyphen when attributive, two words as verb). They also covered the prefix entries, the US and Brit. labels, the not `-ize` entries, computing vocabulary, and each word the skill names. I read the entries those searches returned. The bulk of the entries for proper names, foreign phrases, and scientific and historical terms I did not read.

Doubts. The text is OCR of a two-column layout. My script for separating the columns sometimes split an entry across the column boundary, and accents were lost throughout. Page numbers are printed page numbers taken from the OCR footers and may be off by one. The counts of entry types in the coverage section are approximate. The `-like` entry's condition for a hyphen was garbled. I did not check the citation details (editors, edition) against the title page. I may have missed usage notes worded without the markers I searched for. The claim that the dictionary has no entry for toward rests on a search, not on reading the t entries. For the conflicts section I read the reference files named above, not the books behind them.
