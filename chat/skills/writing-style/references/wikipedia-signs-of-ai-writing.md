# Wikipedia, Signs of AI Writing (WikiProject AI Cleanup field guide)

- **Source:** Wikipedia contributors (maintained by WikiProject AI Cleanup), "Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing," Wikipedia project page (an essay and field guide, not policy), continuously edited. The revision read was the live page as of 4 November 2026.
- **Type:** general web
- **Where to find it:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing (raw wikitext at https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing&action=raw). The page links to subpages on style and markup, and to a WikiProject AI Cleanup guide.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-04
- **Used by:** chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose and framing

The page is a field guide for Wikipedia editors who need to spot text that a chatbot produced and the contributor did not disclose. It collects patterns seen in real article revisions, drafts, talk-page comments and edit summaries, and each pattern comes with dated examples from actual edits. The authors say plainly that the list describes what they have observed and sets no rules. Advice on what to avoid belongs, in their view, in Wikipedia's policies and Manual of Style, so the page declines to be a style guide.

The most important framing point for a writer is the instruction about what to do with the list. The signs are symptoms. The real problems are deeper: invented facts, fabricated citations, unsourced claims and promotional content. The page asks readers not to treat the surface tells as the things to fix, because scrubbing the tells would only make the underlying problems harder to find. For Wikipedia's purposes this is sensible, since the goal is detection. For a writer the same logic cuts the other way: removing a tell from a vague or padded sentence leaves a vague sentence.

The page also limits its own scope. It is built for informational, encyclopedic writing. It says some signs may not carry over outside Wikipedia, and it leaves out the many fiction-specific tells because they matter little there. It also notes that language models learn from human writing, Wikipedia included, so any single sign can appear in human work, and that editorials, blogs and fan fiction contain many of the same habits.

### Caveats about using the signs

The caveats section is the most carefully evidenced part of the page. It warns against relying on automated detectors alone, citing benchmark work showing that detectors beat chance but have meaningful error rates and can be fooled by paraphrasing, markup changes, spacing changes or models they were not trained on. It then warns against trusting one's own judgment. It cites a 2025 study finding that human ability to tell model text from human text was no better than chance, and a 2025 study of German theses in which people recognized model text a little more than half the time and human text a little under two thirds of the time. A third 2025 preprint found that a group of expert annotators who use models heavily identified about 90 percent of model-written text and wrongly flagged about 3 percent of human text, while light users of models did only slightly better than chance. I did not open these studies; I am reporting how the page describes them.

The page adds that human writing is drifting toward model writing, citing studies of spoken podcast content and academic speech and of word choice. It says this convergence is lagging and uneven, and that model output is not drifting toward human writing in return. A section on ineffective indicators lists habits that people often take as proof but that the page rejects: flawless grammar, a mix of casual and formal registers, bland prose, fancy or formal prose in general, transition words taken alone, missing citations, and correct markup. On transition words, the page says only a few are known to be overused and that essay-style human writing and many style guides accept them. This matters for the skill, because the skill bans sentence-opening Additionally and Notably, which the page treats as a weak signal when seen in isolation.

A related section lists signs of human writing. Text dated before ChatGPT's public launch on 30 November 2022 can be treated as human-written. A writer's ability to explain their own choices points to a human. And a set of syntactic habits was found, over 25 years of Wikipedia text, to be more common in human articles than in model articles: simple is and has phrases, plain words where a stiff synonym exists (the page's examples pair wrote with authored, used with utilized, tried with attempted, died with passed away, moved with relocated), superlatives and definitive statements, qualifiers and intensifiers, and isolated wordy constructions such as in order to and the fact that.

### Content signs

The page opens its taxonomy with a theory. Models predict likely continuations, so they regress toward the statistical middle, and later tuning from human feedback pushes output toward whatever is broadly palatable. Specific, unusual facts are statistically rare, so the model swaps them for generic and flattering description. The page's example is a specific invention credit turning into a vague claim about being a titan of industry. The subject gets simultaneously less specific and more exaggerated.

Several content signs follow from this.

- **Undue emphasis on significance and legacy.** Chatbots attach claims that a minor detail reflects, represents or contributes to a broader trend, and they do so for mundane topics such as place-name etymology or population data. The page's watch list includes stands as, is a testament, a pivotal moment, underscores its importance, setting the stage for, evolving landscape, and indelible mark. Biology articles get inflated ecosystem and conservation language even when no conservation work exists.
- **Canned emphasis on notability and media coverage.** Text describes what kinds of outlets covered a subject (trade press, regional media) instead of what the coverage said, often echoing Wikipedia's own notability wording. The page ties this to newer tools released in 2025 or later and says it is narrow and distinct from ordinary press releases.
- **Emphasis on identification by primary or tertiary sources.** Phrasings in which databases, listings or a personal website identify a person as something. The page dates this sign to 2026.
- **Superficial analysis.** A present-participle phrase tacked on the end of a sentence to comment on significance (highlighting, ensuring, reflecting, fostering, contributing to). The page cites Reinhart and colleagues for the participle finding and gives long examples where nearly every sentence ends this way.
- **Vague connection.** Connected with and associated with used in place of a direct statement of a role or event.
- **Vague attribution.** Industry reports, some critics and several sources used when one or two sources exist, plus lists framed as non-exhaustive without support.
- **Outline-like conclusions.** A formulaic challenges-and-future section that begins with a despite clause and ends on a hopeful note.
- **Awards and recognition headings,** and X-and-Y section headings in general.

### Language and grammar signs

The vocabulary section is the one most relevant to this skill. It says many studies show models overusing particular words since 2022, that such words cluster together, and that most of the studies examined scientific abstracts or fiction, though the words also appear in model-built encyclopedias and in AI-generated Wikipedia text. One or two such words in an edit may be chance. Many of them across an edit made after 2022 is one of the strongest tells, according to the page.

The page then does something unusual for a popular list: it dates the vocabulary by model generation. It gives separate word sets for roughly 2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4 era), mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o), mid-2025 to mid-2026 (GPT-5), and mid-2026 onward (a GPT-6 set). It says delve peaked in 2023 and early 2024 and then fell sharply in 2025. The newest set the page reports includes challenge, foster, leverage, multifaceted, prioritize, practical, steady, quietly and several others, and it describes the newest output as using shorter sentences with more commas and periods. Grok is described as favoring pseudo-scientific words such as causal and empirical. The page warns that a word being overused does not mean its synonyms are, and that context matters (a literal underscore is fine).

A hidden note in the page source says a word should be added to the vocabulary box only when at least one reliable, non-pop-science external source corroborates its overuse and editors also see it on Wikipedia. That is a real editorial filter, but it is not a measurement. The page itself never states frequencies or ratios for individual words, and it cites the academic literature only in general terms.

Other language signs:

- **Avoidance of the plain copula.** Serves as, stands as, features, offers and boasts replace is, are and has. The page cites a study that found an over 10 percent drop in is and are in academic writing in 2023, and says the pattern appeared in a test where a model was asked to revise sentences.
- **Negative parallelisms.** Three forms: not only X but Y, not X but Y (including no X, no Y, just Z), and the reversed Y rather than X, which the page calls especially common in recent output. It concedes that human writers use these too, especially in myth-busting listicles, while calling it a stereotypical AI sign.
- **Rule of three.** Models overuse triplets of adjectives or short phrases, often to make a shallow analysis look thorough. The sign is stronger where nobody would bother, as in edit summaries.
- **Leads that treat list titles as proper nouns,** a Wikipedia-specific pattern.

### Style, markup and communication signs

The style list covers title case in headings, heavy boldface in prose, inline-header vertical lists, wrong heading levels, emoji at the start of headings or list items, and curly quotation marks. The markup list covers Markdown syntax left in the text, code fences, leaked internal citation tokens, nonexistent categories and templates. These are largely Wikipedia-specific, but the boldface, list and emoji items match general chatbot formatting.

The collaborative communication section covers text meant for the user that was pasted into the page: offers of further help, invitations to ask for more, assurances like certainly or of course, validation phrases, notes to verify before submitting, and templates the user was meant to fill in. Its watch list includes I hope this helps, Would you like, let me know, is there anything else and here is a. The section on hedging disclaimers traces a chronology from lectures about what is important to note (2023 to 2024), through claims that information is unavailable in the sources (2024 to 2025), to corrective framings and should-not-be-confused statements that the page says became common only from mid-2026. Further sections cover placeholder text and fake URL fields, citation failures (dead links, invalid ISBNs and DOIs, DOIs pointing to unrelated papers, book citations with no page numbers, tracking parameters that name a chatbot), comment-specific signs (fabricated policy shortcuts, clerk-like apologies), and edit-summary templates. A miscellaneous section covers sudden style shifts, differences between models, and a pro-authoritarian bias in some model output.

### Historical indicators

The page moves several well-known signs into a historical section because newer models produce them less. These are promotional travel-guide language (nestled, breathtaking, rich, diverse, renowned, groundbreaking, commitment to), knowledge-cutoff disclaimers, section summaries, refusal boilerplate, abrupt cut-offs, outdated access dates, and elegant variation (a model avoiding repeated words). Two of these bear directly on the skill. The section-summary entry says older models often ended articles with a conclusion heading and ended paragraphs by restating their core idea, and lists in summary, in conclusion and overall as watch phrases. The em-dash entry covers the years 2022 to September 2026. It says models use em dashes more than nonprofessional human writers in the same genre, often with surrounding spaces and in a punched-up, sales-like way. It says OpenAI suppressed the habit starting with GPT-5.1, reports a July 2026 study finding Claude was the only contemporary model using em dashes more than professional writers, and says the very latest models are increasingly built to avoid them entirely. It adds that this suppression has made commas and periods unusually frequent, and that the page's author now treats that as its own sign. The page marks one claim in that passage as needing a citation.

### Strengths and weaknesses of the evidence

The page's strength is breadth, currency and traceability. Every sign is tied to dated real edits, and the authors revise the list as models change, which is why it can say which habits are fading. Its habit of recording that some signs are historical is useful to any writer who wants to avoid a stale list.

Its weakness is that it is a community guide. It reports frequencies in general terms and cites little quantitative evidence for individual words. Where it does cite measurement, it points to the same academic literature this skill draws on directly, so it adds little independent support there. The examples are drawn from Wikipedia contributors who used a model badly, often with a prompt asking for an encyclopedia entry, so the sample is skewed toward encyclopedic prose and toward careless users. The page also admits that detection by style is hard for humans, and its pointer that humans cannot reliably tell model text apart means the list is best read as hypotheses about habits, which is how this skill uses it.

## What the skill applies

The skill uses this page as support for the revision pass in section 10, and as a check on a few rules elsewhere. Because the page is a popular, observation-based list and not a measurement, the skill's reliance on it is limited to items that other, measured sources also back, plus items the page lets the skill call "widely flagged."

**Item 8, overused vocabulary.** The page names many of the skill's words, and a search of the page text confirms which. These appear in its watch lists or vocabulary section: delve, underscore, showcase, intricate, pivotal, meticulous, additionally, crucial, enhance, garner, boasts (the skill's boast), landscape (as an abstract noun), tapestry, robust, foster, vibrant, testament, multifaceted and leverage. Highlight as a verb and align with appear on the page but not in the skill's list. Comprehensive appears on the page only in passing, not as a named tell. These words do not appear anywhere on the page as an overused word: realm, surpass, commendable, innovative, embark, holistic, and navigate. Transformative and seamless appear only inside quoted examples of bad prose, not as listed words; nuanced and journey likewise appear in passing without being flagged. Notably appears in the page's discussion of transitions, where the page calls such openers a weak tell. The skill's list therefore rests on this page for about 19 of its items and on other sources, or on no source in this folder, for the rest. The brief's question about tapestry, robust, foster, embark, vibrant, holistic, testament and figurative navigate or journey has this answer: the page names tapestry, robust, foster, vibrant and testament, names none of embark, holistic, navigate or journey as flagged words, and lists foster (as fostering) in its middle eras and as foster in its newest set. The skill's wording "words machine-written text leans on" is acceptable for the first group. Holistic, realm, commendable, innovative, surpass and the figurative sense of journey rest on the other references, and should not be described as supported by this page. Embark and figurative navigate rested on no reference at all, so the skill dropped them in November 2026; surpass stayed because the Kobak and Juzek and Ward lists both include it. Since the page's own era chart shows the list turning over, the skill is right to say keep one only when it is the most precise word, and not to treat any word as permanently banned.

**Item 6, stock openers and closers.** The page's collaborative-communication watch list supports I hope this helps, let me know, certainly, of course, would you like and is there anything else. It also lists deep dive and more detailed breakdown, which resemble the skill's Let's dive in and Here's a breakdown but are not the same phrases. It does not list Great question, Absolutely, I'd be happy to, or Feel free to reach out in its watch lists. The page frames these as leftover chatbot correspondence pasted into articles, so the support is for the phrases being recognizable, not for a measured frequency.

**Item 5, recap endings.** The page's historical section summaries entry is the closest match. It records that older models closed with a conclusion section and restated the core idea at the end of paragraphs, with in summary, in conclusion and overall as watch phrases. It places this among historical signs, meaning newer models do it less. The skill's rule that a closing paragraph must add something rests on the user's request and the style manuals; the page confirms the habit exists and is recognizable, and its historical placement is a reminder that the form of the habit changes.

**Item 3, trailing commentary clauses.** The superficial-analysis section says a present-participle phrase added at the end of a sentence to comment on significance is a typical sign, and the vocabulary shows the verbs: highlighting, underscoring, ensuring, reflecting, fostering, contributing to. The skill's wording of this item (highlighting, underscoring, showcasing, ensuring, reflecting, making it) matches this pattern closely. Reinhart, in the PNAS paper the page cites, supplies the measurement; see that file. The page is the clearest popular description of the pattern and contributed the idea that the clause often claims significance without evidence, which supports the rule in section 3 to give a significance claim its own sentence with a real subject, so that a reader can see it needs support.

**Item 4, reflexive triplets.** The rule-of-three section supports the skill's rule, including the observation that models use triplets to make analysis look comprehensive. The skill's section 3 rule to list as many examples as you have, instead of trimming or padding to three, is the writer-facing counterpart.

**Item 2, correction framing.** The negative parallelism section lists not only X but Y, not X but Y, and Y rather than X. The skill's item 2 covers the same family, including the "Y, not X" form. The page contributes the observation that the reversed Y-rather-than-X form is a recent growth area, which justifies the skill's including Y, not X. The page also concedes that human writers use these constructions, which is why the skill allows a contrast when the reader actually holds the mistaken view. The ban itself follows from the user's explicit choice, not from this page.

**Item 1, em dashes.** The page's em-dash entry supports the observation that models use them heavily, including spaced and sales-like uses, and that the habit is now notorious. It also says the trend is shifting and that suppression has produced its own artifacts. The skill uses this as background only; the ban is the user's decision. The skill's instruction to check that fixes do not create new hits ("such as a colon-and-fragment construction repeated in every paragraph") is consistent with the page's observation that suppressing one punctuation habit pushes models into monotone comma and period use.

**Section 2, words.** The page's list of human-favored plain constructions (wrote over authored, used over utilized, tried over attempted, died over passed away) and its finding that human articles use simple is and has phrases supports the skill's plain-word rule (use, not utilize; try, not attempt) and the euphemism rule (people die). The page presents this as an observed contrast in Wikipedia text; it is not a controlled study. The skill's other rules in this section stand on the style manuals, not on this page.

**Section 4, evidence and attribution.** The vague-attribution sign (unnamed experts, inflated source counts, lists framed as non-exhaustive) supports the skill's rule to attribute anything unverified to a named source and its warning against false authority.

**Section 6, formatting.** The style signs (heavy boldface, inline-header lists, emoji, leftover Markdown) support item 11 and the section 6 limits on bold and lists.

**Item 7, announcements.** The page records that older models issued disclaimers about what is important to note, which supports the skill's item 7, though the exact phrase it names (It's worth noting) is not in the page's watch lists.

**Section 2 and item 12, plain verbs.** The page's section on avoidance of the plain copula is the source for two additions made in November 2026: a section 2 rule telling writers to use is, are and has when those are the accurate verbs, and revision item 12 (inflated plain verbs), which lists the substitutes the page names (serves as, stands as, features, offers and boasts). The study the page cites for this habit was not read for this file.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out most of the page, because most of it is about Wikipedia or about detection and not about prose quality. It omits all the citation signs (broken links, invalid DOIs, tracking parameters), markup signs, placeholder and template text, edit-summary and talk-page signs, Wikipedia-specific headings and leads, user-page signs, and the discussion of model biases. None of these affect how an agent should write a chat reply or report.

It also omits the page's content-level theory as a separate rule. The skill instead folds the idea of "specific beats generic" into section 2 (be specific) and section 4, where it is backed by the style manuals. It does not copy the page's watch phrases for significance puffery (stands as a testament, pivotal moment, evolving landscape) as a standalone list, since the user's list already contains the single words and since a list of phrases would add to the priming risk the skill tries to limit.

The skill does not adopt the page's era-by-era vocabulary tables. They change too fast to hard-code, and the page's newest set is based on the most recent, least verified observations. The skill also leaves out the page's warnings about false accusations, since the skill governs the agent's own writing and not detection of someone else's. The page's advice to fix deeper problems and not only surface signs is not a rule in the skill, though its first paragraph shares the spirit.

Finally, the skill leaves out the claim that Claude uses em dashes more than professional writers. That claim comes through a secondary summary of a study I did not read, and the user's ban does not depend on it.

## Conflicts with other references

The page is in mild tension with the vocabulary studies in the folder. Kobak, Juzek and Ward, and Liang each measure word overuse against a baseline and report ratios, so their word lists come from counting. This page's list comes from editor observation filtered by a corroboration rule, and it includes words that are absent from Kobak's measured list. The three measured studies also use different measures from one another, so none of them can be used to certify the Wikipedia list. The skill resolves this by treating the measured sources as primary and calling the Wikipedia-sourced words widely flagged but less well measured, which is why the skill's item 8 hedges with the rule to keep a word only when it is the most precise one. See kobak-excess-vocabulary.md, juzek-ward-lexical-overrepresentation.md and liang-ai-modified-peer-reviews.md.

On transitions, the page calls Additionally and similar openers a weak tell in isolation and says many style guides accept them. The skill still lists Additionally and Notably, in item 7, and its reason is that they usually carry no content, not that they signal a machine. The skill and the page agree on the facts and differ on the purpose, since the page is detecting and the skill is editing.

On em dashes, the page says humans use them often and that models used them more, and it reports the habit fading in the newest models. Freeburg (freeburg-em-dashes.md) also shows human writing uses them. The NYT, AP and Chicago manuals allow em dashes in various forms. The skill bans them by the user's choice and therefore is stricter than every source, including this page, which does not call for their removal.

On negative parallelisms, the page says the pattern is common in human listicle writing and an AI stereotype, while Boggia (boggia-artificial-epanorthosis.md) finds models overuse it in oratory but underuse it in informal Q&A. The page and the skill do not conflict, but the page does not distinguish registers, so it supports a ban more broadly than Boggia does. The skill's resolution is the user's explicit ban with the single allowed exception.

On voice and agentless passives, the page does not take a position and so does not conflict with reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md or rhodes-active-passive-voice.md, though it cites Reinhart for the participle finding.

On ban lists, the page's own advice to avoid treating signs as problems and its dating of the vocabulary agree with the caution in semantic-gravity-wells.md that naming words can prime them. The skill follows both by keeping its list only in the revision pass.
