## What the source covers

### Purpose, audience and authority

New Hart's Rules is Oxford University Press's house style manual, recast for a general audience. Its preface traces it to Horace Hart, Printer to the University of Oxford, whose rules began in 1893 as a 24-page booklet for the compositors of the Clarendon Press and ran to 39 editions before being rewritten in 2002 as The Oxford Guide to Style. The 2005 New Hart's Rules returned to the old name and the small handbook format, and the 2014 edition reviewed here is the version bound into the 2016 New Oxford Style Manual alongside the companion dictionary.

The preface states two aims that sit in some tension. The book still explains Oxford's traditional house style, and it also sets out to describe widely used contemporary practice across publishing, including ebooks, websites, newspapers, reports, and theses, while marking where British and American practice differ. The 2014 edition added a whole chapter on US and British English for that purpose. Most of its examples are drawn from the Oxford English Corpus and the Oxford Reading Programme, and its spellings match the current Oxford dictionaries. Its readers are writers, copy-editors, proofreaders, typesetters, and self-publishers; it says outright that it is meant to be used with a dictionary and, for copy-editors, with Judith Butcher's Cambridge handbook.

The manual's authority is institutional and corpus-informed. It reports no studies of readers. Where it gives a reason for a rule, the reason is usually one of three: the rule prevents a misreading, it keeps a text consistent, or it reflects what current usage in the corpus shows. That third reason makes the book more descriptive than its reputation suggests. It repeatedly records a drift in practice (fewer hyphens in compounds, fewer capitals, fewer italics for borrowed words, and singular they) and then recommends following the drift.

### Scope and structure

Hart's has 21 chapters: the parts of a book (1), preparing copy (2), spelling and hyphenation (3), punctuation (4), capitalization (5), names (6), italic, roman, and other type treatments (7), work titles (8), quotations and direct speech (9), abbreviations and symbols (10), numbers and dates (11), languages (12), law (13), science, mathematics, and computing (14), lists and tables (15), illustrations (16), notes and references (17), bibliography (18), indexing (19), copyright and publishing responsibilities (20), and US and British English (21). Roughly half the book concerns production, scholarly apparatus, or specialist material that rarely bears on how an agent writes prose. The chapters this file analyzes in depth are 3, 4, 5, 9, 10, 11, the lists half of 15, and 21, with shorter passages from 2, 7, and 14 where they bear on ordinary writing.

This edition has no general chapter on grammar or usage. Its closest equivalents are chapter 21's section on grammatical and syntactic variation between the two main dialects, and a passage in chapter 2 that sends editors to the Oxford dictionaries for disputed points such as the split infinitive and hopefully as a sentence adverb. The companion dictionary in Part II carries the word-level usage notes.

### The governing principle: consistency in service of the reader

The idea that runs through every chapter is that style choices exist only where two forms are equally defensible, and that once a choice is made it must be applied the same way throughout. Chapter 2 (2.3.1, pp. 33 to 35) gives the reason: inconsistent styling interferes between the text and the reader, can distract or mislead, and lowers a publication's credibility, while a consistently finished text projects reliability. The chapter distinguishes a style question (cooperate or co-operate) from an error (a misspelled place name), which needs no style decision at all, and it observes that leaving text unchanged is itself a style decision. It recommends a style sheet that records each decision with examples so later editors apply it the same way. The capitalization chapter repeats the advice, telling writers to settle capitalization principles before writing much and to keep a record.

The same chapter has a short passage on print versus web style (2.3.2, p. 37) and on web editing (2.1.2, pp. 29 to 30). For screen reading a house style may cap sentence length, encourage shorter paragraphs, more lists and headings, minimize punctuation, hyphens, and capitals, and replace click-here links with descriptive link text. Web editors are told to break material into smaller chunks focused on the essentials, with concise headings and planned links, because readers arrive through links and not in linear order.

Chapter 2 also addresses gendered pronouns (2.1.2, p. 29). It calls generic he old-fashioned or biased, says he or she suits formal contexts but becomes long-winded when repeated, and accepts singular they in speech and writing, especially after indefinite pronouns such as everyone, while telling editors not to impose it on an author who consistently used he or she. The copy-editor's list of duties includes flagging material that is not inclusive with regard to gender, race, sexual orientation, or disability.

### Chapter 3: Spelling and hyphenation

The spelling half (3.1 and 3.2, pp. 48 to 58) starts from the observation that English tolerates many variant spellings, that a dictionary always marks a preferred form, and that a writer who uses an acceptable variant consistently need not be corrected, provided related words are treated alike. It lists the regular American patterns (e for ae and oe, -ense for -ence, -er for -re, -or for -our, -og for -ogue, -ll in fulfill and enroll, and z in analyze) and explains Oxford's traditional -ize spelling: the ending dates from the 16th century, is not an Americanism, and matches the Greek root. It gives the words that take -ise in every variety (advertise, advise, comprise, compromise, devise, exercise, revise, supervise, surprise, televise, and others) and notes that British analyse cannot be spelled -yze, while American English uses analyze. It covers i before e, -able and -ible, the e retained in judgement and acknowledgement in British usage but dropped in American usage, practice and practise, licence and license, consonant doubling (British travelled, American traveled, with input, output, and kidnap as exceptions that double in both), plurals of compounds (brothers-in-law, attorneys general), and foreign plurals. On the last it prefers English plurals in general writing (formulas, forums, stadiums) and keeps the classical plural for words in -is (crises, bases); it notes that indexes is usual for books and indices for mathematics.

The hyphenation section (3.3, pp. 58 to 64) is the most useful part of the chapter for prose. It says hyphenation depends on a word's role and position, partly on house style and partly on taste, so no dictionary can settle it fully. Its rules:

- The trend is toward fewer hyphens in noun compounds, which are increasingly written open or closed. A text full of hyphens looks fussy and dated. Familiarity matters: climate change would look wrong hyphenated, and some established compounds read badly split.
- A compound modifier takes a hyphen before the noun and none after it: a well-known story but the story is well known; up-to-date records but the records are not up to date. Compounds containing an adjective (first-class seats) are hyphenated before a noun, while noun-plus-noun compounds used as modifiers (labour market liberalization) need not be.
- A compound adjective formed from an adjective and a participle (good-looking, double-breasted) is hyphenated in every position.
- No hyphen follows an adverb ending in -ly (a newly discovered compound). An adverb not ending in -ly takes a hyphen before the noun but not after (the much-loved broadcaster; he was very well known).
- A phrasal verb is never hyphenated (build up your pension), but the noun made from it is hyphenated or closed (a hold-up, a backup).
- Prefixes are often closed, especially in American English, but take a hyphen to prevent a misreading or a vowel collision (re-entry, anti-intellectual), to distinguish meanings (re-form and reform, re-cover and recover), and before a capital, a numeral, or a date (anti-Darwinism, pre-1990s). Former is more elegant than ex- before a compound title.
- A whole word used like a suffix (risk-averse, GPS-enabled, military-style) needs a hyphen, and the section shows how dropping it creates real ambiguity.
- Suspended hyphens mark a shared element (three- and six-cylinder models), though when the shared element is unfamiliar each word should be written out.

The rest of the chapter covers word division at line ends, which concerns typesetting only.

### Chapter 4: Punctuation

The chapter opens (4.1, p. 69) by separating two kinds of guidance: some sections describe correct usage that good English requires (it names the comma splice and the apostrophe in plurals), and others describe choices among valid styles where the point is consistency (it names the serial comma).

**Apostrophes (4.2, pp. 69 to 73).** Standard possessive rules, plus several points that matter for ordinary writing. Its and it's are distinguished. Plurals of abbreviations, numbers, names, and words used as nouns take no apostrophe (CDs, the 1990s, dos and don'ts); the so-called greengrocer's apostrophe is simply wrong. An apostrophe is allowed only where a plural would otherwise be unreadable, as with single letters (mind your p's and q's). An abbreviation used as a verb keeps an apostrophe before its suffix (OK'ing, OD'd). Business names that dropped their apostrophe (Harrods, Barclays Bank) are written as the business writes them.

**Commas (4.3, pp. 73 to 79).** Six topics.

- Restrictive and nonrestrictive clauses. A defining clause takes no commas; a nondefining one is enclosed in commas. In British English either which or that can introduce a restrictive clause, and only which a nonrestrictive one; the manual says American English does not use which for restrictive clauses. Appositives follow the same logic: my friend Julie (defining, no commas) against Julie, my friend, (nondefining). A parenthetical phrase needs a matching pair of commas, and the manual shows the error of leaving one off.
- The comma splice. Two main clauses joined by a comma alone are an error, including when the second begins with nevertheless, therefore, or as a result. The fixes are a conjunction, a semicolon or colon, or two sentences.
- Introductory elements. An introductory adverb, phrase, or subordinate clause is often followed by a comma, but a short phrase of time or place need not be, and omitting the comma there avoids clutter. The manual warns that dropping it can create ambiguity and gives a sentence where a year runs into a following number. A comma never separates a subject from its verb. Sentence adverbs such as however and moreover take a comma at the start of a sentence and a pair of commas mid-sentence, but not when however modifies an adjective.
- Adjectives in a series. Two qualitative (gradable) adjectives take a comma between them (a long, thin piece); adjectives of different types or classifying adjectives from different systems do not (a large black gibbon, annual economic growth).
- The serial comma (4.3.5, pp. 77 to 78). The manual records that Oxford has imposed it consistently for a century, which is why it is called the Oxford comma, and that many other publishers use it. Its general rule is consistency either way, but it adds that the comma resolves ambiguity, especially when an item contains its own and, and that an isolated serial comma can be used for clarity even in a text that otherwise omits it. Its examples are a menu in which a missing comma joins the last two dishes and a list of bishops in which reordering the sees creates an ambiguity only the comma resolves. It also requires a comma before a closing etc. or and the like, and it warns that every item joined in a series must fit the words that introduce it: a sentence saying text should be lively, readable, and have humour fails because the third item does not follow should be.
- Other uses. Commas separate thousands in general text. A comma follows namely and for example when they open a parenthetical phrase but not when they introduce a single item. Oxford writes no comma after i.e. and e.g.; the manual notes that American usage does.

**Semicolons (4.4, pp. 79 to 80).** A semicolon joins closely related main clauses that could each stand alone; when one clause explains the other, a colon fits better. Semicolons also separate list items that contain their own commas.

**Colons (4.5, pp. 80 to 81).** The colon points forward, from premise to conclusion, cause to effect, general to example, and does the work of namely, that is, because, or therefore. What follows need not be a sentence. A colon does not come after linking words like namely and must follow a main clause; it must not introduce a list or statement that completes the introducing sentence, so a sentence saying authors worth studying include Thackeray, Trollope, and Dickens takes no colon. The manual says British English lowercases after a colon while American usage often capitalizes a full sentence. It contrasts the colon with the dash, which is more informal and implies an afterthought.

**Full points, ellipses, question and exclamation marks (4.6 to 4.9, pp. 81 to 85).** Headings and titles take no full point even when they are sentences. An abbreviation's point does double duty at the end of a sentence. Ellipses mark omissions from quotations; Oxford keeps a sentence's own full point before an ellipsis. Indirect questions and polite requests phrased as questions do not take a question mark. A question mark in parentheses can signal doubt, but the manual says the device is often unclear about what is doubted and that rewording is usually better. The exclamation mark should be avoided for emphasis and used only minimally in serious texts.

**Dashes (4.11, pp. 86 to 88).** Oxford and most American publishers use a closed-up em dash for parenthetical breaks; other British publishers use a spaced en dash. The em dash rules: no punctuation before a single dash or the first of a pair, no capital after a dash, a pair marks a stronger break than commas and draws more attention than parentheses, at most one pair per sentence, and a single dash before a closing phrase is more casual than a colon and implies an afterthought. The en dash expresses ranges and relations (Dover to Calais crossing, editor to author relationship), and a range is written either from X to Y or with an en dash, never a mix of the two.

**Brackets (4.12, pp. 88 to 90).** Parentheses hold digressions, glosses, translations, expansions of abbreviations, and ancillary information. Square brackets hold an editor's insertions into quoted matter. A complete sentence in parentheses standing alone is capitalized and ends with its own full point inside; one embedded in another sentence is not. Brackets inside brackets should be avoided in running text.

**Slash (4.13.1, pp. 90 to 91).** The slash denotes alternatives (either/or, on/off), but it is sometimes misread as and instead of or, so the manual says it is usually best in running text to spell out the alternatives (his or her, on or off). Its other uses are technical: some abbreviations, dates, URLs, ratios such as miles per day.

**Quotation marks (4.14, pp. 92 to 93).** British practice is single marks outside and double inside; the manual says newspapers often reverse this and American practice does so uniformly. Quotation marks may enclose an unfamiliar, newly coined, or technical word, or distance the writer from a claim or a colloquialism, but only at the word's first occurrence, and they should not be used for emphasis. Asterisks around a word, as in email, stand in for italics and should not replace ellipsis dots.

### Chapter 5: Capitalization

The chapter (pp. 94 to 106) says capitalization cannot be reduced to absolute rules and depends on a word's role, house style, and taste, then gives broad principles whose direction is clear: use fewer capitals. Initial capitals exist to mark a proper name off from the same words used descriptively (the City against the city of London; the West against the west). Where a capital makes no detectable difference in meaning, it should be dropped; overuse is obtrusive and can confuse readers by suggesting distinctions that do not exist. Text in full capitals online reads as shouting and is hard to read.

Specific rules relevant to general prose:

- After a full name has been given, later short references revert to lower case (Cambridge University, then the university). Some formal documents capitalize the short form, but the manual calls the full-capitals-then-lower-case rule easier to apply consistently.
- A plural generic term shared by two names is lower case (lakes Erie and Huron; Oxford and Cambridge universities), because the plural is a description and not part of either name.
- Titles of office are lower case unless used before a name, as a name, or in direct address (the prime minister, but Prime Minister Churchill). Where titles occur often, capitalizing them only before a name is the clearest rule.
- Seasons, centuries, and decades are lower case; days, months, and holidays are capitalized.
- Trade names are capitalized and keep their own unusual casing (iPad, PlayStation), but the verbs made from them (to google) are lower case, and the manual prefers a generic verb or noun where possible (do an online search).
- Personal names follow the form the person prefers or is known by (6.1.1, p. 107), including unusual casing.

The chapter also notes that British English lowercases the first word after a colon while American style may capitalize a complete sentence (5.2, p. 95).

### Chapter 7: Italic, bold and capitals for emphasis

Chapter 7 (pp. 127 to 135) is mostly about typography, but three passages bear on emphasis in prose. Italic emphasis should be used sparingly, and the manual suggests making the emphasis clear through sentence structure or an intensifying word instead (7.2.1, pp. 128 to 129). Bold for emphasis in running text should be avoided as too startling; the manual prefers no typographic distinction or the less obtrusive italic (7.4, p. 132). Bold is acceptable for a newly introduced term that is about to be defined, especially in textbooks, guides, and instructional text. Full capitals are too prominent for emphasis in open text (7.5.1, p. 133). On websites, underlining should not be used for emphasis because readers take it for a link (7.6).

### Chapter 9: Quotations and direct speech

The chapter (pp. 160 to 173) starts from the principle that a direct quotation reproduces the exact words of the original and must be clearly marked, unlike a paraphrase. Short quotations run into the text in quotation marks and long ones are displayed without them; the manual gives fifty words as a rough threshold but says there is no firm rule.

On punctuation with quotation marks (9.2.3, pp. 162 to 164) it sets out the transatlantic split precisely. Traditional British style places commas and full points according to sense: inside the closing mark only if they belong to the quoted matter. American style places commas and full points inside the closing mark regardless, and the manual notes that much modern British fiction and journalism now does the same. Question and exclamation marks go inside only when they belong to the quotation, and a sentence never takes two terminal marks.

Chapter 14 adds an exception that matters for technical text (14.5.2, pp. 287 to 288). When code or markup is quoted in running text, punctuation falls outside the closing quotation mark so it cannot be mistaken for part of the syntax, and the manual says this should be imposed even in American computing texts, though not extended to ordinary quotations. If the code is set in a distinct typeface, quotation marks are unnecessary. Code should be reproduced exactly, and long code inserted from the source instead of retyped.

On altering quotations (9.3, pp. 165 to 168): spelling, capitalization, and punctuation follow the original, but obvious slips may be corrected silently, house quotation marks and dashes may be standardized, and an initial capital may be lowercased to fit the surrounding sentence. Words added to a quotation go in square brackets, sparingly. The sic marker is reserved for removing real doubt about whether an error is the quoter's, not for flagging oddities, and a bracketed exclamation mark as editorial comment is ruled out. Omissions are marked with an ellipsis, but not at the start or end of a quotation, since readers understand that the source continues. Typography such as bold or underlining in the original need not be reproduced.

The section on non-English quotations (9.7) says translation is normal unless there is a reason to keep the original, and that the writer should ask whether the original actually helps the reader.

### Chapter 10: Abbreviations and symbols

The chapter (pp. 174 to 185) gives its general rule for work aimed at a general audience: keep abbreviations and symbols out of open text unless they are very familiar (US, BBC, UN), would be less familiar spelled out (DNA), save needed space, or stand for terms repeated so often that the short form is easier to absorb (10.1, p. 175). They suit parentheses, notes, tables, and captions better than running text. An abbreviation should be spelled out at first mention with the short form in parentheses, kept out of headings where possible, and need not be redefined in each chapter unless readers may read out of order, in which case a glossary helps. A readable short form of a name (the Institute) is often better than its initials on second reference, and similar terms should not be mixed in full and abbreviated forms.

Other rules: avoid ampersands except in established combinations and company names (10.2.2). Informal contractions are acceptable in less formal writing and appear even in academic work, but editors should not impose them except for consistency (10.2.3). Multiletter acronyms and initialisms take no points in British and technical usage, while some American styles use them (U.S.). Lower-case abbreviations cannot begin a sentence. A sentence should not begin with a symbol: spell the word out or recast (10.3, p. 180). The choice of a or an before an abbreviation follows its pronunciation, not its spelling, so the writer must know whether readers say the letters or the words (10.4, p. 181). Abbreviations form plurals with s and no apostrophe (10.5).

Section 10.6 (pp. 182 to 183) is the most directly useful for prose. The writer must not confuse e.g. (for example) with i.e. (that is). In running text the manual prefers the words for example, such as, like, and that is; the abbreviations belong in parentheses and notes. Neither can start a sentence in running text. Oxford writes them without a following comma; American practice adds one. Etc. belongs in technical and scholarly contexts such as notes; elsewhere the manual prefers such as or for example before a list or and so on after it. Etc. should not follow a single item, since at least two are needed to show how the list would continue; it should not end a list introduced by e.g., including, for example, or such as, which already signal an incomplete list; and and etc. is redundant. For lists of people the manual says to write and others. The approximate marker c. is used mainly with dates and quantities; in discursive prose about and some are preferred for quantities, and approximately in scientific text (10.7, p. 184).

### Chapter 11: Numbers and dates

Oxford's own threshold in nontechnical text is to spell out numbers below 100, with technical contexts spelling out only up to ten (11.1.2, p. 186), and the manual says websites tend to use figures more. The threshold is a general rule with exceptions that the chapter then lists.

- Approximation. A rounded number is often better than an exact one, especially when the sentence says about, some, or estimated, and spelling out a round approximation avoids false precision (about a thousand, not about 1000). A precise figure should never carry about, which is a trap in unit conversion: about three kilometres should not become about 1.86 miles (pp. 186 to 187).
- Over and more than. The manual treats them as synonyms in modern usage while noting contexts where one sounds more natural.
- Consistency within a sentence. When one number in a sentence must be a figure, comparable numbers in that sentence take figures too (90 to 100, not ninety to 100). Where two sets of numbers are mixed, words for one set and figures for the other can help (thirty 10-page pamphlets); anything more complex belongs in a table.
- Sentence starts. A number that begins a sentence is spelled out, and a cumbersome one is avoided by recasting (11.1.2, p. 188).
- Ages take figures as cardinals (a girl of 15) and words as ordinals and decades. Legal documents may give amounts in both words and figures.
- Ranges (11.1.4, pp. 189 to 191). Use either from X to Y or an en-dash range, never a mix such as from 1939 to 45 written with a dash; likewise between X and Y, never between X to Y or between X with a dash. Ranges with negative numbers should use words to avoid confusing a minus with a dash. Repeat the magnitude where omitting it would mislead: 1 billion to 2 billion, because 1 to 2 billion literally starts at one.
- Agreement (11.1.5, p. 191). A quantity considered as a unit takes a singular verb (ten miles is a lot to repave); considered severally, plural. The number, the percentage, and the proportion take singular verbs; a number and a proportion take plural ones. None takes the singular when it means not one and the plural when it means not any. Numbers form plurals without apostrophes (the 1960s).
- Units and money (11.2, pp. 193 to 194). Figures go with units, percentages, and expressions of proportion. Nontechnical British text writes per cent; American English writes percent. Symbols precede figures without a space. Trailing zeros (.00) appear only when other amounts in context have decimals. Large sums mix figures and words (£5 million).
- Times (11.3, pp. 194 to 195). O'clock goes only with exact hours in words; a.m. and p.m. go only with figures; a round hour needs no :00; 12 a.m. and 12 p.m. are often misunderstood, so noon and midnight are clearer.
- Dates (11.5, pp. 197 to 198). Use cardinal figures for the day, not ordinals (12 August, not 12th August). British order is day, month, year without commas; American is month, day, year. All-figure dates are inappropriate in running text and dangerous across the Atlantic because 11/2/03 means different days to American and British readers; documents for mixed readers should spell out or abbreviate the month. ISO year-month-day order is increasingly used in technical, computing, and financial contexts.
- Decades (11.6.1, p. 198). Write the 1960s or the sixties, never the '60s, and write both decades in full when giving two. The manual draws a distinction between the word form, which evokes a decade's culture, and the figure form, which labels a span of time.

### Chapter 15: Lists and tables

The lists section (15.1, pp. 299 to 303) holds that the text must make clear what the items in a list have in common, and that lists must be grammatically consistent and balanced, down to whether each item takes an article. An open-ended list should give at least three items, enough to show how it would continue, and a list introduced by includes, for example, or such as should not end in etc. A colon before a run-in list requires a main clause before it.

The manual distinguishes run-in lists, which follow ordinary sentence rules and need no numbers, from displayed lists, which draw attention, make material easy to consult, and break up text, and it asks writers to establish a logic for which material is displayed. For displayed lists: the introduction is usually a partial sentence ending in a colon, and Oxford even allows a stem ending in to with each item completing the infinitive; complete-sentence items start with capitals and end with full points; fragment items are lower case with no end punctuation, and the last may or may not take a point if treated consistently; and and or should be dropped from the end of the penultimate item. The manual recommends choosing one system and rewording stems or items to fit it, and says to avoid mixing sentence and fragment items in one list. It also notes that screen-reading software reads on into the next paragraph if the last item has no closing point. Numbers or letters are used when items are cited elsewhere or when order or hierarchy matters; bullets otherwise.

On tables (15.2.1, pp. 304 to 306): use a table for information too complex for a list or prose, especially for comparison, but consider whether a few sentences would be more digestible; the table must be relevant to the argument and must not merely repeat the text; and in documents, tables should be cited by number and not by position (the table above), since layout changes.

### Chapter 21: US and British English

The newest chapter (pp. 407 to 422) is a localization guide. It catalogs punctuation differences (American commas after for example, that is, e.g. and i.e.; commas and periods inside closing quotes; a capital after a colon before a full sentence; colons in times), units, number formats, dates, spelling patterns, individual variants, and compounds (American English closes more of them: percent, noninvasive, and northeast). It lists word pairs that differ entirely and, more usefully, words used in both dialects with different meanings that a reader may silently misread (21.5.2, pp. 414 to 417). Examples include table as a verb (to bring forward in Britain, to shelve in the United States), quite (somewhat against very), momentarily (for a moment against in a moment), moot (open to debate against irrelevant), through as an adjective, mean, smart, homely, and pants.

Section 21.6 (pp. 420 to 422), on grammatical and syntactic variation, is the edition's nearest approach to a usage discussion. It says that differences of this kind contain no unfamiliar words yet still sound slightly off to a reader of the other dialect, and that fixing them depends mainly on the editor's ear. Its examples are noun countability, verb agreement with collective nouns (American English treats a company as singular), modal preferences, transitivity of some verbs, submodifiers (it says many perfunctory uses of quite, and many uses of very before an adjective, can simply be deleted for American readers), wording of fixed idioms, the subjunctive after verbs of demanding (more common in American English), and tense with already, just, and yet (informal American English uses the simple past, formal usage the present perfect). Section 21.5.3 covers got and gotten and the -t past forms (learnt, spelt) that American English replaces with -ed.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The manual's strengths for this skill are its precision about mechanics, its explicit reasons (ambiguity, consistency, and current usage), and its careful separation of British from American practice, which makes it easy to keep the rules that transfer and set aside those that do not. Its descriptive streak, visible in its acceptance of singular they, its push toward fewer hyphens and capitals, and its willingness to state where usage has moved, keeps it closer to current practice than its age suggests. Its limits: it is British first, so its defaults on quotation marks, -ise spellings, punctuation outside quotes, colons, Mr without a point, and per cent all lose to American sources under this skill's precedence. It is aimed at editors preparing books, so much of its length concerns production. It tests nothing on readers. And the edition reviewed has no grammar and usage chapter, so it says little about word choice, register, or idiom beyond the cross-dialect notes in chapter 21; those questions are handed to the dictionary.

## What the skill applies

The skill took more from this manual than from any other general style guide except the news manuals, mostly in sections 2, 3, 6, and 8. Each entry below names the skill's section and bullet, then the Hart's location, then any place where the skill's wording goes past what Hart's says.

**Section 2 (Words).**

- The abbreviation bullet. Spelling out an unfamiliar abbreviation with the short form in parentheses, using a short form only when readers know it better or the term recurs often, preferring a short plain phrase to initials on second reference, and choosing a or an by pronunciation all come from 10.1 and 10.4. The examples (an HTTP request, a URL) are the skill's own. The clause against coining abbreviations predates this source.
- The e.g., i.e., and etc. bullet. Writing for example and that is in running text and keeping the abbreviations for parentheses and tables follows 10.6, and the ban on etc. after such as or including follows 10.6 and 15.1.2. The advice to name the rest of a category instead of writing etc. goes beyond Hart's, which prefers and so on or the like outside technical text. That wording matches IBM.
- The slash and ampersand bullet. Writing or or and in place of a slash, and keeping the ampersand for names, follows 4.13.1 and 10.2.2. The exceptions for fixed terms such as client/server and read/write, and the rules on and/or and file(s), come from other sources.
- The bullet on words readers take two ways. Momentarily and to table a proposal come from Table 21.3 in 21.5.2. Biweekly and bimonthly come from elsewhere.
- The intensifier bullet. Quite and rather as fillers come from 21.6, which says perfunctory quite and very can often be cut for American readers. Hart's frames this as a dialect point; the skill makes it a general rule.
- The trademark bullet. Writing a name the way its owner does and preferring a generic verb to a trademark verb follow 5.15 and 6.1.1.
- The one-name bullet. Spelling a term one way throughout reflects the consistency principle of 2.3.1 and 3.1.1, though the email and dataset examples are the skill's.
- The idiom bullet. This bullet came from the owner. Hart's has no rule on idiomatic word choice beyond the observation in 21.6 that cross-dialect usage is fixed by ear, which agrees in spirit with the skill's test of what sounds off to a native ear.

**Section 3 (Sentences).**

- The parallel-series bullet. The check of each item against the words that introduce the series comes from 4.3.5 (the lively, readable, and humour example) and 15.1.2. The skill's own example about a web page replaces Hart's.
- The comma bullet. Closing a parenthetical phrase with its second comma comes from 4.3.1, the ban on a comma between subject and verb from 4.3.3, and the comma-splice rule, including however and therefore, from 4.3.2.
- The quotation-mark bullet comes from 4.14. The skill is stricter than Hart's: Hart's allows quotation marks that distance the writer from a claim or a colloquial word at first use, while the skill forbids them for doubt about an ordinary word.
- The exclamation-point bullet agrees with 4.9, which allows the mark only minimally in serious text. The skill's limit to quotations comes from NYT and Chicago.
- The sentence-length bullet has only a faint link here: 2.3.2 says a web house style may cap sentence length. The figures of 25 and 40 words come from other sources.

**Section 4 (Numbers and evidence).**

- The precision bullet (round an estimate, no about before an exact count, round a converted unit to the original's precision) comes from 11.1.2. The skill's example of kilometers and miles adapts Hart's own example.
- The quotation bullet (quote exactly or paraphrase, an ellipsis for a cut, square brackets for an insertion) comes from 9.1 and 9.3.2 to 9.3.3.
- The final clause of the uncertainty bullet, stating doubt in words instead of a bare question mark, comes from 4.8.2.

**Section 5 (People and fairness).** The singular they sentence agrees with 2.1.2, which calls generic he old-fashioned and accepts they. Nothing else in the section comes from this source.

**Section 6 (Structure and format).**

- The list bullets. Making every item the same kind (complete sentences with periods or fragments without end punctuation), rewording an item or the introduction to fit, numbering only when order matters or items are cited, and not ending an item with and or or all come from 15.1.4 and 15.1.5. The skill capitalizes fragment items, which Hart's does not (see Conflicts).
- The table bullet. The rule that a table should add to the text and not repeat it, and that a few sentences can serve better, comes from 15.2.1.
- The bold bullet. Bolding a term at the point of definition, never using bold, italics, or capitals for emphasis inside a sentence, and building emphasis into the sentence's structure come from 7.2.1, 7.4, and 7.5.1. The skill's technique of putting the key word at the end or in a short sentence of its own goes beyond Hart's, which suggests sentence structure or an intensifying word without detail.
- The headings bullet's ban on closing punctuation agrees with 4.6.

**Section 7 (Tone).** The register bullet's last sentence, picking one approach to contractions for a piece, rests on 10.2.3 and the consistency principle in 2.3.1.

**Section 8 (Mechanics).**

- The spelling bullet (American spelling held throughout, the -ize and -yze endings, the list of verbs that end in -ise in every system) comes from 3.1.2 to 3.1.3, 3.2.1, and chapter 21. Hart's lists more -ise verbs than the skill does; the skill keeps the common ones. Gray and toward are from other sources.
- The comma-in-numbers bullet follows 4.3.6 and 11.1.3, which also exempt years. The exemptions for version numbers and code are the skill's.
- The sentence-start bullet comes from 11.1.2 and, for symbols, 10.3.
- The range bullet (to alone, from paired with to, between paired with and, never between paired with to, and a repeated magnitude) comes from 11.1.4.
- The dates bullet. The written month and the warning against all-figure dates come from 11.5 and 21.3.3; decades as the 1990s from 11.6.1; and 4 p.m., noon, and midnight from 11.3. The American month-day-year order replaces Hart's British order.
- The serial-comma bullet is the owner's choice. Its stated reason, that the comma removes the misreading when the last two items could be taken as a pair, is Hart's argument in 4.3.5.
- The colon bullet. Putting a colon only after a complete clause and never before an object comes from 4.5 and 15.1.3. The capital after a colon follows the American sources, against Hart's British rule.
- The compound-modifier bullet (hyphen before the noun, none after, none after an -ly adverb) comes from 3.3.3.
- The phrasal-verb bullet (set up but the setup) comes from 3.3.3. The login example is the skill's.
- The prefix bullet. Hyphens before a capital or numeral and where the closed form misreads (re-create, re-sign) come from 3.3.4. Closing up most prefixes follows the American tendency that 21.4.3 describes. The hyphen after self- is not from the sections I read.
- The apostrophe bullet (no apostrophe in plurals, its and it's) comes from 4.2.1, 4.2.2, and 10.5.
- The quotation-punctuation bullet. The American placement of periods and commas comes from Hart's account of American practice in 9.2.3. Putting a copyable literal in code formatting so no punctuation attaches to it draws on 14.5.2, which keeps punctuation outside quoted code even in American texts; the skill solves the same problem with code formatting.
- The that/which bullet's added sentence on nonrestrictive phrases comes from 4.3.1.
- The agreement sentence (the number of failures is, a number of failures are) comes from 11.1.5. The data and criteria rules are from other sources.
- The last bullet's rule on roman type for borrowed terms such as ad hoc agrees with 7.2.2, which says naturalized foreign words lose their italics. Cannot, all right, and no one come from the dictionary part and other sources; 21.4.3 confirms no one as two words in American English.

**Section 10 (Revision pass).**

- Item 10 adds an estimate given false precision, from 11.1.2.
- Item 11 adds words in capitals or bold for emphasis and lists that mix sentences and fragments, from 7.4, 7.5.1, and 15.1.4.
- Item 13 (Mechanical slips) was built from this source's proposals: scare quotes, a slash or ampersand standing for and or or, e.g., i.e., or etc. in running text, an apostrophe in a plural, a hyphen after an -ly adverb or in a verb, a sentence starting with a figure, an all-figure date, and a missing serial comma. The bare This at the start of a sentence is from other sources.

**Examples.** The new example on describing a tool removes e.g. and etc. from running text, which applies 10.6.

## What the skill omits

Most of Hart's concerns book production, scholarly apparatus, and specialist notation, and the skill leaves it out: the parts of a book, copy preparation and proof marks, names of people and places beyond the preferred-form rule, type treatment of work titles, other languages, law, science and mathematics, illustrations, notes, bibliography, indexing, and copyright. Word division at line ends, Roman numerals, regnal years, historical calendars, era abbreviations, and currency formatting also go, because they rarely arise in an agent's prose.

Among the British conventions Hart's describes, the skill omits single quotation marks, punctuation placed by sense outside closing quotes, a lowercase word after a colon, -ise and -our spellings, per cent, day-month-year dates, contractions such as Mr and Dr without a point, and the spaced en dash. The precedence rule gives the American form in each case.

From my phase 1 proposals, these were left out or cut down:

- Keeping abbreviations out of headings (from proposal 1) did not survive. The skill's headers bullet doesn't address it.
- From the e.g. and etc. proposal, the comma after e.g. and i.e., the ban on etc. after a single item, and and others for lists of people were cut. The comma rule matters less once the abbreviations are limited to parentheses and tables, and the other two are rare.
- Quite and moot were dropped from the bullet on words readers take two ways, though quite reappears as a filler in the intensifier bullet. Moot is rare in agent prose.
- Treating articles alike across the items in a series (proposal 8) was cut, probably as too fine a point for the bullet's length.
- So far as an example of a word that starts a spliced clause (proposal 9) was dropped in favor of however and therefore.
- Two quotation rules from proposal 14 were cut: no ellipsis at the start or end of a quotation, and the narrow use of [sic].
- Lowercase fragment items in lists (proposal 15) were replaced by capitalized fragments, the owner's decision.
- Hart's acceptance of a partial-sentence introduction before a displayed list was overruled by the skill's requirement for a complete sentence.
- The em dash rules (one pair per sentence, no capital after a dash) do not apply, because the owner bans the mark.
- The rule that comparable numbers in a sentence all take figures once one of them does (11.1.2, from proposal 26) was briefly in section 8, narrowed to a series, and was then removed. The NYT manual spells out numbers under 10 even in a series with larger numbers, and the NYT outranks Hart's in the source order, so the skill follows the NYT.

The proposals did not cover several Hart's rules that the skill also lacks. The capitalization chapter's case for fewer capitals (5.3.1), lower case for titles of office except before a name (5.10), and reverting to lower case after a full name has been given once (5.3.2) would help with agents' habit of capitalizing feature names and job titles, and they are candidates for a later revision. The comma between coordinate adjectives (4.3.4), semicolons in a series whose items contain commas (4.4), the missing question mark after an indirect question (4.8.1), and the agreement of none (11.1.5) were left out as lower-frequency points. Hart's treatment of over and more than as synonyms is not in the skill either way.

## Conflicts with other references

**nyt-manual-of-style.md and ap-stylebook.md (serial comma).** Hart's 4.3.5 calls the serial comma Oxford's century-old house rule and argues that it removes ambiguity. The NYT and AP omit it in a simple series and add it only when the sentence would be unclear. The skill now uses it in every series by the owner's explicit choice, which overrides every source under AGENTS.md. Both of those reference files still describe the skill as following the NYT here; their conflict sections predate version 2.0.0.

**nyt-manual-of-style.md (numbers in a series).** The NYT reference records that the 2015 manual spells out numbers under 10 even in a series with larger numbers. Hart's 11.1.2 says the opposite: when one number in a sentence must be a figure, comparable numbers in that sentence become figures too, and google-developer-style-guide.md records the same rule for Google. The NYT ranks first in the source order, so the skill follows it, and section 8 has no rule that converts small numbers to figures in a mixed series.

**ap-stylebook.md and chicago-manual-of-style.md (a number at the start of a sentence).** All three manuals say to spell out a number that begins a sentence. AP exempts years, and the Chicago file records that the 18th edition now lets a year start a sentence while recommending rewording. Hart's makes no exception and suggests recasting, and the skill follows that stricter form; its example of a year (the 2024 release) shows the recast.

**nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, ibm-style-guide.md, and red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md (capital after a colon).** Hart's lowercases after a colon in British English and notes that American style may capitalize a complete sentence. NYT, AP, Chicago 18, and Red Hat capitalize a complete sentence; Microsoft, Google, and IBM lowercase. The skill capitalizes, following the higher-ranked news manuals and Chicago. Hart's agrees with Chicago and Red Hat on the other half of the bullet: a colon needs a complete clause before it and never sits between a verb and its object.

**ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, ibm-style-guide.md, and red-hat-technical-writing-style-guide.md (list items).** Hart's 15.1.4 puts fragment items in lowercase without end punctuation and full-sentence items in capitals with periods, and says not to mix the two. AP gives every item a capital and a period. IBM and Red Hat give fragments no end punctuation and say not to mix. The skill takes AP's capital and the other sources' bare ending, so its fragment style matches none of them exactly. The combination was decided when the skill was revised. On the introduction, Hart's allows a partial sentence ending in a colon, even one ending in to. Chicago, Google, IBM, Red Hat, and the NYT's bullets guidance call for a complete sentence, and the skill follows them.

**nyt-manual-of-style.md (editing quotations).** The NYT reference records that the manual limits editing of quotations and discourages bracketed additions and ellipses that distract, preferring paraphrase. Hart's 9.3 treats brackets and ellipses as normal tools, used sparingly. The skill states both options, quote exactly or paraphrase, and tells the writer how to mark a cut or an insertion. The NYT's preference for paraphrase is compatible with the first half of that bullet.

**nyt-manual-of-style.md (singular they).** The 2015 NYT manual requires he or she after an indefinite pronoun. Hart's 2.1.2 calls generic he old-fashioned and accepts singular they, as Chicago 18 does. The skill's singular they rests on Hart's, Chicago, and current usage against the higher-ranked NYT; the NYT reference explains why.

**ap-stylebook.md, nyt-manual-of-style.md, and chicago-manual-of-style.md (dashes).** Hart's uses a closed em dash with at most one pair per sentence, and notes that other British publishers use a spaced en dash. The owner's ban on em dashes overrides it, as it overrides the other manuals.

**microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, and ibm-style-guide.md (e.g., i.e., and etc.).** Google bans e.g. and i.e. in most contexts, and IBM bans Latin abbreviations outright. Hart's prefers the English phrases in running text but keeps the abbreviations for parentheses and notes. The skill takes Hart's middle position, which also matches its own examples.

**oxford-dictionary-for-writers.md.** The dictionary is Part II of the same volume and shares its spelling authority, so the two do not disagree on any point I found. Hart's hands word-level usage questions to the dictionary (2.1.2), and the skill's last section 8 bullet draws on both. When I read it, the dictionary file's own analysis sections were still placeholders, so I could not check its account in detail.

## Review notes

I worked from OCR text of a scanned copy of the 2016 New Oxford Style Manual. The text came through cleanly enough to read, with occasional garbled characters (italic markers, ligatures, the pound sign, en dashes rendered as hyphens or em dashes), which I corrected from context. I did not view page images. Page numbers cited are the printed Hart's page numbers taken from the running footers.

Read in full: the volume and Hart's prefaces and contents; chapter 3 (pp. 48 to 68); chapter 4 (pp. 69 to 93); chapter 5 (pp. 94 to 106); chapter 7 (pp. 127 to 135); chapter 9 (pp. 160 to 173); chapter 10 (pp. 174 to 185); chapter 11 through 11.8.2 (pp. 186 to 203); section 15.1 and the opening of 15.2 (pp. 299 to 306); and chapter 21 (pp. 407 to 422). Also read: sections 2.1.2 and 2.3 (pp. 27 to 38), 6.1.1 (p. 107), 14.1.1 to 14.1.3 (pp. 266 to 268), and 14.5 (pp. 286 to 289). I searched the whole Hart's text for idiom, participle, split infinitive, hopefully, jargon, concise, passive, and need, needed, and necessary. Not read: chapters 1, 8, 12, 13, and 16 to 20, the rest of chapters 2, 6, and 14, the rest of 15.2, and the calendar sections at the end of chapter 11.

Doubts. The edition is muddled in the front matter: the copyright page lists 2003, 2012, and 2016, while the preface calls the volume a new second edition; I cite it as the 2016 printing of New Hart's Rules, second edition (2014). This edition has no grammar and usage chapter, so I cannot say whether Hart's would endorse the skill's word-level rules beyond what chapter 21 says. I did not confirm a rule on self- prefixes. For the conflicts I read the relevant passages of the other reference files, not the source books, so statements about NYT, AP, Chicago, Microsoft, Google, IBM, and Red Hat rest on those files.
