# The Associated Press Stylebook, 56th edition

- **Source:** The Associated Press, *The Associated Press Stylebook*, 56th edition, Associated Press, 2022. Lead editor Paula Froke. Foreword by Daisy Veerasingham, AP president and chief executive.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** Print (spiral-bound) 56th edition, 2022; also available as the subscription site AP Stylebook Online, which is updated continuously and so differs from the print edition in places. This review used a full-text extraction of the 56th edition.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose, audience, and philosophy

The AP Stylebook began in 1953 as a 60-page booklet on capitalization, punctuation, grammar, and spelling for the staff of a news cooperative. The foreword of the 56th edition says the book is now about ten times that size and serves communicators in many fields, not only wire reporters. The edition is organized like a dictionary, with an A-to-Z section of entries, followed by chapters on punctuation, business, inclusive storytelling, data journalism, polls and surveys, health and science, social media and web reporting, digital security, religion, sports, a briefing on media law, and the AP statement of news values and principles.

The essay by the lead editor on how the Stylebook team works states the philosophy in terms a writing guide can use. The team's goals are accuracy, clarity, fairness, and concision. It calls itself part prescriptivist and part descriptivist: it lays down rules, and it tracks how the language is actually used and adjusts. It says plainly that many entries are judgment calls with gray areas, that the guidance is built for a broad international audience of ordinary readers, and that outside organizations may modify it for their own readers. The punctuation chapter repeats the stance: its entries are guidelines, and guidelines are still not to be treated casually. The editors describe a slow process (weekly meetings, drafts by beat specialists, long debate, revision after public feedback) and openly say the result is a set of compromises. For a writer, the practical lesson is that the book expects judgment. It gives a default and a reason, and it expects the writer to depart from the default when clarity requires it.

Two features distinguish it from the other manuals in this folder. First, it is a wire-service book written for text that must be understood quickly by a mixed audience, so its defaults favor short forms, minimal punctuation, and few typographic devices. Its history shows in odd places: it does not use en dashes because older text-transmission systems could not carry them, and it does not use square brackets for the same reason. Second, a large share of the 2022 edition covers how to describe people and how to handle evidence, with little on mechanics. For agent prose, those two parts turn out to matter at least as much as the comma rules.

### Clarity and word choice

The entry on cliches and jargon is short and direct. It says cliches work as a signal to the reader that nothing new is coming, and it tells the writer to use original, specific phrasing. On jargon it grants that specialized vocabulary has a place among specialists, points out that jargon can also work as insider code or as a way to disguise, soften, or editorialize, and gives the remedy: do not repeat the term, translate it, and push the source for the real meaning. The separate jargon entry says to avoid it in general and, where it is appropriate, to explain any word most readers will not know.

The health and science chapter makes this advice concrete. Its writing-tips section says the writer is a translator between people who speak the language of science and ordinary readers. It lists medical and scientific jargon with plain replacements: clinician becomes doctor, efficacy becomes a statement of how well something works, literature becomes other studies, pathogen becomes germs, proportion becomes share, prevalence becomes how common something is, trials become studies or research, and underlying condition becomes other conditions. It gives a list of science-writing cliches to avoid, including cutting edge, holy grail, game changing, low-hanging fruit, paradigm shift, perfect storm, silver bullet, smoking gun, tip of the iceberg, and wake-up call. It adds that jargon and cliches should be avoided even in quotes. It also adds a warning against oversimplifying: a writer may use high blood pressure for hypertension but may not call a cardiac arrest a heart attack, because they are different events. The rule is to translate only when sure of the equivalent, and otherwise to ask an expert.

Smaller entries enforce exact meaning. Unique means one of a kind and takes no qualifier such as very, rather, or most. Literally means in an exact sense and is wrong when used figuratively. Affect as a verb means to influence and effect as a verb means to cause; effect as a noun means result. Fewer applies to individual items and less to bulk or amounts, with the stated exception that a period of time takes less. Different takes from, not than. Irregardless is a double negative. Compared to asserts similarity without elaboration, while compared with sets items side by side to show similarities or differences. Claim implies doubt and can suggest the reporter does not believe the source, so said is the better default verb; claim fits only when an assertion is open to question and the story presents the other side. Hopefully is accepted both in the traditional sense and in the modern sense meaning it is hoped, a descriptive concession that most prescriptive guides do not make. More than and over are both acceptable for greater numerical value. Author is a noun and not a verb. The racism entries tell writers to avoid racially charged, racially motivated, and racially tinged because those euphemisms convey little meaning; the entry says to describe the specific words or actions. The corrections section of the news values statement likewise bans euphemisms such as recasts or clarifies when the paper is correcting a factual error: a correction is called a correction. The disabilities entry bans the euphemisms handi-capable, differently abled, and physically challenged. The common thread is that a vague soft word costs the reader information.

On abbreviations the stylebook is firm. Avoid alphabet soup, and do not use any abbreviation or acronym the reader would not quickly recognize. Do not follow an organization's full name with its acronym in parentheses or set off by dashes. If the acronym would not be clear on second reference without that device, do not use it. Names not commonly before the public should not be shrunk to acronyms solely to save a few words.

### Sentences, punctuation, and lists

The comma entry opens with a principle: clarity is the biggest rule, and a comma that does not help make the meaning clear should be omitted, while a comma whose omission could confuse should be kept. In a simple series there is no comma before the conjunction. The book adds a clarity exception: include it when omitting it would make the meaning unclear, when an element of the series itself contains a conjunction, and in a complex series of long phrases. It also notes that rephrasing is often better than relying on the comma. Commas separate independent clauses joined by a conjunction when each clause has its own stated subject, and they are omitted when the subject is shared and not repeated.

The distinction between essential and nonessential clauses and phrases receives its own entries, with the book's preferred names for what other styles call restrictive and nonrestrictive. An essential element is needed to know which thing is meant and takes no commas. A nonessential element adds information, and the sentence would keep its meaning without it, so commas set it off. The book gives the quick test of dropping the clause. It pairs this test with the guidance for that and which: that for essential clauses, which with commas for nonessential ones, with the tip that a clause one can drop without losing meaning takes which. The entry for the conjunction that says to include it when the sentence reads awkwardly without it, to drop it after said when the clause follows immediately, to keep it when a time element intervenes, and, when in doubt, to include it.

Colons: the first word after a colon is capitalized only when it is a proper noun or begins a complete sentence. The dash entry covers the em dash, which the book simply calls the dash. It lists acceptable uses (an abrupt change in thought or an emphatic pause, a series inside a phrase that would otherwise be set off by commas, attribution after a quotation, datelines, lists) and puts a warning in the same breath: avoid overusing dashes to set off phrases when commas would do. Dashes take a space on each side. The separate entry on dashes and hyphens says AP never uses the en dash, uses the hyphen for ranges and compound modifiers, and puts no spaces around hyphens. The parentheses entry is openly hostile: parentheses are jarring to the reader, the temptation to use them is a clue that a sentence has become contorted, and the writer should try to write it another way, with commas or dashes as the usual alternative. Exclamation points are for strong surprise or emotion, and mild interjections get a comma or a period. The ellipsis is for deleting words from quotations, and it never starts or ends a direct quote in running text. The semicolon is for long series with internal commas and for linking independent clauses that lack a conjunction.

Lists get their own entry. The book uses dashes where others use bullets, and says either is acceptable. It requires a space after the marker, an initial capital, and a period at the end of each item. Most relevant for writers, it requires parallel construction: every item should start with the same part of speech, use the same voice, the same verb tense, and the same sentence type, or else every item can be just a phrase. The list is introduced with a short phrase or sentence. The book states no rule about when a list is better than a paragraph, so the skill's preference for paragraphs does not come from here.

### Numbers

The numerals entry spells out one through nine and uses figures for 10 and above. Figures always go with units of measure and ages, in tables, and in statistical and sequential forms. Spell out a number that begins a sentence, except years and numeral-letter combinations. Percentages: since a 2019 change, the book uses the % sign with a figure, writes percentage when no number accompanies it, and spells out both words only if a sentence must begin with a percentage. It requires decimals instead of fractions in percentages and a zero before a decimal under one. It separates percent from percentage points with a worked example: 10% to 13% is 3 percentage points and a 30% increase. Poll results go to whole numbers.

### Inclusive storytelling

The chapter is new in the 56th edition and covers ground that is partly about sourcing practice and partly about vocabulary. The editors present it as a matter of accuracy and fairness, not of etiquette. The chapter on language states that a single word shapes what readers think, and its recommendations are: be as specific as possible rather than using labels (the example is a precise description by age and ethnicity instead of a general phrase, and a number instead of a vague age category); limit the word community for groups of people because it implies that everyone in the group thinks alike; avoid the plural-noun forms such as the homeless, the blind, the mentally ill, and the poor; know the difference between person-first and identity-first language, find out which a person prefers, and mix the two when preferences are unknown; and pause before using disability- or illness-based metaphors such as insane schedules, lame ideas, turning a blind eye, and he must be deaf, since there is nearly always a more specific way to say it. The disabilities entry says to mention a disability only if it is relevant and, in most cases, if a diagnosis has been made or the person uses the term. The ages entry makes the same point about age. The pronoun entries tell the writer to use singular they for people who use it, to avoid the phrase preferred pronouns, and to recast the sentence to avoid a pronoun where that reads smoothly, while keeping clarity paramount and making sure a singular they cannot be mistaken for a plural. The gender-neutral language entry prefers job and role terms that apply to any gender, such as chair, firefighter, police officer, and workforce, while asking for balance with common sense. Its tone toward the task is practical rather than ideological, and the chapter repeatedly pairs a principle with a concrete substitute.

### Data journalism, polls, and surveys

The data chapter's reporting section is the part of the book most useful to an agent that states numbers. Its first rule is that a number alone says little and must be compared with something: a prior year, other places, a benchmark, or a goal, with matching units and collection methods. Per capita or per-10,000 rates allow comparison of groups of unequal size. Rankings should carry raw numbers. Dollar amounts compared across years should be adjusted for inflation. The base population should fit the question. For change, the book explains actual change, percent change, and percentage-point change, and warns that percentage and percent-change comparisons from a small base should be replaced with raw numbers. For averages it explains mean, median, and mode, says to use the median when outliers distort the mean, says to label a median as a median, and says never to average averages. It separates correlation from causation and tells the writer to look for the mechanism and for lurking third factors. Under writing with numbers, it says to round to avoid false precision, to keep no more than about eight to ten digits in a paragraph, to recast numbers as fractions, ratios, or percentages for readability, to add caveats and measures of uncertainty such as margins of error or confidence intervals, and to mention sample sizes, especially small ones. It also says to avoid obscuring analysis with technical terms and to avoid overstating conclusions. The visualization section sets a zero baseline for bar charts and discourages 3D effects and decorative color, which falls outside prose.

The same chapter and the news values statement say that data should be vetted for the method behind it, sample size, collection date, and the availability of other data to check it, and that analyses should be reproducible and documented.

The polls chapter adds numerical language rules. The mere existence of a poll is not news. A poll should disclose its questions, results, and method, come from a source with no stake in the outcome, represent the population, and be timely. Margin of sampling error is expressed in percentage points, not percent, and applies to every response. The book gives a decision rule for describing a gap between two options: more than twice the margin of error means one leads or is larger; between one and two times the margin means it can be called slightly ahead or apparently leading; less than the margin means the race is close or about even. It bans the phrase statistical dead heat, since it is inaccurate when any difference exists. A majority means more than half and a plurality means the largest group short of half. Subgroups under 100 respondents should not be reported, and a huge sample from a text-message or website poll is a warning sign rather than a mark of quality. Sampling error is only the part of total error that can be quantified, so real error is often larger.

### Health and science reporting

The numbers section of the health and science chapter turns on one example: saying patients did better tells the reader little, while saying 60 of 100 survived on the drug against 30 of 100 without it tells them a great deal. It then teaches relative risk against absolute risk with the standard example: a group with a 6 in 100 chance of a disease against a group with a 4 in 100 chance is 50 percent more likely, and readers deserve both views. A drug that extends life by 50 percent can mean six months instead of four. It explains p-values, p-hacking, confidence intervals (a range crossing 1 means the result is not significant, a very wide range is a warning), and the gap between statistical significance and practical significance. It also repeats the percent versus percentage-point distinction with a worked example: high blood pressure falling from 80% to 40% is a 50% decline and a 40-point difference.

The reporting guidance asks for independent experts, reading the actual study instead of the press release, disclosure of who paid for the research, caveats, side effects, and costs whenever benefits are described, and a realistic time to market. The pitfalls section addresses false balance directly. A writer should not give a platform to unqualified claims in the name of balance, and the book gives climate change and smoking as examples where no other side comment is needed. It then adds the counterweight: when a false claim is itself newsworthy, such as a policymaker rejecting mainstream science, the claim must be reported with prominent fact-checking showing that the science says otherwise. It advises quoting patients on their experience and scientists on science, treating celebrity medical advice as nonexpert, avoiding cure for cancer, and being careful with cancer clusters and toxic exposure claims.

### Attribution and sourcing

The attribution entry and the news values statement set the rule that facts the writer did not gather or confirm must be credited, and that anything that could reasonably be disputed should be attributed, with enough detail to show why the source is credible. Quotations are never altered to fix grammar; the writer paraphrases instead. The statement of values asks for a response from anyone portrayed negatively and says to explain in the story what efforts were made when no one could be reached. Anonymous sources are allowed only for vital information and not opinion, and only when it cannot be had otherwise.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The Stylebook is an institutional judgment, not a study. It cites no experiments on reader comprehension, and most of its rules rest on newsroom experience, the preferences of a dictionary (Webster's New World), and the editors' sense of what readers will accept. Its strengths are consistency, long refinement, explicit reasoning in many entries, and transparency about uncertainty. The worked examples in the data, polls, and health chapters are sound statistical practice drawn from specialist contributors, and they are the best-grounded material in the book. Its weaknesses for our purpose are that many rules are conventions chosen for a news wire (the use of the percent sign, the omission of the serial comma, the ban on en dashes) and not truths about clarity. Some guidance is specific to American news reporting, such as titles, datelines, sports, and the legal briefing. The inclusive-language guidance reflects current American newsroom norms and is stated as evolving. The book also contains tensions with itself, for example where it allows dashes while warning that they are overused, and where it allows hopefully but is otherwise strict about usage.

## What the skill applies

**Section 2, Words.** The cliche rule in SKILL.md follows the AP entry's central argument, that a stock phrase tells the reader there is nothing new, and the specific list (game changer, deep dive, low-hanging fruit, perfect storm, silver bullet, wake-up call, paradigm shift, cutting edge) is built from the AP science-writing cliche list, with additions from other sources. The jargon rule ("translate jargon") follows the AP instruction to translate rather than repeat, and the plain-word substitutions (share, not proportion; how common, not prevalence; studies, not literature) are taken directly from the health and science writing tips. The sentence that jargon should never be used to sound expert or to soften something comes from the AP observation that jargon can disguise or editorialize. The skill's note that technical terms are fine for an expert audience tracks the AP concession that jargon has a place in specialized worlds. The exact-sense rules for unique and literally copy the AP entries, including that unique takes no very. The euphemism rule in SKILL.md (people die, get fired, lose money) draws on the AP's insistence on specifics over softeners in its racism, disabilities, and corrections entries; the examples themselves are the skill's own. The abbreviation rule ("don't coin acronyms to save a few words," spell out and use a descriptive phrase if the reader would not recognize it on second reference) is a close paraphrase of the AP's avoid-awkward-constructions guidance. The claim-versus-said reasoning supports Section 4's instruction that "said" is the right verb for attribution.

**Section 3, Sentences.** The rule that a series must stay parallel is reinforced by the AP list entry, which spells out parallelism by part of speech, voice, tense, and sentence type. The rule that punctuation exists to show how parts relate, and that a mark that does not clarify should go, is the comma entry's governing principle applied to all punctuation. The advice to mark asides with commas, colons, parentheses, or a new sentence includes parentheses against the AP's cooler view of them (see Conflicts). The exclamation-point rule (only inside quotations) is stricter than the AP, which permits an exclamation for strong emotion and says to avoid overuse. The AP supplied the idea of avoiding overuse, and the skill goes further. The AP essential-clause logic is not stated in Section 3 but underlies the rule on that and which in Section 8.

**Section 4, Numbers and evidence.** This section draws most heavily on the AP data journalism and health chapters, and the changes in wording are mostly compressions. "Give every number a comparison" mirrors the AP question of compared with what, and its example (a drop to 2 percent from 7 percent) is the skill's own illustration of the AP's own example about 40 percent of male students versus 55 percent of female students. "Give absolute numbers alongside relative ones" and the 6-in-100 versus 4-in-100 illustration come directly from the relative-risk and absolute-risk passage. "Distinguish percent change from percentage-point change" uses the same 10-to-13 example the AP uses in three places (the percent entry, the data chapter, and the health chapter). "Avoid percentages on small bases" is the AP rule in both the data chapter and the news values statement. "Show raw numbers with rankings, and adjust money for inflation" restate two bullets of the data chapter. "Say where facts come from" and the use of said follow the attribution entry and news values. "Put uncertainty where it belongs" is shaped by the AP instruction to include measures of uncertainty and add caveats, though the skill's specific advice to state data plainly and hedge only the interpretation comes from another source. "Don't create false balance" is the AP pitfall, including the second half: when a false claim matters to the story, report it alongside what the evidence shows. The skill rule leaves out the names of the examples.

**Section 5, People and fairness.** The rule to mention race, gender, age, disability, or religion only when relevant is the AP position in its disabilities, age, race, and autism entries. Leading with the person and using the characteristic as an adjective, and avoiding the plural-noun form ("deaf employees," not "the deaf"), come from the AP language list that rejects the homeless, the blind, the mentally ill, and the poor. The skill's choice of a person-first or adjective-first form is a simplification: AP asks writers to find out the preference and to mix person-first and identity-first when unknown, and the skill states the preference to use people's own labels first. The avoidance of metaphors built on disability or illness uses the AP list (insane, lame, blind eye, deaf) and the AP reasoning that a more specific phrase usually exists. Gender-neutral nouns and singular they follow the gender-neutral language and pronouns entries, including the idea of recasting the sentence to avoid the pronoun and the instruction to respect the pronouns a specific person uses.

**Section 8, Mechanics.** The spelling-out of one through nine and the use of figures for 10 and above follows the AP numerals entry; the skill adds that measurements, money, ages, percentages, and data always take figures, again from AP. The serial comma rule does not follow the AP comma entry: the owner chose the serial comma in version 2.0.0, which agrees with Chicago, Oxford, Google, Microsoft, Apple, IBM, and Red Hat and departs from NYT and AP. The colon rule (capitalize after a colon when a complete sentence follows) is the AP rule, narrowed to drop the proper-noun case, which is unlikely to matter. The rule on that and which uses the AP's essential and nonessential logic in its simplest form, minus the AP tip about dropping the clause. The rule on fewer and less repeats the AP entry.

**Section 6, Structure.** The rule that a list needs an introduction and items of the same grammatical shape comes directly from the AP lists entry, including the reference to starting each item with the same part of speech and using one tense and voice. The skill now asks for a complete introductory sentence, where the AP accepts a short phrase.

**Section 10, Revision pass.** Item 10 (bare numbers) restates the data chapter's rules. Item 8 (overused vocabulary) does not draw on AP. Item 1 draws on the AP warning about overusing dashes, although the ban itself is the user's.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out the great majority of the book, because most of it is reference matter for news production. Capitalization of titles and places, state and city abbreviations, datelines, sports scoring, military ranks and units, legal and media-law material, copyright, anonymous-source procedure, corrections procedure, headline rules, the digital security and social media chapters, the religion chapter, the weather and business appendices, and the individual entries on products, companies, and geography have no bearing on how an agent writes a chat reply or a report.

It omits the AP percent convention. The AP uses the % sign with a figure in running text. The skill follows the New York Times and writes the word percent in prose, reserving the symbol for tables and charts (see Conflicts). It omits the AP rule of rounding and many of the finer numeral conventions, such as 10 or more digits in a paragraph as a ceiling. The ceiling of about eight to ten digits is a heuristic from the data chapter, and it appears nowhere in the skill. It may have value for agent reports with many figures and could be added, but it has no evidence behind it beyond the editors' judgment, so it was left out.

The skill omits the poll rules (the margin-of-error decision thresholds, the rule against statistical dead heat, the rules on majority and plurality, the 100-respondent subgroup floor, and the whole-number rounding of poll results) because they apply to a narrow task. They would be worth adding to the skill's Section 4 if agents are routinely asked to summarize surveys, and a future revision could do so. For the same reason it omits the p-value, confidence interval, and statistical-significance guidance, the data visualization rules (zero baselines, area scaling, color), the instruction not to average averages, and the explanation of mean, median, and mode. Those rules are sound, and where an agent writes about data they could matter, but the skill is about prose style and not statistical practice.

The skill does not adopt the AP view that parentheses are jarring and a sign of a contorted sentence. It does not adopt the AP rules on the ellipsis, quotation marks, dialogue, Q and A format, or the rule against altering quotes, although the principle of not altering quotations is broadly sound and nothing in the skill conflicts with it. It omits the AP ban on square brackets, which is a wire-technology artifact. It omits the AP permission of dashes in attribution and datelines because the skill bans dashes outright by the user's choice. It omits the AP acceptance of hopefully, over for more than, and the AP stand on claim versus said beyond the one-line attribution rule. It omits the AP inclusive-storytelling guidance on sourcing and visuals (casting a wide net for sources, avoiding tokenism, accessibility of images, audience diversity) because it concerns newsgathering rather than word choice in a single response. It also omits the AP guidance on specific groups (race and ethnicity terms, immigration vocabulary, religion terms, gender and sexual orientation terms, terrorism, military terms), because the skill's Section 5 sets a general policy and leaves term-by-term decisions to context. Those entries change often, and the book itself says that its language guidance is evolving.

The skill also omits the AP stance on pronouns beyond a one-line summary, the AP preference for dashes over bullets, the AP rule that every list item ends in a period, and the AP rule on capitalizing the first word of each list item. Those rules are formatting conventions for print and do not matter in chat.

## Conflicts with other references

**Dashes (nyt-manual-of-style.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, freeburg-em-dashes.md).** The AP allows the spaced dash for abrupt breaks, for a series inside an aside, and for attribution, while warning against overuse. The NYT also allows the spaced dash with limits, and Chicago uses unspaced em dashes and en dashes for ranges, which AP never uses. The three manuals agree that the dash has uses but disagree on typography. Freeburg's finding that human writing also uses em dashes shows that a heavy ban is not supported by frequency alone. The skill resolves the dispute by following the user's explicit instruction to ban em dashes, which is stricter than every manual. It writes ranges with the word to, which avoids the en dash question entirely and matches the AP hyphen convention only loosely.

**Parentheses (SKILL.md Section 3).** The AP calls parentheses jarring and a symptom of a tangled sentence, and tells writers to prefer commas or dashes. SKILL.md lists parentheses as one of several acceptable ways to mark an aside. With dashes banned, the AP's preferred alternatives shrink to commas, so the skill keeps parentheses available. The tension is small, because the skill's wider rule (use a mark only if it clarifies, or rewrite) agrees with the AP's advice to recast the sentence.

**Percent (nyt-manual-of-style.md).** The AP writes the % sign with figures in text, a change from its earlier rule. The NYT spells out percent in text, which the skill follows because the NYT ranks above the AP in precedence. Chicago also uses the word in nontechnical text and keeps the symbol for scientific copy, as chicago-manual-of-style.md records. The AP's own casual-use rule and its sentence-start rule both spell the word out, so the AP and the skill meet in those cases.

**Serial comma (chicago-manual-of-style.md, nyt-manual-of-style.md).** The AP and NYT omit the comma before the final conjunction in a simple series and keep it where ambiguity would arise. Chicago requires it. The owner chose the serial comma in version 2.0.0, which agrees with Chicago, Oxford, Google, Microsoft, Apple, IBM, and Red Hat and departs from NYT and AP.

**Numbers (chicago-manual-of-style.md).** The AP spells out one through nine and uses figures from 10. Chicago's general rule spells out zero through one hundred, which differs sharply from the AP, though Chicago also offers an alternative rule of zero through nine that matches it (see chicago-manual-of-style.md). The skill follows the NYT and AP, which agree. Both the AP and the skill use figures for measurements, ages, and percentages regardless of size.

**Colons (chicago-manual-of-style.md).** The AP capitalizes the first word after a colon when a complete sentence follows or when the word is a proper noun; the 18th edition of Chicago now does the same, so the three manuals and the skill agree. Older Chicago editions lowercased in this case, which is why the brief for this file listed it as a conflict.

**Voice (reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md, rhodes-active-passive-voice.md).** The AP reviewed here has no entry prescribing active voice in running text, though it requires parallel voice in lists. Its authorship ideal, name the source and attribute facts, is consistent with the skill's accountability rationale for the active voice. Reinhart's finding that models use agentless passives at about half the human rate sits in tension with the skill's active-voice default, and the AP does not resolve it.

**Hedging and uncertainty (durik-hedges-credibility.md).** The AP asks for caveats and measures of uncertainty and says science is rarely definitive, so the skill can state the data plainly and hedge the interpretation. The AP does not draw the data-versus-interpretation distinction that the skill uses, and does not discuss credibility effects. The skill's rule comes from the Durik material, with AP support.

**Ban lists (one-token-away-from-collapse.md, taboo-negative-constraints.md, anthropic-prompting-docs.md).** The AP gives a list of cliches and jargon to avoid, and it names them directly in the entry. The Anthropic documents favor positive instructions, and the two studies found that bans placed in a model's prompt are obeyed but make its answer thinner. The skill resolved this tension by keeping its cliche examples in Section 2 as short illustrations and its longer lists in the revision pass. That arrangement is a partial concession to the AP's practice, and the tension is not fully settled. An earlier version of this entry also cited a claim that naming banned items primes them; that claim was dropped, and the Taboo study found that naming forbidden words cut their use sharply.

**Vocabulary lists (kobak-excess-vocabulary.md, wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md).** The AP's cliche and jargon list is a human-editorial list drawn from journalism, and it has no overlap with the machine-text vocabulary lists in the other references. It does not conflict with them.

**Said versus varied attribution verbs.** The AP names said as the better default and treats claim as a loaded word. The NYT manual leans the same way. No reference in this folder pushes the other direction, so no conflict arises.

In the punctuation chapter I read colon, comma, dash, ellipsis, exclamation point, parentheses, periods, question mark, and semicolon. I also read the entry on dashes and hyphens. I read the inclusive storytelling chapter through its language and bias sections, the data journalism chapter on evaluating data, reproducibility, reporting on data, measures of change, averages, correlation, writing with numbers, and visualization, the polls and surveys chapter through margin of error and the start of the evaluation section, and the health and science chapter sections on numbers, reporting, pitfalls, and writing tips. I read the statement of news values sections on attribution, corrections, data, quotations, and responses.
