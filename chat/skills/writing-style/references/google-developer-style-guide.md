# Google developer documentation style guide

- **Source:** Google, *Google developer documentation style guide*, Google for Developers. A web publication with no edition numbers; Google revises it continuously and logs significant changes on its What's new page, whose latest entry at review time was dated October 16, 2026.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** https://developers.google.com/style (the review used a crawl of 78 pages taken on 2026-11-04)
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-04
- **Used by:** chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose and stance

The guide records the house style for Google's developer documentation, written for software developers and other technical practitioners. Its philosophy page is unusually candid about what it is. It says the guide codifies Google's own decisions, claims no objective correctness, and does not try to be an industry standard, a rival to other manuals or a complete course in basic writing. It explains few of its rules. The stated reasons are that most rules serve the same handful of goals (accessibility, localization, globalization and ease of understanding), that many rules pick one acceptable option among several for consistency's sake, and that readers usually want a short answer more than an argument. The opening page frames everything as guidelines that a writer can depart from when the departure improves the content, provided the writer then stays consistent.

The guide sets its own place in a reference hierarchy. Project-specific style comes first, then this guide, then outside references: Merriam-Webster for spelling, the 17th edition of *The Chicago Manual of Style* for nontechnical questions and the Microsoft Writing Style Guide for technical ones, with a caution that some Microsoft guidance applies only to Microsoft products. It mentions the Apple Style Guide and Red Hat's supplementary guide as further reading outside Google style. It also suggests checking established usage in an organization's own documentation or in a broad corpus such as the Google Ngram Viewer.

### Scope and structure

The crawl covered the guide's main sections. A highlights page condenses the most important rules into a short list: a conversational and friendly tone without frivolity, no pre-announcements, descriptive link text, accessible and globally readable writing, second person, active voice, conditions before instructions, sentence-case headings, numbered lists for sequences, serial commas, code font for code and bold for interface elements. Below that sit pages on general principles (accessibility, excessive claims, future features, global audiences, inclusive language, jargon, prescriptive documentation, third-party content, timeless documentation, voice and tone), on language and grammar (abbreviations, active voice, anthropomorphism, articles, capitalization, contractions, plurals, possessives, prepositions, tense, pronouns, person, sentence structure, verb forms in reference documentation), on punctuation, on formatting and organization (dates, figures, headings, lists, numbers, paragraphs, procedures, tables, units), on linking, on code (API reference comments, code in text, code samples, command-line syntax, placeholders), on interface elements and HTML, and a long word list of several thousand lines. The What's new page logs changes back to at least 2019.

### Voice, tone and register

The guide asks for a voice that is conversational, friendly and respectful, sounding like a knowledgeable friend who understands what the developer is trying to do. It warns writers against copying their speech, since speech is usually looser and wordier than good documentation, and against both extremes of entertainment and dryness. The primary purpose, it says, is to give information to a reader who may be in a hurry. A list of things to avoid includes buzzwords and jargon, cuteness, figurative language, filler phrases such as please note, choppy or long-winded sentences, starting every sentence with the same words, pop-culture references, exclamation marks, phrasing built on let's, words such as simply or easy in a procedure, and internet abbreviations. The tone page closes with a three-column table of examples running from too informal through about right to too formal, which shows by contrast that the target sits close to plain conversational English. A short section on politeness says that please in ordinary instructions overdoes it.

The contractions page follows from this register. It recommends common two-word contractions throughout, and it gives a specific reason for negative ones. A scanning reader can miss a standalone not, while a contracted negative is harder to misread as its positive. It rules out invented contractions and three-word forms.

Exclamation points get a graded rule on the punctuation page. Concept and reference documents never use them, procedures avoid them, blog posts may use them occasionally, and tutorials may use one to mark a milestone. The reasons given are that they can look unprofessional or alarming and translate badly.

### Clarity for a global audience

The longest principle page concerns readers who use translation tools or read English as a second language. Its rules are concrete. Use the simple word: start in place of commence, so in place of consequently, use in place of utilize or leverage, some or many in place of a number of. Write shorter sentences, because translated sentences grow and long ones raise translation cost and review time. Avoid phrasal verbs where a single verb works. Limit noun stacks, with a specific ceiling of two nouns modifying a third. Put modifiers such as only next to the words they modify. Prefer present tense and active voice. Since October 2026 the page also asks writers to limit participles and gerunds, replacing an ambiguous using with by using or that use. Use each word in one sense, and avoid using one word as both noun and verb in close quarters. Keep helper words that conversational English drops (then, that, of, articles) and repeat a word when the repetition removes ambiguity. Clarify pronoun antecedents. Address the reader directly as you, and give context in place of assuming knowledge. Prefer positive constructions where a negative is not needed. Use one term per concept, with the same capitalization each time, because translators and translation memory treat different words as different concepts. Put conditional clauses first. Avoid idioms, slang, humor, seasonal references and culture-specific references.

The article page adds that articles stay in place even in headings, and the pronoun page adds that a demonstrative such as this should usually be followed by a noun. The accessibility page contributes a specific target of fewer than 26 words per sentence, along with rules to put the distinguishing information first in a paragraph, to keep similar structures parallel and to avoid double negatives.

### Precision and claims

Several pages push toward literal, checkable statements. The excessive-claims page defines the problem as a statement about performance or cost that the reader cannot verify, a security statement that a breach would falsify, or a subjective or disparaging remark about another company's product. It tells writers to avoid superlatives such as best, simplest and fastest, and absolutes such as always and never, and to use ensure and guarantee only when something truly is ensured or guaranteed. It asks for a source for any specific performance figure, and it recommends describing what a security feature is designed to do in place of promising what it prevents. Its test looks ahead. A claim must stay true over the life of the document.

The inclusive-language page carries a section on precise, objective language that was generalized in October 2026. It says that words such as simple, easy and quick express the writer's opinion of a task that may be hard for a newcomer; that careful and thorough are vague and imply the reader is otherwise careless; and that proper and properly are unclear and can sound superior. The word list repeats each point in its own entries, and its entry for just calls the word filler that can usually be deleted, with only, instead or previously as replacements when a more specific meaning is intended.

The anthropomorphism page is short. Software and hardware should not be given human qualities, because such language is figurative, less precise and harder to translate. Its examples contrast an object that specifies where to split a string with one that tells a component what to do, and a computer that detects a device with one that sees it.

The prescriptive-documentation page asks writers to recommend a path in place of listing options, and it ties that to the choice of auxiliary verbs. Must marks a required action, can an optional action or a possible outcome, might a possible or uncertain outcome. The guide says to avoid should in general because it leaves the reader unsure whether an action is required or optional. It allows should for recommendations that are widely recognized, such as using a strong password, and suggests we recommend as the usual way to mark advice. It also asks writers to replace a sentence saying a value should be true with whichever of three meanings is intended: a value the reader must set, a value the software sets or a value the reader changes if it is false. The word list reserves may for policy and legal contexts and asks writers to avoid will and would.

### Tense and timelessness

The present-tense page asks for the present when describing general behavior and keeps the future for something that happens later in a sequence, such as an asynchronous message or a file archived on the next backup run. It also rules out the hypothetical would.

The timeless-documentation page asks writers to document the current version of a product without reference to earlier or later versions. It lists the words that anchor text to a moment (as of this writing, currently, does not yet, eventually, existing, in the future, latest, new, now, old, at present, soon) and groups them by why they fail: some disclose plans, some are implied by the document's existence, some date quickly and some assume the reader knew an earlier version. It explicitly allows such words in time-stamped content such as blog posts, press releases and release notes, and it allows soon in a procedure to describe a state change that follows a step. The separate future-features page forbids pre-announcing anything without legal approval.

### Person and voice

The guide asks writers to address the reader as you, to use the imperative for instructions and to use the third person for what software or an end user does. It reserves user for the users of the software the reader builds. It permits we for the organization behind a document when the antecedent is clear, and its word list says not to use let's at all if that can be avoided. The page on pronouns endorses singular they and rules out he/she constructions.

Active voice is the default because passive constructions hide who is supposed to act, which in technical text can be the reader, the server, an end user or a visitor. The page lists three cases where the passive is fine: when the object matters more than the action, when the actor should be played down and when the reader does not need to know who acted.

### Procedures and sentence order

The sentence-structure page states a rule that the guide applies in many places: put the circumstance, condition or goal before the instruction, so a reader to whom it does not apply can skip it. The procedures page builds on it. A procedure is a numbered list, one action per step with small sequential menu choices allowed in one step, introduced by a complete sentence and never by a fragment that the steps complete. Each step starts with an imperative verb. The location of an action comes before the action, and a goal comes before the action unless that phrasing would make a required step look optional, in which case a colon form is used. A result follows its action in the same paragraph. Optional steps begin with a label. The page also asks for the shortest accessible route when several exist, for one method instead of alternatives, for no keyboard shortcuts and no please, and for introductions to commands that say what the command does in place of telling the reader to run the following command.

### Headings, lists, paragraphs and links

Headings use sentence case with no closing period. Task headings start with a bare infinitive and concept headings use a noun phrase; an -ing verb as the first word is discouraged because it translates inconsistently and takes space. Headings should be unique, hierarchical, free of numbers marking sequence, free of links and followed by content.

Lists come in three types: numbered for sequences, bulleted for nonsequential items and description lists for term and definition pairs. A list never holds a single item, except a one-step procedure. It is introduced by a complete sentence, its items share one grammatical structure, and item capitals and end punctuation follow fixed rules. Description lists separate a term from its description with a colon or period and never with a dash. Inline lists use the serial comma and avoid trailing etc.

Paragraphs should each hold one idea, with the main point first. A paragraph longer than five or six sentences is flagged as a likely sign of too much content, though a longer paragraph about a single idea is acceptable.

The linking pages ask for short, unique, descriptive link text that makes sense out of context, either the title of the destination or a phrase describing it. Click here, this document and bare URLs are ruled out, partly because screen-reader users jump from link to link. The pages also ask writers to choose links sparingly, since each one is a decision and an exit for the reader, and to give a short explanation on the page when a few sentences would do.

### Code in text and API comments

Code font marks anything a reader types or reads verbatim, including class and method names, file names and paths, commands, environment variables, literal values and placeholders. The guide's page on the grammar of code elements says not to use a code element as an English noun or verb and not to inflect it. Writers add a plain noun after the element and inflect that noun, so a method gets called, a request gets sent and a file gets named with a following noun.

The API reference page covers documentation comments that generate reference pages. Every public class, member, method, parameter, return value and exception gets a description. The first sentence of a class description states its purpose in terms that the name and signature do not already reveal, and it avoids repeating the class name. Method descriptions use the present tense and start with the verb of the action (Gets, Sets, Checks whether, Creates). Boolean parameters state what happens when true and when false. Deprecation notes name the replacement. The guide does not cover ordinary implementation comments, commit messages or pull requests.

### Inclusive and accessible language

The inclusive-language page treats much of its guidance as a case of avoiding figurative language. It lists ableist terms (crazy, insane, blind to, cripple, dumb, sanity check) and gives literal replacements, rules out gendered terms such as man-hours, asks for person-first language with the caveat that some communities prefer identity-first language, and rules out euphemisms such as differently abled. It also asks writers to avoid socially charged technical terms such as blacklist and native feature, and it gives procedures for writing around terms such as master and slave when they are fixed in code. One item bears directly on this skill. The page asks writers to avoid referring to people as native or non-native English speakers, and to consider whether a document needs to raise the point at all. The jargon page defines jargon as specialized and often figurative group language, including overloaded words such as solution and workload, and gives a decision sequence: write around the term, replace it with a more specific term, or define it at first use.

### Mechanics

On mechanics the guide is mostly conventional American technical style. It uses the serial comma. It spells out zero through nine and uses numerals from 10, with numerals for versions, technical quantities, prices, percentages, measurements and any number below 10 that shares a sentence with a larger one. It writes percentages with the % sign and no space, except at the start of a sentence. It uses the unspaced em dash for a break in a sentence and does not use the en dash at all; ranges take a hyphen. After a colon it lowercases the next word unless that word is a proper noun, a heading, a quotation or text after a label such as Note. It writes that for restrictive clauses and which, after a comma, for nonrestrictive ones. It bans e.g., i.e., via, vice versa and and/or in most contexts, says to use after where once means after and because where since means because, and treats data as a singular mass noun. Its word list replaces allows you to and enables you to with lets you and asks for has in place of with to express ownership.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The guide is institutional judgment, comparable to the news manuals in this folder in kind though narrower in purpose. It cites almost no evidence. The few figures it gives are borrowed or practical: a World Health Organization estimate that about 15 percent of people have an accessibility need, the 26-word sentence target, the five-to-six-sentence paragraph signal and the 4.5:1 contrast ratio. Its claim that inconsistent terminology raises translation costs was softened in July 2026, which suggests the editors recognize that some reasons are plausible more than proven. The strength of the guide lies elsewhere. It is current, it is maintained by working technical editors, its rules are concrete enough to apply mechanically, and almost every rule comes with a recommended and a not-recommended example. Its reasons, where it gives them, are checkable on their face: a reader skipping an inapplicable step, a screen reader jumping between links, a translator facing two names for one thing.

The findings generalize unevenly. The rules on procedures, link text, code font, API comments and tense are specific to technical documentation and transfer well to an agent's instructions, comments and README files. The tone rules assume a company speaking to its customers, and the global-audience rules assume translation pipelines that an agent's chat reply never passes through, though they still help any reader who skims or reads in a second language. The inclusive-language rules overlap heavily with the news manuals.

## What the skill applies

Most of what the skill takes from this guide sits in section 9, which the skill adds for documentation, instructions, code comments, commit messages and pull requests. The bullets below are numbered in the order they appear in that section.

**Section 9, bullet 1 (imperative steps, goal before action).** This bullet joins the procedures page and the sentence-structure page. The imperative, one action per step and a numbered list for ordered steps come from the procedures page. The instruction to put the goal or condition before the action, and the reason that a reader who does not share the goal can skip the step, come from the sentence-structure page almost directly. The skill's example (deleting a cache with a make command) is its own, built on the pattern of Google's example about deleting a document. The skill dropped Google's colon variant for required steps and its rule that the location of an action comes first.

**Section 9, bullet 2 (you, third person, no let's or we).** This bullet condenses the page on second and first person and the word-list entries for let's and we. Google's permission to use we for the authoring organization, with a clear antecedent, became the skill's exception for a named team or organization. The skill did not take Google's narrow sense of user.

**Section 9, bullet 3 (present tense).** This restates the present-tense page, including its exception for future events in a sequence. The skill's example (a server returning an error) follows Google's server and acknowledgment example in structure. The skill did not carry over the separate rule against the hypothetical would.

**Section 9, bullet 4 (must, can, might, should).** This comes from the prescriptive-documentation page and the word-list entries for those verbs. The skill took the three-way mapping of must, can and might to required, optional and possible. On should, the skill tells writers to avoid the word when the reader cannot tell whether it means required or recommended. That matches Google's reasoning (should is ambiguous between the two) and leaves room for Google's own exception for widely recognized recommendations without stating that exception. The skill omits Google's advice to say we recommend, and its reservation of may for legal contexts.

**Section 9, bullet 5 (timeless documentation).** The instruction to describe the version the text covers, without words that date it, paraphrases Google's rule to document the current version and the reason behind its list of time-anchoring words. Google exempts time-stamped content such as release notes and blog posts; the skill extended the exemption to commit messages and changelogs on its own judgment, since those records exist to describe a change.

**Section 9, bullet 6 (claim only what you can show).** This is the excessive-claims page stated as a positive rule. Describing a product or result by what it does follows Google's advice to write factually and its security example, which describes what a feature is designed to do in place of promising an outcome. The requirement to cite a source for performance claims is Google's. Two parts are the skill's own. It widened the scope from descriptions of products to results, so the rule covers an agent reporting its own work, and it added a measurement as an alternative to a source.

**Section 9, bullet 7 (describe steps plainly).** This comes from the precise-language section of the inclusive page and the word-list entries for simple, easy, quick and just. Google's reason is that a task simple for an experienced engineer may be hard for a newcomer. The skill's reason (a reader who struggles with a step labeled easy feels slow) states the cost to that newcomer. The skill did not take Google's objections to careful, thorough or proper.

**Section 9, bullet 8 (anthropomorphism).** This is the anthropomorphism page. The skill's example blends Google's two examples: the delimiter that specifies where to split a string and the computer that detects a device in place of seeing it. The added phrase about deciding is the skill's.

**Section 9, bullet 9 (code formatting, exact names).** The list of things to put in code font summarizes Google's code-in-text table. The clause about using identifiers only as the exact names they are compresses Google's rules on the grammar of code elements: no inflecting, no use as verbs and spelling that matches the code.

**Section 9, bullet 10 (link text).** This is the linking page's rule on descriptive link text, with Google's own example of a phrase to avoid. The skill's positive example is its own.

**Section 9, bullet 11 (headings).** Sentence case, no closing punctuation, a verb for task headings and a noun phrase for concept headings all come from the headings and capitalization pages. The skill's examples are its own. It did not take the rule against -ing words at the start of a heading, though its verb rule produces the same result for task headings.

**Section 9, bullet 12 (readers whose first language is not English).** Keeping that and articles, avoiding idioms and avoiding modifier stacks are three rules from the global-audience page, with articles reinforced by the articles page. The skill says several modifiers where Google sets a ceiling of two nouns before a noun. The bullet's description of its readers follows Google's inclusive page and its word-list entry for native, which both advise against describing people as native or non-native speakers. An earlier draft of the bullet used that phrase.

**Section 9, bullet 13 (code comments).** Google contributes only part of this. Its API comments page asks that the first sentence of a class description give information the name and signature do not, which is the same idea as saying what the code cannot. The rest of the bullet (the reason for a choice, a constraint or a trap, and the ban on restating the next line) does not come from this guide, which does not cover implementation comments.

**Section 9, bullets 14 and 15 (commit messages and pull requests).** Nothing in these bullets comes from this guide, including the later additions on what users will notice and the costs of an improvement, which come from the Linux kernel's patch guide. The only related point is the timeless page's exemption for release notes, which supports the statement in bullet 5 that change records can say what changed.

The guide also reinforces rules in earlier sections that rest mainly on the news manuals.

**Section 1 (open with the answer).** The paragraph-structure page asks for the main point first in each paragraph, and the accessibility page asks for the distinguishing information in the first sentence. Both support the section's rule, which comes from the newspaper model.

**Section 2 (words).** The global-audience page's plain-word substitutions (use for utilize and leverage, start for commence) back the first bullet; the skill's list of pairs is mostly from the news manuals. The rule to call a thing by one name gains Google's translation reason. The jargon page supports the rule to translate jargon, and the abbreviations page supports spelling out an unfamiliar abbreviation on first use. The word-list entries for impact and key agree with the skill's exact-sense bullet, though the skill's wording follows the NYT. The word-list preference for has over with in statements of ownership is one of several supports for the new rule on is, are and has; the rule's examples and its link to revision item 12 are the skill's.

**Section 3 (sentences).** The tone page's warning against starting every sentence the same way matches the rule to vary how sentences begin. The in order to entry matches the padding bullet. The active-voice page, with its reason that passive voice hides who acts, supports the active-voice default, and its listed exceptions line up with the skill's two exceptions. The exclamation-point rule in the punctuation pages agrees with the skill's rule to use them only in quotations, which is stricter. The global page's preference for positive constructions is a mild support for the rule to state claims directly.

**Section 5 (people and fairness).** The inclusive page's ableist-language list supports the rule against metaphors built on disability, and its pronoun guidance supports singular they.

**Section 6 (structure and format).** The lists page's rules on introductory sentences and parallel items support the rule on lists. The guide's ban on one-item lists is consistent with the skill's preference for prose.

**Section 10, items 7, 8 and 13.** The tone page's rejection of filler such as please note fits revision item 7. Google's entry against leverage where use is meant is one of the reasons leverage appears in item 8, alongside the corpus studies. Item 13 (technical overclaiming and dating) holds the three word lists that section 9 draws from this guide: best, fastest, always, never, ensure and guarantee from the excessive-claims page, which also names simplest; simple, easy, quick and just from the precise-language section and word list; and now, new, currently, soon and at present, a selection from the timeless page's longer list. An earlier draft of section 9 named these words in the drafting rules. They moved to the revision pass because naming a word can prime it, the finding in semantic-gravity-wells.md, and because anthropic-prompting-docs.md favors positive instructions. Google's guide works the other way and puts its lists in front of the writer, which suits human editors who look words up; the skill's split suits a model that reads its instructions just before drafting.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out most of the guide, for reasons of scope. Everything about HTML markup, alt text, tables, screenshots, interface elements, bold for button names, placeholders, command-line syntax, code-sample indentation, phone numbers, dates, units, trademarks and product names applies to published web documentation, and an agent's prose rarely needs it. The code-sample rules defer to language style guides, which already govern code.

It leaves out the specific numeric targets: the 26-word sentence limit and the five-to-six-sentence paragraph signal. The skill asks for short and medium sentences and one idea per sentence without a number, and the guide offers no evidence for its figures.

It leaves out the tone guidance that assumes a company talking to customers: the knowledgeable-friend persona, the advice to let personality show and the rules on please. Section 7 sets tone for the skill, and the news-manual model it rests on ranks higher.

It leaves out the ban on humor for global audiences as a general rule. Section 7 permits dry humor in conversation, and the skill applies the global-reader rules only to technical and persisted text in section 9.

It leaves out the word list as a whole. The skill takes a handful of entries that bear on its rules and drops the thousands of spelling, capitalization and product-term entries, which are Google house decisions with no bearing on agent prose. It also does not adopt the bans on e.g., i.e., via and vice versa, or the rules for once and since, though none of them conflicts with anything in the skill.

It leaves out the guide's advice on transitions. The tone page suggests transitions such as though to smooth paragraphs and warns that however can sound stilted. The skill has no rule on transitions beyond deleting additionally and notably at the start of a sentence, a choice that rests on the corpus evidence behind section 10.

It leaves out Google's treatment of contractions, colons, percentages, dashes and the serial comma, because the news manuals settle those points for the skill. The next section covers each.

## Conflicts with other references

Section 9 states that where it disagrees with sections 1 to 8, the earlier section wins, and the user's precedence order puts the New York Times, the AP and Chicago ahead of the technical guides. Every conflict below is resolved by that order.

**Serial comma (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** Google requires the serial comma in every series of three or more, as does Chicago. The NYT and AP omit it in a simple series. Section 8 follows the NYT and adds the comma only where an item contains and or the sentence would otherwise be ambiguous. The skill applies that rule to technical text too.

**Colons (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** This is the sharpest mechanical conflict in the guide. Google lowercases the first word after a colon except for proper nouns, headings, quotations and text after labels. The NYT, AP and the 18th edition of Chicago capitalize the first word when a complete sentence follows, and section 8 does the same. Google's fallback for nontechnical questions is the 17th edition of Chicago, which capitalized in fewer cases, so Google's rule may partly reflect the older edition. The skill keeps its capital.

**Percent (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md).** Google writes figures with the % sign in text, like the AP. The NYT writes the word percent in text and keeps the symbol for tables and charts, and section 8 follows the NYT. Google agrees with both on spelling out percent at the start of a sentence.

**Numbers (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** Google spells out zero through nine, where the NYT and AP rule as the skill states it covers one through nine; the difference is only zero. A larger difference is the mixed series: Google uses numerals for every number in a sentence that contains one of 10 or more, while the NYT, according to its reference file, keeps small numbers spelled out even in a mixed series. Section 8 does not address the mixed case, so a writer following it would spell out the small number. Google's rule that versions and technical quantities always take numerals fits section 8's exception for measurements and data.

**Dashes (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, freeburg-em-dashes.md).** Google uses the unspaced em dash for a break in a sentence, rules out the en dash entirely and writes number ranges with a hyphen. The skill bans the em dash at the user's explicit request and writes ranges with to. Google's guidance does support one part of the skill's revision item 1. It rejects a dash between a term and its description and asks for a colon or period, which is the same substitution the skill asks for in every case.

**Contractions (nyt-manual-of-style.md).** Google recommends contractions throughout its documentation and gives a reason the skill does not answer. A reader scanning quickly may miss a standalone not but will rarely misread a contracted negative. Section 7, which follows the NYT, allows contractions in conversation and spells them out in formal documents, and section 9 cannot override it. A writer of formal technical text under this skill can protect against a missed not by placing the negative early and keeping the sentence short. Google's point is reasonable. The skill resolves the conflict by precedence alone and gives no counterargument.

**Tone and humor.** Google warns against humor in documentation for global readers, while section 7 permits dry humor in conversation. The two rules govern different text. Section 7 covers chat and section 9 covers persisted technical text, where the skill's own rule against idioms points the same way as Google's.

**Headings and lists.** Google encourages headings, lists and short paragraphs to break up walls of text. Section 6 defaults to paragraphs and keeps headers for documents long enough to navigate. In long documentation the two agree; in a chat reply the skill's section 6 governs, since Google writes for scanned reference pages.

**Should (no other file).** Google narrows should. It advises avoiding the word in general and allows it for widely recognized recommendations. Section 9 takes the same position in fewer words, so the two agree.

**Active voice (rhodes-active-passive-voice.md, reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md).** Google's active-voice default rests on accountability, the same reason section 3 gives. Rhodes found no comprehension difference between active and passive voice, and Reinhart found that instruction-tuned models use agentless passives at about half the human rate. Neither result touches Google's argument, which concerns whether the reader can tell who acts. Google's three exceptions for the passive fit inside the skill's two.

**Other technical guides.** Google names the Microsoft Writing Style Guide as its fallback for technical questions, which puts Microsoft second to Google in Google's own hierarchy. The skill ranks the technical guides together below the news manuals and does not order them among themselves. Reference files for Microsoft, Apple, git and the Linux kernel were not in this folder at review time, so this file does not compare Google with them in detail. Google says nothing about commit-message subject lines, so it plays no part in the subject-length question that git, the kernel and AGENTS.md address.

## Review notes

I worked from a local text crawl of the guide taken on 2026-11-04: 78 pages, each marked with its title and URL.
