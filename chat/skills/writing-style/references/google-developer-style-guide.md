# Google developer documentation style guide

- **Source:** Google, *Google developer documentation style guide*, Google for Developers. A web publication with no edition numbers; Google revises it continuously and logs major changes on its What's new page, whose latest entry at review time was dated October 16, 2026.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** https://developers.google.com/style (the review used a crawl of 78 pages taken on November 4, 2026)
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

### Purpose and stance

The guide records the house style for Google's developer documentation, written for software developers and other technical practitioners. Its philosophy page is unusually candid about what it is. It says the guide codifies Google's own decisions, claims no objective correctness, and does not try to be an industry standard, a rival to other manuals, or a complete course in basic writing. It explains few of its rules. The stated reasons are threefold. Most rules serve the same few goals: accessibility, localization, globalization, and ease of understanding. Many rules pick one acceptable option among several for consistency's sake. And readers usually want a short answer more than an argument. The opening page frames everything as guidelines that a writer can depart from when the departure improves the content, provided the writer then stays consistent.

The guide sets its own place in a reference hierarchy. Project-specific style comes first, then this guide, then outside references. Those are Merriam-Webster for spelling, the 17th edition of *The Chicago Manual of Style* for nontechnical questions, and the Microsoft Writing Style Guide for technical ones. The guide cautions that some Microsoft guidance applies only to Microsoft products. It mentions the Apple Style Guide and Red Hat's supplementary guide as further reading outside Google style. It also suggests checking established usage in an organization's own documentation or in a broad corpus such as the Google Ngram Viewer.

### Scope and structure

The crawl covered the guide's main sections. A highlights page gives the most important rules in a short list. They call for a conversational and friendly tone without frivolity and for no pre-announcements. They also call for descriptive link text, accessible and globally readable writing, second person, and active voice. Conditions come before instructions, headings use sentence case, and sequences get numbered lists. The list ends with serial commas, code font for code, and bold for interface elements.

Below that are pages on several groups of topics. General principles cover accessibility, excessive claims, future features, global audiences, inclusive language, jargon, prescriptive documentation, third-party content, timeless documentation, and voice and tone. Language and grammar cover abbreviations, active voice, anthropomorphism, articles, capitalization, contractions, plurals, possessives, prepositions, tense, pronouns, person, sentence structure, and verb forms in reference documentation. Formatting and organization cover dates, figures, headings, lists, numbers, paragraphs, procedures, tables, and units. Code covers API reference comments, code in text, code samples, command-line syntax, and placeholders. Separate pages cover punctuation, linking, interface elements, and HTML. A long word list runs to several thousand lines. The What's new page logs changes back to at least 2019.

### Voice, tone, and register

The guide asks for a voice that is conversational, friendly, and respectful: the voice of a knowledgeable friend who understands what the developer is trying to do. It warns writers against copying their speech, since speech is usually looser and wordier than good documentation, and against both extremes of entertainment and dryness. The primary purpose, it says, is to give information to a reader who may be in a hurry. The guide also lists things to avoid: buzzwords and jargon, cuteness, figurative language, filler phrases such as `please note`, and choppy or long-winded sentences. The list adds starting every sentence with the same words, pop-culture references, exclamation marks, phrasing built on let's, words such as simply or easy in a procedure, and internet abbreviations. The tone page closes with a three-column table of examples running from too informal through about right to too formal, which shows by contrast that the target sits close to plain conversational English. A short section on politeness says that `please` in ordinary instructions overdoes it.

The contractions page follows from this register. It recommends common two-word contractions throughout, and it gives a specific reason for negative ones. A scanning reader can miss a standalone not, while a contracted negative is harder to misread as its positive. It rules out invented contractions and three-word forms.

Exclamation points get a graded rule on the punctuation page. Concept and reference documents never use them, procedures avoid them, blog posts may use them occasionally, and tutorials may use one to mark a milestone. The reasons given are that they can look unprofessional or alarming and translate badly.

### Clarity for a global audience

The longest principle page concerns readers who use translation tools or read English as a second language. Its rules are concrete. Use the simple word: start in place of commence, so in place of consequently, use in place of `utilize` or `leverage`, some or many in place of `a number of`. Write shorter sentences, because translated sentences grow and long ones raise translation cost and review time. Avoid phrasal verbs where a single verb works. Limit noun stacks, with a specific ceiling of two nouns modifying a third. Put modifiers such as only next to the words they modify. Prefer present tense and active voice. Since October 2026 the page also asks writers to limit participles and gerunds and to replace an ambiguous using with by using or that use. Use each word in one sense, and avoid using one word as both noun and verb in close quarters. Keep helper words that conversational English drops (then, that, of, articles) and repeat a word when the repetition removes ambiguity. Clarify pronoun antecedents. Address the reader directly as you, and give context in place of assuming knowledge. Prefer positive constructions where a negative is not needed. Use one term per concept, with the same capitalization each time, because translators and translation memory treat different words as different concepts. Put conditional clauses first. Avoid idioms, slang, humor, seasonal references, and culture-specific references.

The article page adds that articles stay in place even in headings, and the pronoun page adds that a demonstrative such as this should usually be followed by a noun. The accessibility page contributes a specific target of fewer than 26 words per sentence, along with rules to put the distinguishing information first in a paragraph, to keep similar structures parallel, and to avoid double negatives.

### Precision and claims

Several pages push toward literal, checkable statements. The excessive-claims page defines the problem as a statement about performance or cost that the reader cannot verify, a security statement that a breach would falsify, or a subjective or disparaging remark about another company's product. It tells writers to avoid superlatives such as best, simplest, and fastest, and absolutes such as always and never, and to use ensure and guarantee only when something truly is ensured or guaranteed. It asks for a source for any specific performance figure, and it recommends describing what a security feature is designed to do in place of promising what it prevents. Its test looks ahead. A claim must stay true over the life of the document.

The inclusive-language page carries a section on precise, objective language that was generalized in October 2026. It says that words such as simple, easy, and quick express the writer's opinion of a task that may be hard for a newcomer. It says careful and thorough are vague and imply the reader is otherwise careless. And it says proper and properly are unclear and can sound superior. The word list repeats each point in its own entries, and its entry for just calls the word filler that can usually be deleted, with only, instead, or previously as replacements when a more specific meaning is intended.

The anthropomorphism page is short. Software and hardware should not be given human qualities, because such language is figurative, less precise, and harder to translate. Its examples contrast an object that specifies where to split a string with one that tells a component what to do, and a computer that detects a device with one that sees it.

The prescriptive-documentation page asks writers to recommend a path in place of listing options, and it ties that to the choice of auxiliary verbs. Must marks a required action, can an optional action or a possible outcome, might a possible or uncertain outcome. The guide says to avoid should in general because it leaves the reader unsure whether an action is required or optional. It allows should for recommendations that are widely recognized, such as using a strong password, and suggests we recommend as the usual way to mark advice. It also asks writers to replace a sentence saying a value should be true with whichever of three meanings is intended. The value may be one the reader must set, one the software sets, or one the reader changes if it is false. The word list reserves may for policy and legal contexts and asks writers to avoid will and would.

### Tense and timelessness

The present-tense page asks for the present when describing general behavior and keeps the future for something that happens later in a sequence, such as an asynchronous message or a file archived on the next backup run. It also rules out the hypothetical would.

The timeless-documentation page asks writers to document the current version of a product without reference to earlier or later versions. It lists the words that tie text to a moment: as of this writing, currently, does not yet, eventually, existing, in the future, latest, new, now, old, at present, and soon. It groups them by why they fail. Some disclose plans, some are implied by the document's existence, some date quickly, and some assume the reader knew an earlier version. It explicitly allows such words in time-stamped content such as blog posts, press releases, and release notes, and it allows soon in a procedure to describe a state change that follows a step. The separate future-features page forbids pre-announcing anything without legal approval.

### Person and voice

The guide asks writers to address the reader as you, to use the imperative for instructions, and to use the third person for what software or a user does. It reserves user for the users of the software the reader builds. It permits we for the organization behind a document when the antecedent is clear, and its word list says not to use let's at all if that can be avoided. The page on pronouns endorses singular they and rules out pairing he and she with a slash.

Active voice is the default because passive constructions hide who is supposed to act, which in technical text can be the reader, the server, a user, or a visitor. The page lists three cases where the passive is fine: when the object matters more than the action, when the actor should be played down, and when the reader does not need to know who acted.

### Procedures and sentence order

The sentence-structure page states a rule that the guide applies in many places: put the circumstance, condition, or goal before the instruction, so a reader to whom it does not apply can skip it. The procedures page builds on it. A procedure is a numbered list, one action per step with small sequential menu choices allowed in one step, introduced by a complete sentence and never by a fragment that the steps complete. Each step starts with an imperative verb. The location of an action comes before the action, and a goal comes before the action unless that phrasing would make a required step look optional, in which case a colon form is used. A result follows its action in the same paragraph. Optional steps begin with a label. The page also asks for the shortest accessible route when several exist and for one method instead of alternatives. It asks for no keyboard shortcuts and no `please`. And it wants introductions to commands that say what the command does in place of telling the reader to run the following command.

### Headings, lists, paragraphs, and links

Headings use sentence case with no closing period. Task headings start with a bare infinitive and concept headings use a noun phrase; an -ing verb as the first word is discouraged because it translates inconsistently and takes space. Headings should be unique, hierarchical, free of numbers marking sequence, free of links, and followed by content.

Lists come in three types: numbered for sequences, bulleted for nonsequential items, and description lists for term and definition pairs. A list never holds a single item, except a one-step procedure. It is introduced by a complete sentence, its items share one grammatical structure, and item capitals and end punctuation follow fixed rules. Description lists separate a term from its description with a colon or period and never with a dash. Inline lists use the serial comma and avoid trailing `etc.`

Paragraphs should each hold one idea, with the main point first. A paragraph longer than five or six sentences is flagged as a likely sign of too much content, though a longer paragraph about a single idea is acceptable.

The linking pages ask for short, unique, descriptive link text that makes sense out of context, either the title of the destination or a phrase describing it. Click here, this document, and bare URLs are ruled out, partly because screen-reader users jump from link to link. The pages also ask writers to choose links sparingly, since each one is a decision and an exit for the reader, and to give a short explanation on the page when a few sentences would do.

### Code in text and reference comments

Code font marks anything a reader types or reads verbatim, including class and method names, file names and paths, commands, environment variables, literal values, and placeholders. The guide's page on the grammar of code elements says not to use a code element as an English noun or verb and not to inflect it. Writers add a plain noun after the element and inflect that noun, so a method gets called, a request gets sent, and a file gets named with a following noun.

The API reference page covers documentation comments that generate reference pages. Every public class, member, method, parameter, return value, and exception gets a description. The first sentence of a class description states its purpose in terms that the name and signature do not already reveal, and it avoids repeating the class name. Method descriptions use the present tense and start with the verb of the action (Gets, Sets, Checks whether, Creates). Boolean parameters state what happens when true and when false. Deprecation notes name the replacement. The guide does not cover ordinary implementation comments, commit messages, or pull requests.

### Inclusive and accessible language

The inclusive-language page treats much of its guidance as a case of avoiding figurative language. It lists ableist terms (crazy, insane, blind to, cripple, dumb, `sanity check`) and gives literal replacements. It rules out gendered terms such as man-hours and euphemisms such as differently abled. It asks for person-first language, with the caveat that some communities prefer identity-first language. It also asks writers to avoid socially charged technical terms such as `blacklist` and native feature, and it gives procedures for writing around terms such as `master` and `slave` when they are fixed in code. One item bears directly on this skill. The page asks writers to avoid referring to people as native or non-native English speakers, and to consider whether a document needs to raise the point at all. The jargon page defines jargon as specialized and often figurative group language, including overloaded words such as solution and workload. It gives a decision sequence: write around the term, replace it with a more specific term, or define it at first use.

### Mechanics

On mechanics the guide is mostly conventional American technical style. It uses the serial comma. It spells out zero through nine and uses numerals from 10, with numerals for versions, technical quantities, prices, percentages, measurements, and any number below 10 that shares a sentence with a larger one. It writes percentages with the % sign and no space, except at the start of a sentence. It uses the unspaced em dash for a break in a sentence and does not use the en dash at all; ranges take a hyphen. After a colon it lowercases the next word unless that word is a proper noun, a heading, a quotation, or text after a label such as Note. It writes that for restrictive clauses and which, after a comma, for nonrestrictive ones. It bans `e.g.`, `i.e.`, via, vice versa, and `and/or` in most contexts, says to use after where once means after and because where since means because, and treats data as a singular mass noun. Its word list replaces allows you to and enables you to with lets you and asks for has in place of with to express ownership.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The guide is institutional judgment, comparable to the news manuals in this folder in kind though narrower in purpose. It cites almost no evidence. The few figures it gives are borrowed or practical. They are a World Health Organization estimate that about 15 percent of people have an accessibility need, the 26-word sentence target, the five-to-six-sentence paragraph signal, and the 4.5:1 contrast ratio. Its claim that inconsistent terminology raises translation costs was softened in July 2026, which suggests the editors recognize that some reasons are plausible more than proven. The strength of the guide lies elsewhere. It is current, it is maintained by working technical editors, its rules are concrete enough to apply mechanically, and almost every rule `comes with` a recommended and a not-recommended example. Its reasons, where it gives them, are checkable on their face: a reader skipping an inapplicable step, a screen reader jumping between links, a translator facing two names for one thing.

The findings generalize unevenly. The rules on procedures, link text, code font, API comments, and tense are specific to technical documentation and transfer well to an agent's instructions, comments, and README files. The tone rules assume a company speaking to its customers. The global-audience rules assume translation pipelines that an agent's chat reply never passes through, though they still help any reader who skims or reads in a second language. The inclusive-language rules overlap heavily with the news manuals.

## What the skill applies

Google's guide was the main source for the technical rules that sat in the skill's section 9 before version 2.0.0. Those rules, together with the old revision item 13, moved to the technical-writing skill. That skill's copy of this file maps them. Section 9 of writing-style now only points to technical-writing. What remains here is the guide's support for sections 1 to 8, the revision pass, and the examples, most of which rest mainly on the news manuals, Chicago, the Oxford references, and the IBM guide.

**Section 1 (open with the answer).** The paragraph-structure page asks for the main point first in each paragraph, and the accessibility page asks for the distinguishing information in the first sentence. Both support the section's rule, which comes from the newspaper model.

**Section 2 (words).** The global-audience page's plain-word substitutions (use for `utilize` and `leverage`, start for commence) back the first bullet; the skill's list of pairs comes mostly from the news manuals and the IBM guide. The rule to call a thing by one name gains Google's translation reason. The jargon page supports the rule to translate jargon, and the abbreviations page supports spelling out an unfamiliar abbreviation on first use. The word-list entries for impact and key agree with the exact-sense bullet, though the skill's wording follows the NYT. Google's ceiling of two nouns before a noun is stricter than the noun-stack bullet, which allows three. Its rules for once and since agree with the bullet that gives connecting words one job each. Its bans on `e.g.`, `i.e.`, and `and/or` agree with the bullets on Latin abbreviations and slashes, which ibm-style-guide.md traces mainly to IBM; Google bans the abbreviations in most contexts, and the skill keeps `e.g.` and `i.e.` for parentheses and tables. The word-list preference for has over with in statements of ownership is one of several supports for the rule on is, are, and has; the rule's examples and its link to revision item 12 are the skill's.

**Section 3 (sentences).** The tone page's warning against starting every sentence the same way matches the rule to vary how sentences begin. The pronoun page's advice to follow a demonstrative such as this with a noun agrees with the bullet on this, that, these, and those. The global-audience page asks writers to put only next to the word it modifies and to replace an ambiguous using with by using or that use, which agrees with the bullet on opening phrases. The file ibm-style-guide.md records IBM as the source of the by-using distinction. The same page's request to keep that and articles agrees with the bullet on keeping articles and that, and its preference for positive constructions supports the bullet on stating conditions positively. Before version 2.0.0 the rules on that and articles sat in section 9's bullet on readers whose first language is not English; the rest of that bullet, on idioms, moved to technical-writing. The active-voice page, with its reason that passive voice hides who acts, supports the active-voice default, and its listed exceptions line up with the skill's two exceptions. The exclamation-point rule in the punctuation pages agrees with the skill's rule to use them only in quotations, which is stricter.

**Section 5 (people and fairness).** The inclusive page's ableist-language list supports the rule against metaphors built on disability, and its pronoun guidance supports singular they.

**Section 6 (structure and format).** The lists page asks for a complete sentence to introduce a list and for items that share one grammatical structure, which agrees with the list bullets; ibm-style-guide.md records IBM as their main source. The guide's ban on one-item lists is consistent with the skill's preference for prose. The headings page's rule of sentence case with no closing period agrees with the header bullet. Google's verb and noun-phrase patterns for task and concept headings moved to technical-writing with the rest of the old section 9.

**Section 10 (revision pass).** The tone page's rejection of filler such as `please note` fits item 7. Google's entry against `leverage` where use is meant is one of the reasons `leverage` appears in item 8, alongside the corpus studies. Google's October 2026 request to limit participles and gerunds, made for translation, points the same way as item 3 on trailing participles. Several slips in item 13 are ones Google also rules on: a demonstrative with no noun, `e.g.` and `i.e.` in running text, `and/or`, and a series without its serial comma.

The old item 13 (technical overclaiming and dating) held three word lists drawn from this guide. It moved to technical-writing as that skill's first revision item, and technical-writing's copy of this file covers it, including the reason the lists sit in a revision pass and not in the drafting rules.

**Examples.** The describing-a-tool example removes `e.g.`, `etc.`, and an allowing construction, all of which Google's word list discourages.

## What the skill omits

The technical rules this guide supplied (procedures, person, tense, modal verbs, timeless documentation, claims, anthropomorphism, code formatting, link text, task and concept headings, idioms for global readers, and API comments) are no longer in this skill. They moved to the technical-writing skill, whose copy of this file covers them and what that skill leaves out.

The skill leaves out much of the rest of the guide for reasons of scope. HTML markup, alt text, tables, screenshots, interface elements, bold for button names, placeholders, command-line syntax, code-sample indentation, contact numbers, and units apply to published web documentation, and an agent's prose rarely needs them. The code-sample rules defer to language style guides, which already govern code.

It leaves out the five-to-six-sentence paragraph signal. Google's 26-word sentence target is close to section 3's request to keep most sentences under about 25 words. The skill's figure follows the IBM guide's threshold, as ibm-style-guide.md records, and the 40-word point for splitting a sentence is the owner's. The guide offers no evidence for its figures.

It leaves out the tone guidance that assumes a company talking to customers: the knowledgeable-friend persona, the advice to let personality show, and the rules on `please`. Section 7 sets tone for the skill on the model of a good newspaper, which writes for a reader and not for a customer.

It leaves out the ban on humor for global audiences as a general rule. Section 7 permits dry humor in conversation, and the rules for global readers now sit in technical-writing's section 6, which applies only to technical and persisted text.

It leaves out the word list as a whole. The skill takes a handful of entries that bear on its rules and drops the thousands of spelling, capitalization, and product-term entries, which are Google house decisions with no bearing on agent prose. It does not adopt the bans on via and vice versa; section 8 writes vice versa in roman type as an ordinary borrowed term.

It leaves out the guide's advice on transitions. The tone page suggests transitions such as though to smooth paragraphs and warns that however can sound stilted. The skill has no rule on transitions beyond deleting `additionally` and `notably` at the start of a sentence, a choice that rests on the corpus evidence behind section 10.

It leaves out Google's treatment of contractions, colons, percentages, numbers, and dashes, because the news manuals or the owner's choices settle those points. The next section covers each.

## Conflicts with other references

Each entry below records the form the skill chose and the reason for it. The owner's explicit choices (no em dashes, the serial comma, and the revision-pass bans) override every source.

**Serial comma (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** Google requires the serial comma in every series of three or more, as Chicago does. The NYT and AP omit it in a simple series. Earlier versions of section 8 followed the NYT and added the comma only where an item contained and or the sentence would otherwise be ambiguous. Version 2.0.0 adopted the serial comma by the owner's choice, so Google and the skill now agree, and the remaining conflict is between the skill and the NYT and AP.

**Colons (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** The sharpest mechanical conflict in the guide concerns colons. Google lowercases the first word after a colon except for proper nouns, headings, quotations, and text after labels. The NYT, AP, and the 18th edition of Chicago capitalize the first word when a complete sentence follows, and section 8 does the same. Google's fallback for nontechnical questions is the 17th edition of Chicago, which capitalized in fewer cases, so Google's rule may partly reflect the older edition. The skill keeps its capital.

**Percent (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md).** Google writes figures with the % sign in text, like the AP. The NYT writes the word percent in text and keeps the symbol for tables and charts, and section 8 follows the NYT. Google agrees with both on spelling out percent at the start of a sentence.

**Numbers (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md).** Google spells out zero through nine, where the NYT and AP rule as the skill states it covers one through nine; the difference is only zero. A larger difference is the mixed series. Google uses numerals for every number in a sentence that contains one of 10 or more. The NYT, according to its reference file, keeps small numbers spelled out even in a mixed series. Section 8 does not address the mixed case, so a writer following it would spell out the small number. Google's rule that versions and technical quantities always take numerals fits section 8's exception for measurements and data.

**Dashes (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, freeburg-em-dashes.md).** Google uses the unspaced em dash for a break in a sentence, rules out the en dash entirely, and writes number ranges with a hyphen. The skill bans the em dash at the owner's explicit request and writes ranges with to. Google's guidance does support one part of the skill's revision item 1. It rejects a dash between a term and its description and asks for a colon or period, which is the same substitution the skill asks for in every case.

**Contractions (nyt-manual-of-style.md).** Google recommends contractions throughout its documentation and gives a reason the skill does not answer. A reader scanning quickly may miss a standalone not but will rarely misread a contracted negative. Section 7, which follows the NYT, allows contractions in conversation and spells them out in formal documents, and technical-writing sets no rule of its own on contractions. A writer of formal technical text under this skill can protect against a missed not by placing the negative early and keeping the sentence short. Google's point is reasonable. The skill keeps the NYT's split because it ties register to the setting, with spelled-out forms where text is formal or likely to be translated, but it gives no counterargument to Google's point about scanning.

**Lets you (ibm-style-guide.md).** Google's word list replaces allows you to and enables you to with lets you. Section 2 and revision item 12 treat lets you like the other two. They ask for the reader as the subject, as in "You can export the report as CSV." The skill's rule follows the IBM guide's entries for allow, enable, let, and permit. The file ibm-style-guide.md records them. Google's fix removes the inflated verb but keeps the step between the reader and the action. The skill removes both.

**Vice versa.** Google bans vice versa in most contexts. Section 8 lists it among the borrowed terms written in roman type, so the skill allows it. The skill follows its own section 8 here.

**Tone and humor.** Google warns against humor in documentation for global readers, while section 7 permits dry humor in conversation. The two rules govern different text. Section 7 covers chat, and technical-writing's section 6 covers technical text, where its rule against idioms points the same way as Google's.

**Headings and lists.** Google encourages headings, lists, and short paragraphs to break up walls of text. Section 6 defaults to paragraphs and keeps headers for documents long enough to need them. In long documentation the two agree; in a chat reply the skill's section 6 governs, since Google writes for scanned reference pages.

**Should.** An earlier version of this file recorded Google's narrowed use of should as an agreement with section 9. The modal-verb rule moved to technical-writing, and that skill's copy of this file covers it.

**Active voice (rhodes-active-passive-voice.md, reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md).** Google's active-voice default rests on accountability, the same reason section 3 gives. Rhodes found no comprehension difference between active and passive voice, and Reinhart found that instruction-tuned models use agentless passives at about half the human rate. Neither result touches Google's argument, which concerns whether the reader can tell who acts. Google's three exceptions for the passive fit inside the skill's two.

**Other technical guides.** Google names the Microsoft Writing Style Guide as its fallback for technical questions, which puts Microsoft second to Google in Google's own hierarchy. The skill sets no order among the technical guides; each entry in this file records the choice it made. Google says nothing about commit-message subject lines, and the commit rules now belong to technical-writing, whose references compare git, the kernel, and AGENTS.md.
