# Oppenheimer on the cost of needlessly long words

- **Source:** Oppenheimer, Daniel M. "Consequences of Erudite Vernacular Utilized Irrespective of Necessity: Problems with Using Long Words Needlessly." *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 20(2), 139-156. Published online October 31, 2005; print issue 2006. John Wiley & Sons.
- **Type:** peer-reviewed journal article
- **Where to find it:** DOI `10.1002/acp.1178`. A PDF of the article is hosted on the site of a New Zealand plain-language consultancy (frankgroup.co.nz); the publisher page was not used.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

### Purpose and framing

Style guides agree that writers should use simple words, and Oppenheimer opens by noting that the advice appears in the standard manuals and in the submission guidelines of psychology journals. He then points out that actual student behavior runs the other way. He polled 110 Stanford undergraduates. About 86 percent said they had at some point swapped words in an academic essay for more complicated ones so the essay would sound more valid or intelligent. Nearly two thirds said they reach for a thesaurus for that purpose. The paper asks whether the habit works.

He gives reasons it might. Vocabulary size correlates with intelligence, so big words could signal a bright author. Readers assume writers follow a convention of clarity, so a hard text might be read as a hard idea that resisted simplification. Readers who struggle through a text may also justify the effort by deciding the content is worth it. He cites earlier work showing that the share of long words in students' class assignments correlated with their SAT scores and exam grades, and observes that such work never asked how readers perceived those authors. The competing prediction comes from processing fluency research: text that is easy to read tends to be judged more true, more familiar, more confident, and more likable, and the effect is stronger when the ease is surprising. If so, unnecessary complexity should cost the author.

### Method in brief

The paper reports five experiments, all with Stanford University undergraduates who took part for course credit or, in one case, were recruited outside dining halls. Every experiment is a between-subjects design in which each participant read a single short passage and gave one or two ratings on seven-point scales. Participants also rated how hard the passage was to understand. Oppenheimer uses that rating as his measure of fluency and as a check that the manipulation changed difficulty. Complexity is operationalized as word length, which he defends as a crude but replicable proxy that leaves out grammatical complexity and word rarity.

### Experiment 1 on inflating graduate application essays

Seventy-one students each read an excerpt (138 to 253 words) from one of six graduate personal statements in English literature, collected from writing-help websites. The statements differed widely in content and quality. Each excerpt came in three versions. The original was unchanged. In the highly complex version every noun, verb, and adjective was replaced with its longest synonym in the Microsoft Word 2000 thesaurus, where a synonym of the same sense existed. The moderately complex version replaced every third eligible word. Participants were told the excerpt came from an application to the Stanford English department, decided whether to accept the applicant, and rated their confidence. Oppenheimer combined the two into a scale running from negative seven to positive seven.

Difficulty ratings rose with complexity, with means of about 2.9 for originals, 4.0 for moderate, and 4.3 for high. Admission ratings fell in the same order. Originals averaged about two thirds of a point on the signed scale, the moderate version about 0.17, and the highly complex version around negative two. The minus signs did not survive in the text I extracted, so the sign of the lowest mean is inferred from the reported direction of the effect. A footnote says the moderate condition did not differ reliably from either of the others, so the clear contrast is between original and highly complex. Excerpt quality varied reliably, but there was no interaction between quality and complexity: inflation did not rescue weak essays or help strong ones. A mediation analysis found that difficulty of comprehension accounted for most of the link between complexity and rejection, and the Sobel test for that mediation passed the conventional threshold.

### Experiment 2 on two translations of Descartes

Complexity by thesaurus substitution raises the objection that the substitutions may have produced awkward or misused words. For Experiment 2 Oppenheimer sought natural stimuli instead: two published English translations of the first paragraph of Descartes' fourth Meditation, one of 98 words judged more complex by two raters, one of 82 words judged simpler. Thirty-nine students read one version and rated the author's intelligence. Half were told the text was by Descartes and half were told the author was anonymous, which tested whether a prior belief in the author's brilliance would turn complexity into a plus.

Both factors had effects. Attribution to Descartes raised ratings substantially (means around 6.5 for the simple version and 5.6 for the complex version, against 4.7 and 4.0 for the anonymous versions). Within each attribution the simpler translation was rated higher by roughly 0.7 to 0.9 points, and the two factors did not interact. The mediation pattern pointed the expected way but did not pass the conventional threshold on the Sobel test, which Oppenheimer concedes while arguing that the test is known to be conservative. He also acknowledges the obvious confound that one translator may simply be more skilled than the other.

### Experiment 3 on simplifying a dissertation abstract

To address that confound and the worry that any algorithmic edit degrades text, Experiment 3 ran the Experiment 1 procedure in reverse. From 25 sociology dissertation abstracts at Stanford, Oppenheimer picked the one with the highest share of words of nine letters or longer and took its first 144 words. In the simplified version every word of nine or more letters was replaced by the second-shortest synonym in the thesaurus. Eighty-five students (35 in a lab session, 50 recruited outside dining halls) rated the author's intelligence. The simplified abstract was rated as less complex (4.9 against 5.6) and its author as more intelligent (4.80 against 4.26). The difference is modest, with an effect size near 0.44, and the mediation test again missed the conventional threshold. The result matters because an automated edit that makes text better, on this account, undercuts the claim that the earlier penalty came from clumsy substitution.

### Experiment 4 on a hard-to-read font

Because the first three experiments only measure fluency, Experiment 4 manipulates it directly. Fifty-one students read the best essay from Experiment 1, either in plain Times New Roman or in an italic decorative typeface (Juice ITC), and rated the author's intelligence. Instructions and rating scales appeared in the same typeface so participants would blame the experimenter rather than the author for the font. Brief interviews with five participants supported that. The hard-to-read version received lower intelligence ratings (4.04 against 4.50), with a one-tailed test and an effect size near 0.48. So reduced fluency alone, with no change in wording, lowers perceived intelligence.

### Experiment 5 on discounting when the cause of difficulty is obvious

Fluency theory predicts that when readers can see an irrelevant cause of low fluency, they will correct for it and may overcorrect. Twenty-seven students read an essay from Experiment 1 printed either normally or on a printer running low on toner, which gives streaky, faint text with an evident cause. The low-toner group rated the author as more intelligent (5.0 against 4.0) and was more inclined to accept the applicant. Effect sizes were large, near 0.9 and 1.1, on a very small sample. Oppenheimer reads this as evidence that fluency is the operative mechanism, because a mood explanation would predict lower ratings in the low-toner group.

### Conclusions and limits stated by the author

Oppenheimer concludes that needless complexity lowers judgments of the author, that this applies across genres (personal statements, abstracts, philosophy), across judgments (admission and intelligence), across essay quality and prior belief, and that reduced fluency is at least partly responsible. He is open about limits. The participants are well-educated, motivated Stanford undergraduates who could decode the long words. Experts reading their own field's jargon might respond differently, and plain writing might even signal an outsider. Only written text was studied. A long word that is the most precise or most concise choice may well help. The mediation evidence is uneven across experiments, so other factors, such as assumptions about the author's intent or the expected register of a genre, probably contribute. He speculates that insecure writers use big words most, so the strategy may hurt the people who feel least confident, and closes by endorsing the standard advice to write simply.

### Assessment of the evidence

The design is clear and the five-experiment sequence answers its own objections in order, which is a strength. The conclusion that gratuitous long words do not make readers think better of the writer is well supported by consistent direction across three experimental setups. Several features limit how much weight the numbers can bear. Samples are small (27 to 85) and come from one elite university. Many of the reported tests are near the conventional significance threshold, and some rely on one-tailed tests. The key mediation evidence reached significance only in Experiment 1. The excerpts are short and read cold, with a single rating each, and the stakes are low. Word length stands in for complexity, and synonym substitution by thesaurus produces words that are rarer as well as longer, so length and unfamiliarity are not separated. The paper measures judged intelligence and acceptance, which are not the same as comprehension or recall. The article is also an early, widely cited fluency study from the period before the replication debates in social psychology, and exact effect sizes should be treated as rough. The direction of the finding is the dependable part.

For writers, the practical lessons are modest and consistent with the manuals. Choose the shorter, more familiar word when it has the same meaning. Do not expect vocabulary to disguise weak content or to lift strong content. Keep a long word when it is the precise one. Fluency also affects how credible a reader finds a text, so anything that adds reading effort without adding information (rare words, dense phrasing, hard-to-read formatting) costs trust.

## What the skill applies

Section 2 of SKILL.md ("Words") opens with the rule to use the plain word, and the sentence that justifies it, that fancy words add length and make the writer sound less sure of the point, rests on this paper. The paper supplied the reason the rule exists. Without it the rule is a matter of taste, and with it the rule says that readers rate an author lower when the vocabulary is inflated, even holding content constant. The example pairs in that rule (`use` for `utilize`, `before` for `prior to`, and similar pairs) are the skill's own, although the paper's title is itself a deliberately inflated rendering of a plain phrase.

The same section's rule on jargon tells the writer to translate technical terms for non-experts and never to use them to sound expert. It relies on the fluency mechanism and on the paper's stated boundary that experts may find their own jargon fluent. The skill therefore keeps jargon for expert readers who use it and does not ban it outright.

The skill's opening principle is that each rule either wastes the reader's time or blurs the claim. Together with the instruction to break a rule when it would make a sentence worse, it follows the paper's own qualification that a long word can be right when it is the most precise or concise choice. Rule 8 of the revision pass says to keep an overused word only when it is the most precise one available, which echoes that point even though that list is built from a different evidence base.

Section 6, which tells the writer to stop when the content is done and to avoid padding, and section 3, which trims empty openers and inflated phrases, share the fluency rationale that unneeded reading effort costs the writer credibility. The paper treats word choice, not sentence trimming, so this is an extension of its logic and not a direct finding of it.

## What the skill omits

The skill does not reproduce any of the experimental detail, since it is guidance for agent prose and not a review of the literature. It omits the thesaurus-substitution method and the specific effect sizes, because they would not change any rule. It also leaves out the processing-fluency theory as a general account (the effects of fluency on truth, liking, and confidence), which is background that the writer does not need to apply sentence by sentence.

The skill does not adopt word length as a rule. The paper uses length as a convenient proxy and admits that rarity and grammar also matter, and the skill's own lists are about plain versus inflated choices, not about counting letters. The skill likewise does not forbid all long or Latinate words, consistent with the paper's caveat.

The paper's speculation about insecure writers, stereotype threat, and leaders undermining confidence is left out as untested in this study. So is the low-toner discounting result, which has no bearing on prose style beyond a loose suggestion not to rely on hard-to-read formatting. The skill makes no claim that a reader will mark an AI-sounding text lower in intelligence, since this study did not test machine-written text.

## Conflicts with other references

This source does not conflict with the style manuals; it agrees with their advice for plain words and supplies empirical backing that the manuals mostly lack. The reference folder was empty when I reviewed this file, so I could not read the other references for comparison, and what follows is based on the brief and on what this paper contains.

The one real tension is in scope. The vocabulary studies the skill relies on for its revision list (Kobak, Juzek and Ward, Liang, and the popular lists in the Wikipedia reference) concern words that language models use too often. Many of those words are short or ordinary, such as `showcase` or `boast`. Oppenheimer's finding concerns long and rare words regardless of who writes them. The two do not measure the same thing, so the skill treats them as separate rules: section 2 handles plain-versus-inflated word choice on the Oppenheimer rationale, and revision item 8 handles machine-default vocabulary on the frequency evidence. Where a word fails both tests, the skill gains no extra authority from stacking them.

A second, mild tension is with the "have a view" and authority tone of section 7. Oppenheimer shows readers read hard text as a sign of lower intelligence, but his experts-as-readers caveat means a plain register is the right default for general readers and could differ for specialist audiences. The skill's resolution is to default to plain words and permit field terms for readers who use them.

## Review notes

I read the full text of the article, all ten pages of the PDF copy hosted by the New Zealand consultancy, including the summary, introduction, all five experiments, the general discussion, and the acknowledgments. I did not read the appendix stimuli or the reference list in detail. The figures did not extract as text, so I relied on the text's own descriptions of them. Several minus signs and the less-than and equals symbols were lost in text extraction, which is why I report the lowest mean in Experiment 1 as around negative two and treat the sign as inferred. For the same reason I give the Experiment 5 acceptance ratings only as a direction and not as signed means.

The statistics I quote (sample sizes, means, effect sizes) come straight from the article; I rounded some. My source for the 2006 Ig Nobel Prize for Literature that the paper received is a web summary on the Improbable Research site, which I checked. I did not read the prize announcement itself, and the prize is a humor award that does not speak to the paper's quality. My assessment paragraph on small samples, one-tailed tests, and the proxy problem is my own reading and not something the paper claims. I did not look at replications of the fluency effects.
