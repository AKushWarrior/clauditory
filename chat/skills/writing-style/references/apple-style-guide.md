# Apple Style Guide (June 2026 edition)

- **Source:** Apple Inc., *Apple Style Guide*, June 2026 edition (Cupertino, Calif.: Apple Inc., 2026). Document number 028-00839. No individual authors or editors are named.
- **Type:** style manual
- **Where to find it:** PDF at https://help.apple.com/pdf/applestyleguide/en_US/apple-style-guide.pdf (244 pages, created June 16, 2026). Apple also publishes the guide as web pages, which this review did not consult.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-04
- **Used by:** chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose, audience and authority

The guide sets editorial rules for text in Apple's instructional materials, technical documentation, reference material, training and user interfaces. Its stated aim is a consistent voice across those materials. It addresses Apple's own writers and editors and also third-party developers, whom it asks to follow the guidelines in any text their users will see.

The guide does not try to be a complete style manual. Its opening pages tell writers to follow Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary and The Chicago Manual of Style in general, and to treat the guide as a list of exceptions and additions. When the two outside references disagree, Chicago settles style and usage and Merriam-Webster settles spelling. The guide does not say which edition of Chicago it means. It marks its departures from both books inline, with a parenthetical note that a rule is an exception to Chicago or to Merriam-Webster. Its conventions are U.S. English, and it sends writers of localized content to resources for their own language. It also mentions that some Apple departments, marketing among them, keep supplemental guides, and that interface design questions belong to Apple's Human Interface Guidelines.

### How the guide is built

The body is a single alphabetical list, Style and usage A to Z, which runs from page 11 to page 222 of 244. Four short chapters follow it: Writing inclusively (about seven pages), Units of measure (about seven pages, mostly tables of prefixes and unit symbols), Technical notation (two pages) and International style (five pages). There is no general chapter on writing well. Everything the guide says about voice, tone, sentence construction and word choice sits in scattered A-to-Z entries such as passive voice, future tense, contractions and first person.

Most entries concern Apple's vocabulary. They fix the names and capitalization of products and features, the verbs for interface actions (click, tap, choose, press, swipe), the names of onscreen elements (dialog, sheet, pop-up menu, More button) and the treatment of hardware terms. A large share of the list is of no use outside Apple's own documentation. The front matter includes a change log for each edition back to February 2019, and the logs show the guide is pruned as well as extended: the June 2026 edition adds 11 entries, changes about 30 and deletes about 100, most of them obsolete hardware terms such as fax, trackball and World Wide Web. The logs also date the inclusive-language work. Many of the entries on master and slave, blacklist and whitelist, sanity check, kill, grandfathered and disability terms first appeared in the June 2021 edition, and the disability chapter was revised in July 2022.

Entries vary in form. Some are a single line naming the preferred term. Others give a reason, a rule and paired correct and incorrect example sentences. The guide states its rules flatly and rarely explains them, so a reader learns what Apple prefers far more often than why.

### General writing entries in the A-to-Z list

Taken together, a few dozen entries amount to a short writing guide.

**Voice and person.** The contractions entry describes Apple's voice as informal and recommends contractions across most documentation, interface text and marketing. It allows common contractions of be-verbs and auxiliaries with not and with pronouns, and it rules out contractions formed from nouns or product names, multipart contractions such as could've, and rare or awkward forms. It asks writers to keep localization in mind. The first person and we entries forbid we, us and I and tell writers to recast the sentence around the reader or the product. The user entry tells writers to address the reader as you when the readers are users, and to keep user for end users when writing for developers or administrators, who are themselves addressed as you. The recommend entry rejects both we recommend and Apple recommends; it prefers a construction built on recommended, or an informal phrase saying something is a good idea.

**Voice of verbs.** The passive voice entry says to avoid the passive when possible and to prefer the active. It grants that the passive is sometimes necessary, naming two cases: when the active would force a convoluted sentence, and when it would require excessive anthropomorphism. It adds a teaching case. In a tutorial, a passive description can keep the reader from trying an action before the lesson asks for it. The prompt entry makes the same trade the other way: if a writer must say that the reader is prompted, the sentence should be passive so that the hardware or software is not the actor.

**Tense and modality.** The future tense entry asks for the present tense wherever it will do, including for a sequence of steps or events and for conditional sentences of the form if this parameter is set, the function returns. Future tense is kept for things that have not happened yet, such as a product that is not yet available. The can, might, may entry assigns can to capability, might and may to possibility (with might suggesting the lower probability) and may alone to permission. The guide has no entry on must or should, and its own corrected example in the we entry uses should to express a recommendation.

**Plain and precise words.** A run of short entries pushes toward the shorter or more exact word. In order to is allowed only when unavoidable. Via is allowed only when space is tight, with by, from, through, using or with as replacements. And/or should be rewritten. The Latin abbreviations e.g., i.e., etc. and et al. are banned in favor of their English equivalents. Please is to be dropped from instructions and cross-references. Over should not stand in for more than. Comprise is to be avoided altogether because its correct use confuses readers. Functionality gives way to features, capability gives way to a statement of what the reader can do, and access gives way to a more specific verb such as log in or connect. The allow and let entries ask writers to restructure sentences so the reader is the subject, though let is acceptable in text that describes what a feature does. Smaller entries settle if against whether (whether when two outcomes are possible), since against because, while against although and whereas, fewer against less, and that against which.

**Jargon and abbreviations.** The jargon entry is two sentences: avoid it where possible, and define technical terms on first use. The abbreviations and acronyms entry asks writers to spell out a term on first use if the audience might not know it, with the short form in parentheses, and to reverse the order when the short form is the better-known one. It also covers articles before abbreviations, plurals without apostrophes and pronunciation keys for acronyms.

**Time-bound words.** The new entry tells writers not to call a product or feature new, because the text will date quickly, and to name the software version that introduced the feature. The latest entry makes a similar point about software updates.

**Tone devices.** Exclamation points are allowed now and then in promotional text and dialogue and are to be avoided in documentation. The humor entry approves of humor, mostly in examples, and warns that it can offend and may not translate. The Note entry asks for notes and other notices to be used sparingly, since they lose force when they appear often, and forbids stacking them.

### Punctuation, numbers and lists

The punctuation entries follow Chicago with a few stated exceptions.

The commas entry requires the serial comma before and or or in a series of three or more. The em dash entry allows the mark to set off an interruption, a change of direction or a long list that would tangle the syntax, asks writers not to overuse it, and closes it up with no spaces. The en dash covers ranges of numbers, compound adjectives in which one element is itself two words, and the minus sign. Ranges of times are an exception: the time of day entry prefers the word to between the endpoints in running text. The colons entry capitalizes the first word after a colon when it starts a complete sentence, capitalizes the first word after a colon in any heading, and puts a colon before every list whether or not the lead-in is a complete sentence. The guide marks the first and third of these as exceptions to Chicago. Quotation marks are curly outside code, with periods and commas inside them.

The numbers entry spells out cardinal numbers one through nine and ordinals through ninth, and spells out a number that starts a sentence while advising a rewrite. It uses numerals for numbers as numbers, for units of measure and time however small, for approximations, for addresses, bits, slots and other technical references, and for every number of a category within a paragraph once any number in that category exceeds nine. Commas group digits only from five digits up, so the guide writes four-digit numbers such as 1024 with no comma. Ranges take an en dash and a full closing number. The percent entry always puts a numeral before the word percent, allows the % symbol in technical appendixes, specification lists and tables and when the word would repeat to the point of clumsiness, and keeps percentage for vague quantities. Fractions with denominators of 10 or less are spelled out in user materials.

The two list entries give purposes for each kind. A bulleted list stresses the parallelism of items that need no order, and its items must be parallel, start with a capital letter, and take a closing period only when they are complete sentences. A numbered list stresses sequence. In a numbered list of steps, each step is a complete sentence in sentence-style capitalization with closing punctuation, and the guide's example introduces the steps with an imperative lead-in and a colon. The capitalization entry defines sentence style and title style and leaves the choice between them for headings to each department, except in interface text, where onscreen names are reproduced exactly.

### Writing inclusively

This chapter is the one piece of the guide that reads as an argument. It opens by saying that Apple's users reflect the diversity of the world and that the guidance will change over time and differs by locale.

The general guidelines ask writers to imagine the text from the reader's side, to research the origin and current understanding of a doubtful word, and to weigh context (the guide's example is a word that is unacceptable for a person and acceptable for silencing a device). Writers should avoid terms that are violent, oppressive or ableist when describing technology, and the chapter names kill and hang, master and slave, and sanity check. It advises against describing software or hardware with human or biological attributes, because of unintended hurtful implications. It asks writers to drop idioms and colloquialisms, for the sake of language learners and translation, and to stop using colors as metaphors for good and bad, as in blacklist or white hat. Its final guideline is caution: when a term seems questionable, choose another, since an equally clear one usually exists.

On representation, the chapter asks for example names drawn from many ethnicities and genders, and it reminds writers that not every culture orders names as given name then family name. It asks that depictions of people vary in ethnicity, gender, age, body type and ability, that examples of holidays, food and sport not come only from Western culture, and that examples not assume affluence or a single family structure.

On gender, it asks for gender-neutral wording where gender is beside the point and allows specific genders where the context needs them. Singular they is endorsed, with a plural verb, and so are the alternatives of a plural noun or a rewritten sentence that needs no pronoun. Writers should not infer a specific person's pronouns from a name or appearance, should ask when they are unsure, and should ask about titles such as Mx. or else refer to people by name alone.

The A-to-Z list carries the same policy into individual entries: man and its compounds are replaced by neutral words such as staff and workforce, and grandfathered, man-in-the-middle, dummy, rule of thumb and black box all have entries with replacements.

### Writing about disability

The disability section asks writers to focus on a person's accomplishments, personality or story, and says a disability may not need mentioning at all unless it is essential to the content. It explains identity-first language (a deaf person, an autistic person), which treats the disability as part of identity and culture, and person-first language (a person who is deaf), which puts the individual first. Preference varies, and the section tells writers to ask the person or group. Its summary table leans toward identity-first language for people who consider the disability part of their identity, and for autism it lists person-with-autism forms among the terms to avoid while noting that many autistic people prefer identity-first wording.

The section asks writers to treat disability as a spectrum and to avoid ableist framing: nondisabled people should not be called normal or healthy, and disabled people should not be described as brave or inspiring or as overcoming their disability. For instructions it adds a rule I have not seen elsewhere in this folder's sources: avoid wording that assumes a sense, such as saying the reader sees a message or hears an alert, and describe the event instead (a message appears, an alert plays). It also bans idioms that carry a negative message about disability, naming crazy, falling on deaf ears and turning a blind eye. It then limits the rule. Everyday phrases such as seeing someone's point or watching a movie are fine, because readers understand the intent, and so is the cross-reference word see.

The table lists preferred identity-first and person-first forms and terms to avoid for about a dozen groups. Among the terms to avoid are differently abled, special needs, handicapped, hearing impaired, wheelchair-bound, confined to a wheelchair, high-functioning and low-functioning, and mute. It notes capitalization conventions such as Deaf for cultural identity and DeafBlind in some communities.

### Technical notation

This two-page chapter applies mainly to developer documentation. It asks writers to follow the capitalization of the programming language under discussion and to use code font for code. In syntax descriptions, literals go in code font, placeholders in italics and the brackets around optional parts in plain text. Placeholder names join words with internal capitals and must be named consistently.

In running text, code font covers expressions in a programming language and the names of files, volumes, directories and libraries. Three rules govern how code words sit in English sentences. A command or function name is not used as a verb, so the reader runs a command on something rather than using the command's name as the verb. A word in code font is not pluralized or otherwise inflected; the sentence is rewritten around it. Punctuation after a code word stays in body font unless it belongs to the code. A placeholder is written exactly as in the syntax and never used as an ordinary English noun; to refer to the thing in prose, the writer uses ordinary words. The chapter also asks writers to avoid foo, bar and baz in examples and to use names that suggest the kind of item.

The A-to-Z code font entry adds that code font marks text the reader types, code listings, language elements and commands in running text, and lists places where code font does not belong in user materials, such as headings, cross-references and web addresses.

### International style and units of measure

The international style chapter serves readers with limited English and translators, human or machine. It reduces to four rules: write in simple structures, avoid idioms and colloquialisms, avoid shortcuts, symbols and abbreviations that could be spelled out, and express data in standard international conventions unless a house convention has a compelling advantage. Each convention cites a standards body and a rationale. Country and language names use ISO two-letter codes in tables. Currency amounts use ISO three-letter codes after the number, because symbols such as the dollar sign are shared by several currencies. Dates run year, month, day with hyphens, times use a 24-hour clock with offsets from Coordinated Universal Time, large numbers are grouped with nonbreaking spaces instead of commas or periods, telephone numbers follow the ITU international format, and quantities use SI units with symbols, optionally followed by non-SI equivalents in parentheses. The localization entry in the A-to-Z list adds that translated text grows longer, so paragraphs should stay short, and that examples tied to one nation or culture should be avoided.

The units of measure chapter applies to U.S. user documentation. It spells out a unit on first use with its symbol in parentheses, spells out nonmetric units in text, puts a space between a number and a unit symbol, hyphenates spelled-out units in compound adjectives, and never pluralizes or hyphenates symbols. Its tables of SI prefixes and unit symbols are reference material.

### Strengths and limits as evidence

The guide is corporate house style. It cites no research, and its authority comes from Apple's editorial practice and the size of the documentation it governs. Its rules are clear and testable, and the paired correct and incorrect sentences make them easy to apply. The reasons it does give are practical: dated text goes stale, symbols are ambiguous across currencies, idioms resist translation, and certain words hurt readers. The inclusive chapter gives the most reasoning, though even there the case rests on respect and caution, with no data on how readers respond.

Three limits matter for this skill. First, the guide's scope is user documentation and interface text for Apple products, so much of it settles questions an agent will never meet. Second, its rules govern terminology and mechanics far more than register. Its example sentences carry a marketing tone, and several of them use words that the skill's revision item 8 flags as machine-default vocabulary: examples in the entries for Apple Intelligence, AutoMix and photos use enhance, seamlessly and showcase, product examples call chips powerful, and the corrected sentence in the gender identity section says that hiring diverse people fosters a culture of innovation. Third, the guide does not follow all of its own advice in its own prose. Its chapters use closed-up em dashes freely, including in the inclusive-writing guidelines, which is consistent with its em dash entry but shows the house tolerates the mark in ordinary explanatory text.

## What the skill applies

This source mainly corroborates rules whose wording comes from elsewhere. I compared the skill against the guide entry by entry, and for most rules the news manuals (for sections 1 to 8) or the Google and Microsoft developer guides (for section 9) state the rule more fully or more closely to the skill's wording. A few rules match Apple more closely than any other source I checked, and I note those below. My judgment of influence rests on comparing texts, since I have no record of how the skill was drafted.

**Section 2, Words.** The plain-word bullet agrees with Apple's in order to, functionality, access and capability entries, though none of the skill's listed pairs (use for utilize, before for prior to and the rest) appears in Apple's list. The rule to translate jargon or define it on first use matches the jargon entry almost exactly. The acronym rule (spell out an unfamiliar abbreviation on first use) matches the abbreviations and acronyms entry, and the skill's further step of using a descriptive phrase on later references goes beyond Apple. The rule to prefer verbs to nouns built from verbs has support in the capability and functionality entries, which turn a noun of ability into a statement of what the reader does.

**Section 3, Sentences.** The padding example of in order to becoming to is Apple's in order to entry, which is also in the NYT manual. The active-voice default and the carve-out for an actor who is unknown or beside the point agree with Apple's passive voice entry in direction. Apple's reasons for the passive (avoiding convoluted sentences and anthropomorphism) differ from the skill's, and the skill does not borrow them. The skill's rule allowing exclamation points only in quotations is close to Apple's ban in documentation, and stricter.

**Section 5, People and fairness.** The overlap here is strong, though Chicago and AP cover the same ground. The rule to mention a disability only when relevant matches the disability section's advice that it may not need mentioning. The rule to use the labels people use for themselves matches Apple's repeated instruction to ask the person. The skill's example of deaf employees is an identity-first adjective form that Apple's table endorses. In the rule against metaphors built on disability or illness, the example crazy idea matches Apple's list of ableist idioms; Apple is the only reference in this folder whose text I found naming crazy, so it is the likeliest source of that example. The gender rule (neutral nouns, singular they or a rewritten sentence, respect for a specific person's pronouns) matches the gender identity section and the pronouns and man entries point for point.

**Section 6, Structure and format.** The rule that list items share one grammatical shape matches the bulleted list entry's parallelism rule. Apple's distinction between bulleted lists for unordered parallel items and numbered lists for sequence supports the skill's use of lists only for steps, options and specs.

**Section 8, Mechanics.** Apple agrees with the skill on spelling out one through nine, on figures for measurements and time, on the word percent in running text with the symbol in tables and specification lists, on capitalizing a complete sentence after a colon, on that for restrictive and which for nonrestrictive clauses, and on fewer for counted things. All of these also appear in the NYT manual, which the skill follows by precedence, so Apple adds agreement without changing any wording.

**Section 9, Technical and persisted text.** Apple contributes most here, still largely as a second voice beside Google and Microsoft.

- The rule to address the reader as you and use the third person for software and other people matches the user entry, including its split between developers addressed as you and their end users called users.
- The present-tense rule matches the future tense entry, including its example of a conditional sentence about a function's behavior.
- The rule on must, can and might agrees with the can, might, may entry on can and might. Apple has no must entry, and its own example sentences use should for recommendations, so the skill's caution about should does not come from Apple.
- The timeless-documentation rule matches the new entry closely. Apple's advice to name the version that introduced a feature is the most concrete form of the skill's instruction to describe the thing as of the version the text covers. The section 9 bullet no longer names the dating words; they sit in revision item 13, whose list includes new, and Apple's new and latest entries back that part of the item.
- The rule against describing software as if it thinks or wants has partial support. Apple's inclusive chapter advises against human or biological attributes for software and hardware, its passive voice entry names anthropomorphism as a reason to accept the passive, and its prompt and sign in entries keep devices and apps from acting as people. Apple's stated reason is possible hurt to readers, while the skill's is accuracy.
- The code-formatting rule, especially its clause about using code words only as the exact names they are, matches the technical notation chapter. Apple's rules against using a command name as a verb, inflecting a code word or using a placeholder as an ordinary noun are the clearest statement of that clause among the sources I checked.
- The rule on readers whose first language isn't English matches the international style chapter, which frames its audience as readers with limited English proficiency and translators, and the localization entry on idioms. Apple has no rule about keeping optional that and articles or about stacked modifiers; microsoft-writing-style-guide.md traces all four parts of the bullet to Microsoft's global-English tips.
- The numbered-steps rule agrees with the numbered list entry's requirement that steps be complete sentences. The goal-before-action wording has some support in the want entry, whose suggested rewrite of an if-you-want clause leads with an infinitive of purpose.

Several section 9 rules have no support in Apple at all. The bullet asking writers to describe steps plainly and leave the difficulty to the reader has no counterpart, nor does the part of revision item 13 that removes simple, easy, quick and just from steps; Apple's own examples describe products as easy to use. The bullet asking writers to claim only what they can show, and the part of item 13 that removes best, fastest, always, never, ensure and guarantee, also have none. Apple's ensure entry defines the word without cautioning against it, and its example sentences make unmeasured claims freely. Apple has no rule on link text; it prefers click a link over follow a link and approves of see in cross-references, but it never addresses vague link labels. It leaves heading capitalization to each department. It says nothing about code comments, commit messages or pull requests, so those bullets, including the commit bullet's requests to say what users will notice and what a claimed improvement costs, rest on the git and kernel references.

## What the skill omits

**Apple's vocabulary.** The product names, feature names, interface element names and interface verbs make up most of the A-to-Z list. They apply only to writing about Apple products, and an agent that needs them can consult the guide directly.

**Small usage entries the skill does not carry.** Apple's bans on please in instructions, on via, on and/or, on Latin abbreviations, on over for more than and on comprise have no matching rules in SKILL.md. The plain-word rule in section 2 covers some of them in spirit. They are reasonable rules for documentation, and please and the Latin abbreviations in particular bear on agent-written instructions, but the skill keeps its word lists short to limit length and priming, and these were not added. A later revision could consider please and e.g. for section 9.

**Contractions and the first-person ban.** Apple recommends contractions throughout and forbids we in all documentation. The skill keeps contractions for conversation and spells them out in formal documents, and it allows we when it names a team. These are deliberate departures, described under conflicts.

**International notation.** ISO dates, 24-hour times, currency codes, space-grouped digits and SI-only units suit text that will be translated or read worldwide. The skill's mechanics follow the NYT manual for prose a U.S.-style newspaper reader would expect, and adopting international notation would conflict with section 8 (for example on money, which the skill writes with a dollar sign).

**Typography and document furniture.** Curly quotes, italics for placeholders and titles, hyphenation of compound modifiers, cross-reference formats, notes and warnings, break points in web addresses and unit symbol spacing are left out. Much agent output is Markdown or plain text, where code formatting does the job that Apple gives to italics for placeholders, and the skill avoids house-style detail it cannot apply consistently.

**Inclusive-language rules beyond disability and gender.** Section 5 includes the disability metaphor rule and the gender rules, and leaves out Apple's guidance on violent technical terms (kill, hang), oppressive terms (master and slave, blacklist and whitelist, grandfathered), color metaphors, diverse example names and representation in depictions of people. These rules mostly concern technical vocabulary and illustrative content, and a coding agent writing comments and commit messages meets them often (master branch, kill a process). Their absence is the omission with the most practical cost, and it may deserve a section 9 rule later. The sense-based rule for instructions (say a message appears, not that the reader sees it) is also left out, though it would fit section 9.

**Tone devices.** Apple's acceptance of humor in examples and occasional exclamation points in promotional text is not taken up. The skill allows dry humor in conversation and limits exclamation points more tightly than Apple does.

## Conflicts with other references

The technical guides rank below NYT, AP and Chicago, and section 9 states that sections 1 to 8 win where they disagree with it. Every conflict below was resolved against Apple for that reason or by the user's explicit choices. The skill does not rank the technical guides among themselves, so where Apple disagrees with Google or Microsoft, the news manuals or the skill's own reasoning decide.

**Serial comma (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple requires the serial comma, which agrees with Chicago, Google and Microsoft. NYT and AP omit it in a simple series, and the skill follows NYT, adding the comma only when an item contains and or the sentence would be ambiguous. A writer following any of the three corporate guides would add commas the skill removes.

**Dashes (nyt-manual-of-style.md, ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md, freeburg-em-dashes.md).** Apple uses the unspaced em dash for interruptions and long embedded lists, warns only against overuse, and uses the en dash for ranges and complex compound adjectives. That matches Chicago's typography and differs from the spaced dashes of NYT and AP. Google and Microsoft also use the unspaced em dash, though Google rules out the en dash and writes ranges with a hyphen. Apple's own explanatory prose uses the em dash often, which fits Freeburg's evidence that human writers use the mark at real rates. The skill bans the em dash and writes ranges with to, by the user's choice, which is stricter than every manual and guide in this folder. Apple's preference for to in ranges of times is the one place it agrees with the skill's ranges rule.

**Numbers (nyt-manual-of-style.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple and NYT share the one-through-nine rule, where Google and Microsoft spell out zero through nine. They part on mixed series: Apple uses numerals for every number of a category in a paragraph once any of them exceeds nine, which resembles Chicago's consistency rule and the category rules recorded for Google and Microsoft, while the NYT reference records that the paper applies the cutoff even inside a series that mixes small and large numbers. The skill states the cutoff without a category exception, which follows NYT. Apple's practice of grouping digits with commas only from five digits up has no counterpart in the skill.

**Percent (ap-stylebook.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Here Apple sides with the skill. It writes the word percent after a numeral in text and keeps the symbol for tables, specification lists and heavy repetition, as NYT and Chicago do. Google, Microsoft and AP write the % sign with figures in text.

**Colons and lists (chicago-manual-of-style.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple flags its capital letter after a colon before a complete sentence as an exception to Chicago. The Chicago reference records that the 18th edition now capitalizes in that case too, so the exception note appears to date from an earlier edition. NYT, AP, Chicago, Apple and the skill now agree on this point. Google and Microsoft lowercase the word after a colon in that case, so Apple is the one technical guide that matches the skill. Apple's other colon rule, a colon before every list even after an incomplete lead-in, still departs from Chicago, which requires a complete sentence before the colon. The skill says only that a list gets a short introductory phrase or sentence and takes no position on the colon.

**Contractions (SKILL.md section 7).** Apple recommends contractions across documentation as part of an informal house voice, as Google and Microsoft do (see google-developer-style-guide.md and microsoft-writing-style-guide.md). The skill uses them in conversation and spells them out in formal documents. Section 7 governs, so for formal documentation the skill is more formal than Apple.

**First person (SKILL.md sections 7 and 9, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple bans we, us and I in documentation. Microsoft discourages we in general but allows it where the company must be clear that it is speaking. The skill allows first person in conversation and allows we in technical text when it plainly means a named team or organization, which is close to Microsoft's position. The skill's narrower rule fits agents, which often report their own actions and their team's decisions.

**Recommendations and the passive (rhodes-active-passive-voice.md, reinhart-llm-grammatical-style.md, google-developer-style-guide.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** Apple prefers the active voice, like the skill, but its recommend and prompt entries prescribe agentless passives, the first to avoid saying who recommends and the second to avoid making software the actor. On recommendations the technical guides split: Apple rejects we recommend in favor of a recommended construction, while Google suggests we recommend as the usual way to mark advice and Microsoft allows it in preference to the impersonal passive. Reinhart finds that instruction-tuned models already use agentless passives at about half the human rate, and Rhodes finds no comprehension difference by voice. The skill justifies the active voice by accountability and allows the passive when the actor is beside the point. Apple's prompt case fits that carve-out. Its recommend case does not, because the skill would name who recommends, which puts it with Google and Microsoft.

**Should (SKILL.md section 9, google-developer-style-guide.md).** Apple has no rule on should and uses it for recommendations in its own corrected examples. Google narrows should to widely recognized recommendations, and the skill avoids it when the reader cannot tell whether it means required or recommended. Apple's uses are clearly recommendations, so they fit both rules, but Apple gives no support for the skill's rule.

**Future tense in sequences (SKILL.md section 9).** Apple says the present tense is enough for a sequence of steps or events. The skill keeps the future for events that come later in a sequence. The difference is one of degree, and the skill's wording governs.

**Goal before action (SKILL.md section 9).** Apple's let entry rewrites a feature description as an action followed by its purpose, which puts the action first. Its want entry leads with the purpose. The guide is inconsistent on this point, and the skill follows the goal-first pattern.

**Ban lists (semantic-gravity-wells.md, anthropic-prompting-docs.md).** Much of Apple's A-to-Z list consists of terms marked don't use, named in the entry. The Semantic Gravity Wells reference warns that naming a forbidden word can prime it, and the Anthropic prompting docs favor positive instructions. The skill takes Apple's content where it applies but phrases the drafting sections around the word to use and keeps its lists in the post-draft revision pass.

**Disability language (ap-stylebook.md, chicago-manual-of-style.md, microsoft-writing-style-guide.md).** The AP and Chicago references describe person-first and identity-first language as two legitimate approaches and tell writers to learn the person's preference, and Microsoft prefers person-first language by default while accepting identity-first where a community prefers it. Apple agrees on asking, but its table leans toward identity-first language and lists person-with-autism forms among terms to avoid, so it and Microsoft lean in opposite directions. The skill's phrase about leading with the person could be read as a person-first rule. Its example, deaf employees, is identity-first, and its first clause tells writers to use people's own labels, which is what all four sources ask. The tension lies in the wording more than in the practice.
