# Kobak and colleagues on excess vocabulary in biomedical abstracts

- **Source:** Dmitry Kobak, Rita González-Márquez, Emőke-Ágnes Horvát and Jan Lause, "Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary," Science Advances, 2025. Earlier versions circulated as the arXiv preprint 2406.07016 (five versions; v5 reviewed here).
- **Type:** peer-reviewed journal article (the reviewed text is the v5 preprint of the same work)
- **Where to find it:** DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adt3813; preprint at https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016 (HTML at https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5); code and data at github.com/berenslab/llm-excess-vocab
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** chat/skills/writing-style/SKILL.md

## What the source covers

### Purpose and idea

The paper asks how much of recent scientific writing was produced or edited with a large language model (LLM), and which words give the use away. The authors sort earlier attempts into three groups: black-box LLM detectors, models that treat word frequencies as a mixture of human and machine text (Liang and colleagues are the leading example), and studies that track a hand-picked list of marker words. All three need ground-truth sets of human and machine text to train or calibrate against. The authors object that such sets embed assumptions about which models scientists use and how they prompt them, that a detector cannot be inspected at the word level, and that none of the earlier work compared LLM-induced change with earlier shifts in scholarly language. This paper aims to remove the need for ground truth. Kobak and colleagues avoid the problem by borrowing the logic of excess-mortality studies from epidemiology. Public health researchers estimate pandemic deaths by comparing observed deaths with the number a pre-pandemic trend would have predicted. The authors do the same with word frequencies: they predict how often each word should have appeared in 2024 abstracts if the pre-ChatGPT trend had continued, and then look at where reality exceeds the prediction.

The approach has two stated advantages. It needs no labeled LLM text, and it can be run on any earlier year, so it gives a historical baseline of how much vocabulary normally shifts. The authors also say they did not use an LLM to write or edit their own manuscript.

### Data

The corpus is the annual PubMed snapshot released in early 2025. The authors kept English abstracts between 250 and 4,000 characters, which left about 24.8 million abstracts, and analyzed the years 2010 to 2024, a little over 15.1 million abstracts. They removed boilerplate with more than a hundred text patterns (copyright lines, citation prompts and similar), which changed several hundred thousand abstracts, and dropped several thousand errata and retraction notices. Each abstract was turned into a yes-or-no record of which words it contains, for roughly 273,000 distinct words of at least four letters. Singular and plural forms count as different words, so the later lists contain several forms of one lemma. The core statistical analysis for 2024 uses the roughly 26,700 words that appear in more than one abstract in ten thousand in both 2023 and 2024 (over 100 uses a year). For the year-by-year comparison, a word is considered in year Y only if it exceeds that frequency in both Y and the year before. Each paper was also assigned an affiliation country (first affiliation of the first author), one of 39 fields (by keywords in the journal name) and, for subgroup work, an inferred gender of the first and last author.

The choice of PubMed has consequences for how far the results travel. It is a large, uniform corpus of one genre: scientific abstracts, in a design where the unit of measure is the share of abstracts containing a word at least once. The authors name emails, news and social media only as future applications, so the paper itself says nothing about chat replies, news or fiction.

### Excess words: definitions and measures

For each word, the authors compute its frequency in each year, with a small correction to avoid dividing by zero. To estimate what the 2024 frequency would have been without LLMs, they look only at 2021 and 2022 (they leave out 2023 on purpose, since it may already be affected) and extend the line between them forward two years. The extrapolation is never allowed to fall below the 2022 value, so the counterfactual leans high and the resulting excess is conservative.

Two measures of excess follow. The frequency gap is the observed frequency minus the counterfactual, and the frequency ratio is the observed frequency divided by the counterfactual. They answer different questions. The ratio catches rare words whose use multiplies, and the gap catches common words whose use rises by a few points. A word counts as an excess word if its gap exceeds one percentage point of abstracts, or if its ratio exceeds a frequency-dependent threshold (the formula in the text I have is garbled in extraction, but it appears to require a ratio of about 2 for the rarest words analyzed and a smaller ratio for more common ones). The authors chose both thresholds so that most words in the pre-COVID years fell well below them. The same machinery is run for every year from 2013 onward, which gives the control the single-year analysis lacks.

### Results by year

Before COVID the method found essentially nothing. From 2013 to 2019 no word cleared the gap threshold of one percentage point. The pandemic produced a bump, with a peak of 190 excess words in 2021. The 2024 count was 454, more than double the pandemic peak, and it arrived about a year after ChatGPT's release. These counts treat each inflected form as its own word. In lemma terms (counting all forms of a word once) the figures were 343 for 2024 and 180 for 2021.

The more telling result is the kind of word. The authors manually labeled all 900 unique excess words found across 2013 to 2024 as content words (naming a topic, such as masks, a drug or a method) or style words (carrying no subject matter, such as a verb or adjective of emphasis). Just over half (51.3 percent) were content and a bit under half (45.2 percent) were style, with a small remainder left unlabeled as ambiguous. The annotators worked from an alphabetized list without knowing which year each word came from, and discussed doubtful cases among the authors. The pandemic-era excess was nearly all content: terms for the virus, lockdowns, respiratory illness and treatments. The 2024 excess was nearly all style: of 379 excess style words in 2024, about two thirds were verbs and about one seventh adjectives. Previous vocabulary shocks in science were driven by events in the world. This one was driven by how people (or their tools) chose to write about the same subjects.

Individual words show the scale. The ratio for delves was about 28, for underscores about 14 and for showcasing about 11, meaning abstracts used them 11 to 28 times as often as the trend predicted. For comparison, the Ebola and Zika outbreaks produced ratios of about 10 and 40 for their names in their peak years, and COVID words exceeded 1,000. Among common words, the largest gaps in 2024 belong to potential (about 5 points), findings (about 4 points) and crucial (about 4 points). So the words that mark LLM text include both rare showpieces that jump many-fold and ordinary academic words that rose by several points.

### Estimated share of LLM-processed abstracts

A single word gives only a weak lower bound. The word potential, with a gap of 5.2 points, implies that at least 5.2 percent of 2024 abstracts went through an LLM, and no single word does better. The authors therefore compute the gap for a group of words: the share of abstracts containing at least one group member, 2024 observed versus counterfactual. They assume nothing about independence between words. To avoid searching every possible group, they build two non-overlapping sets. One is the rare set: every 2024 excess style word with frequency under a threshold, where the threshold that maximized the gap was 2 percent, giving 291 words. The other is a common set of ten words, picked from high-gap style words with some manual adjustment to maximize the gap: across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly and within. The two sets give nearly identical gaps (about 13.6 and 13.4 points), and the authors average them to say that at least 13.5 percent of 2024 PubMed abstracts were processed with an LLM. At roughly 1.5 million indexed papers per year, that means at least about 200,000 papers. The same method applied to four core COVID words gives about 6.9 points, so the LLM effect is about twice as large as the pandemic's effect on vocabulary.

The agreement of two independently built sets is reassuring. It does not prove the estimate is accurate, because both sets are chosen from the same excess list.

### Differences by field, country and journal

The authors repeat the estimate, with the same two word sets so that groups stay comparable, for the 39 fields, the 50 countries and 100 journals with the most papers, and a few pooled journal groups. A subgroup gets an estimate only if it had at least 300 papers in each year from 2018 to 2023. The spread is wide. Computation and bioinformatics sit near 20 percent. Papers from the United Kingdom and Australia sit near 5 percent, while those from China, South Korea and Taiwan sit near 20 percent. Inferred gender (from first names, available for about 55 percent of authors) shows small differences, about 9 percent for men and 7 to 8 percent for women. By journal, Sensors reaches about 25 percent, Cureus about 20 percent, and the MDPI and Frontiers families about 21 and 20 percent, while Nature, Science and Cell are near 7 percent and the Nature family near 10. The authors read this as higher LLM signal in outlets with a faster, lower-prestige review process. Intersections go higher: South Korean papers in Sensors reach about 34 percent and Chinese computation papers about 41 percent. In a two-dimensional map of about 4.1 million abstracts from 2022 to 2024, built from a biomedical language model's embeddings and grouped by topic, some neighborhoods approach 50 percent. One example is a cluster of roughly 400 deep-learning object-detection papers, mostly Chinese-affiliated and more than half in Sensors. The supplement notes that these regions mix papers from all three years, so the map groups by topic and not by marker words. It also points to an island of 2022 papers, many from journals shut down in 2023 for paper-mill problems, of which over a quarter were retracted. Across all subcorpora the estimate runs from under 5 percent to over 40 percent.

### Related work and implications the authors draw

The authors report that the marker words found in earlier work all appear in their list, including intricate and meticulously (which Gray had found to roughly double in 2023) and pivotal, intricate, showcasing and realm (the top words in Liang and colleagues' corpus-based analysis). They note that Liang and colleagues estimated 7 percent of Nature Portfolio papers and 17 percent of computer science preprints as LLM-assisted in early 2024, and that Kobak's study uses 5 to 200 times more papers per year. They also give examples of flowery 2023 abstract sentences, with the subject matter redacted, stacking meticulously, delving, intricate, comprehensive and pivotal. On implications, they list benefits writers seek (grammar, readability, translation, summaries) against risks: invented references, inaccurate summaries, confident false claims, inherited bias, homogenized ideas and citations, and paper-mill fakes. They mention retrieval-based tools and supplying one's own facts as mitigations, note that publishers and funders have restricted LLM use in peer review and authorship, and suggest the method could monitor compliance and extend to grant applications, peer review, news and social media.

### Limits the authors state

The method cannot tell direct LLM drafting from people absorbing LLM-favored words, though the authors guess the second effect is smaller and slower. It cannot separate different models. The number is a lower bound, since abstracts that avoid every marker word are invisible, and native speakers may be better at scrubbing telltale words, so the subgroup differences may partly reflect detection rather than use. Fields and journals with fast publication cycles show changes sooner, so the authors expect the bounds to rise as more publication cycles accumulate. They argue the true level may be near the highest subgroup bounds (above 30 percent), which they say matches surveys of researchers' self-reported LLM use. Gender is inferred approximately, from first names, for only 55 percent of authors. The content-or-style labels required judgment, and ambiguous words were left unlabeled.

### Strengths and weaknesses of the evidence

The strengths are scale (millions of abstracts, five to two hundred times more than comparable studies), a falsifiable design with a placebo (the quiet years before 2020 yield no excess words, and COVID yields content words rather than style words), a conservative counterfactual and openly released code and data. The weaknesses are that the evidence is correlational and rests on the assumption that the only new force acting on style vocabulary in 2024 is LLMs. The word list reflects the models in use around 2023 and 2024, which were earlier than the models a reader meets now, and each model release can shift habits. The measure is also restricted to words of four or more letters in the English alphabet, so it is blind to punctuation, sentence structure, rhetorical frames and short words. Finally, it measures presence of a word in a scientific abstract, which says little about how often a given word shows up in chat-style output.

### Implications for writers

For anyone producing prose, the main message is that a modest, identifiable set of words has become a visible signature. The authors' own example sentences show how the words cluster (several of them in one sentence), so a reader who has seen them many times can spot them quickly. The paper does not test how readers react. The signature is mostly verbs and adjectives of praise and emphasis, which carry little information and can be replaced with the plain word or removed.

### The authors' word list

The file excess.csv from the repository contains 900 rows, one per unique excess word across the years studied, each with a type label, a part of speech and an occasional comment. By the labels, 462 are content words, 407 are style words, 24 are marked as both content and style, 2 as style with a possible content use, and 4 as other. One row carries a mistyped label that reads as style with a count. The content words are mostly nouns (366 of 462) and are dominated by pandemic and medical terms, with comments explaining abbreviations such as a class of blood-pressure drugs. Of the style words, 268 are verbs, 53 adjectives, 43 nouns, 23 adverbs and 12 prepositions, with a handful of pronouns and particles. A few part-of-speech labels contain typos in the source file. Because the list covers 2013 to 2024, it includes the pre-LLM years' content words as well as the 2024 style words, so it must be filtered to style words before it is read as an LLM vocabulary.

Checking the word list in the revision pass of SKILL.md (item 8) against it gives these results. Words with at least one form on the authors' style list: delve (delve, delved, delves, delving), underscore (all four common forms), showcase (showcased, showcases, showcasing, but not the bare form), intricate (with intricately), pivotal, realm (with realms), meticulous (with meticulously), notably (and notable), additionally, crucial, comprehensive, multifaceted, nuanced (with nuances), seamless (with seamlessly), transformative, groundbreaking, enhance (enhance, enhanced, enhances, enhancing, enhancements), garner (garnered, garnering), surpass (four forms), commendable, innovative, and foster (fostering, fosters, but not the bare form). Leverage appears only as leverages and leveraging. Landscape appears, marked as both content and style because it also has a technical meaning.

The 291 rare style words printed in the paper's supplement (all below 2 percent frequency) include delve and its forms, the underscore forms, showcased, showcases, showcasing, intricate, intricately, pivotal, realm, realms, meticulous, meticulously, seamless, seamlessly, transformative, groundbreaking, garnered, garnering, surpass forms, commendable, innovative, multifaceted, nuanced, nuances, leverages, leveraging, fostering and fosters. Others in the skill's list (additionally, crucial, comprehensive, notably) are common words, and the paper puts additionally, comprehensive, crucial and notably in its common set of ten.

Words in the skill's list that do not appear in the authors' list at all: boast, tapestry, robust, vibrant, holistic, testament and journey. The skill's first version also listed embark and figurative navigate; both were removed in November 2026 because no reference in this folder showed them to be overused. Those words come from popular lists of machine-written vocabulary rather than from this measurement. They may well be overused by chat models, but this paper provides no evidence either way, because its corpus is abstracts and its list is limited to what rose measurably in them.

## What the skill applies

**Section 10, item 8 (overused vocabulary).** This is the rule the source shaped most. The list in the rule is built around words with a measured excess, and most of its entries (delve, underscore, showcase, intricate, pivotal, realm, meticulous, notably, additionally, crucial, comprehensive, multifaceted, enhance, garner, surpass, commendable, innovative, foster) appear in the authors' style list, which gave the rule an evidence base beyond anecdote. The paper also shaped the instruction to keep a flagged word only when it is the most precise word available. The authors show that the words cluster in verbs and adjectives that add emphasis, so a swap to a plain verb or a deletion is usually possible without losing content. The other items in the list (landscape, nuanced and the figurative uses) come from this paper only in part: landscape appears with a mixed label, and the figurative use of journey comes from other references.

**Section 2 (words).** The source supports the rule to use plain words and to cut intensifiers and vogue vocabulary. The 2024 excess is made of formal-sounding verbs and adjectives, so the paper gives a concrete picture of what over-formal word choice looks like and why readers notice it. The skill's preference for specific over inflated language is consistent with this finding, though the paper does not test readability.

**How to use this skill (placement of the list).** The paper does not address where a list belongs. The decision to keep the word list in the post-draft pass comes from one-token-away-from-collapse.md and taboo-negative-constraints.md. What the paper contributes is the reason the list exists at all and the reminder that the signature is statistical: one word proves nothing, but a cluster is visible to readers.

**Section 10, item 8 versus item 3.** The paper's finding that style-word excess is dominated by verbs is loosely consistent with the revision pass's attention to trailing commentary clauses (verb forms such as underscoring, ensuring and highlighting). That link is the skill authors' inference. The paper does not discuss clause structure.

## What the skill omits

The skill leaves out the prevalence estimates (13.5 percent, subgroup ranges, journal comparisons), since they describe scientific publishing and do not help an agent write a given reply. It also omits the method itself, the counterfactual construction, the group-gap computation and the COVID comparison, which are research design rather than writing guidance. The 900-word list is not copied wholesale. Most of it is content vocabulary, and the style portion includes many ordinary academic words (across, within, particularly, findings, potential) whose excess is real in abstracts but whose use in a reply is often the right choice. Banning them would damage ordinary prose. The skill therefore keeps only a short selection of the most distinctive words. The paper's social-science claims (gender, country, prestige) are left out as irrelevant and, for gender and country, as sensitive and weakly supported by approximate name-based inference. The paper's discussion of risks (fabricated references, homogenization, paper mills) and policy is outside what a prose style skill governs.

The skill also does not adopt the paper's frequency thresholds. It treats the list as a prompt to reconsider a word, not as a mechanical filter.

## Conflicts with other references

No direct conflict with the style manuals: NYT, AP and Chicago all advise plain words, and none of them rules on vocabulary at the word level, so this source supports rather than contradicts them. The Kobak list is a measured list from abstracts, and it overlaps strongly with the skill's list, but the two are not the same list.

The real tensions are with other empirical and popular sources. The file juzek-ward-lexical-overrepresentation.md and liang-ai-modified-peer-reviews.md use different corpora and measures (Kobak's paper itself describes Liang and colleagues' method as a mixture model of human and LLM word distributions calibrated on known LLM text, in contrast to its own trend-based excess measure). The measures are not comparable. Kobak measures a 2024 shift in published abstracts relative to a pre-LLM trend. I did not re-read the Juzek and Ward or Liang files for this review. Words that top one list can be middling in another, and the skill does not claim they agree. It uses their overlap as a signal and does not rank the lists. The file wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing.md is a popular, editor-built list that includes words absent from Kobak's measured list, as shown in the comparison above (tapestry, testament, vibrant, holistic, robust and others). The skill keeps those words because other evidence or editor experience supports them, but the evidence is weaker than for the words in the measured list.

A further tension is with one-token-away-from-collapse.md and taboo-negative-constraints.md, which found that bans given while drafting make a model write a thinner answer. This paper does not speak to that. Those findings are the reason the skill places the Kobak-derived list only in the revision pass. An earlier version of this file cited a claim that naming banned words primes them; that claim was dropped. The anthropic-prompting-docs.md file recommends positive instructions, and the skill follows that for the drafting stage and uses a list only afterward. Finally, freeburg-em-dashes.md and boggia-artificial-epanorthosis.md cover punctuation and rhetorical frames, which this word-based method cannot see, so the skill's dash and "not X, but Y" rules rest on those files and the user's explicit choices, not on this one.
