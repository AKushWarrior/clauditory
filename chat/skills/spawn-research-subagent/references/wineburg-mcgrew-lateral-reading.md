# Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information

- **Source:** Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew, "Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information," Teachers College Record 121(11), pages 1 to 40, November 2019. DOI 10.1177/016146811912101102. An earlier version circulated as Stanford History Education Group Working Paper No. 2017.A1, September 2017 (SSRN abstract 3048994).
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed paper (an exploratory expert and novice study of human participants). It makes no claims about AI systems, so the repository's recency rule for AI-performance sources does not apply to it.
- **Where to find it:** https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102 (journal version); https://purl.stanford.edu/yk133ht8603 (Stanford Digital Repository); https://ssrn.com/abstract=3048994 (2017 working paper)
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05

## What the source covers

The study compares how three groups judged the credibility of online information: 10 history professors with Ph.D.s, 10 professional fact checkers from news and political fact-checking organizations, and 25 first-year Stanford undergraduates. Each participant spent about 45 minutes on six tasks with live websites and thought aloud while researchers recorded the screen. Each task had a time limit. The paper reports three tasks. A footnote in the working paper says the other three gave broadly consistent results but does not report them.

### Tasks

1. **Bullying (10 minutes).** Compare an article from the American Academy of Pediatrics, the main professional body, with one from the American College of Pediatricians, a small splinter group with contested positions on LGBT issues whose site looks official and scientific.
2. **Minimum wage (up to 8 minutes).** Judge minimumwage.com, a project of the Employment Policies Institute, which presents itself as a nonpartisan research nonprofit but is run by a Washington public relations firm working for restaurant and hotel interests. Participants who hadn't found the institute after five minutes were prompted.
3. **Vergara v. California (5 minutes).** Search the open web for who paid the more than $1 million in legal fees for nine student plaintiffs in a teacher-tenure lawsuit. The answer is a Silicon Valley entrepreneur, David Welch.

Conclusions were scored with rubrics the authors developed through pilot testing. Two coders scored about a quarter of the data and agreed 92 percent of the time (Cohen's kappa 0.90). Group differences were tested with Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney tests.

### Findings

- **Bullying.** Every fact checker judged the Academy the more reliable source and scored the maximum. On the College site, historians averaged 0.7 out of 2 and students 0.16. Fact checkers left the College's landing page after 32 seconds on average to look it up elsewhere, against about 88 seconds for historians and 100 seconds for students; nine students never left. Seven of 10 historians cited the College's reference list as a reason to trust it, and 60 percent of students picked the College as more reliable, mostly for its look, name, and logo.
- **Minimum wage.** Before prompting, fact checkers averaged 3.3 on a 0 to 4 rubric, historians 1.3 and students 0.52. Fact checkers found the parent institute in 51 seconds on average without help; most historians and about four in five students needed the prompt. All 10 fact checkers traced the sites to the public relations firm after visiting about six sites each, against six historians and 40 percent of students. One fact checker went from the site to the institute, searched it, chose a watchdog wiki over the top results, then followed the wiki's citation to a public radio story and searched it to confirm the claim.
- **Vergara.** Students reached Welch's name fastest (about 1 minute 42 seconds, against about 2 minutes for both expert groups), but fact checkers scored highest on quality: 3.6 out of 4, against 2.4 for historians and 2.3 for students. The top score required finding Welch through a well-established outlet and confirming him with another source. Only the fact checker versus student difference was significant. The paper contrasts a student who accepted Welch's name from a partisan teachers' union site with a fact checker who read the results page for nearly 20 seconds before clicking, skipped the results tied to either party, and confirmed Welch in a second outlet.

From these cases the authors name three practices: taking bearings (finding out what a source is before reading it closely), lateral reading (leaving a site to learn what other sources say about it, as opposed to vertical reading, which judges a site from its own features), and click restraint (reading the whole results list and its snippets before clicking). They argue that the cues most participants used, such as domain, logo, nonprofit status, scientific formatting, and references, are weak heuristics, and that the checklists common in media-literacy teaching direct attention to exactly those cues.

### Limits

The study is small and descriptive. Ten people per expert group were recruited by convenience, and the authors concede that small samples exaggerate differences; two of the 10 historians behaved like fact checkers. Two of the three tasks used sites built to hide their sponsors, and the third was a who-paid question, so the tasks favor a method that finds out who is behind a source. The study does not test claims whose truth depends on the evidence rather than on its sponsor. The Vergara rubric scored corroboration as quality, so the finding that experts corroborate is partly built into the measure. The setting was artificial, and the study cannot show that the practices caused the better results. In the 2017 working paper, the Kruskal-Wallis statistic printed for Vergara repeats the figure from the bullying task and looks too large for the reported means, which suggests a transcription error.

A later study by the same group gives causal evidence that lateral reading can be taught: Wineburg, Breakstone, McGrew, Smith, and Ortega, Journal of Educational Psychology 114(5), 893 to 909, 2022 (DOI 10.1037/edu0000740), taught six one-hour lessons over three months in high school government classes in an urban district (271 treatment students, 228 control) and reported significantly greater growth in credibility judgments for the treatment group. This review verified those details from Crossref and the SSRN abstract only, not the full paper; effect sizes are unverified.

### Transfer to research subagents

The participants were people using browsers. A subagent reads retrieved text, which carries its own surface cues: analytic register, citations, credentials, an organization's self-description, and search rank. Those correspond to the cues that misled historians and students, and recent AI work (see the conflicts section) finds models taking similar shortcuts. The study cannot show that a one-line instruction makes a small model read laterally. Showing that would need a test on models.

## What the skill applies

The study shaped the sentences on sources in the paragraph of the template that covers search conduct.

- **Judge sources by provenance and method: who produced the claim and how they know it.** Asking who is behind a source is the study's central finding. The second part, how the source knows the claim, goes beyond this study. Fact checkers largely skipped content, and historians who examined an article's apparent method were misled by its look. The method clause rests on general research practice, and the study warns that method can be judged by appearance.
- **Treat polish, popularity and authority cues as weak signals.** Polish and authority cues map directly onto the weak cues the study documented: design, logos, domains, nonprofit status, scientific formatting, credentials, and reference lists. Popularity has only partial support here. Students trusted top-ranked search results and fact checkers knew rankings can be gamed, which is the point of click restraint, but the study never measured popularity. That part rests mainly on Ashkinaze et al. (arXiv 2607.19355), a reference behind the system prompt, which found that models weigh a source's popularity about twice as much as its reliability.
- **Weigh each source on its own record instead of distrusting all of them equally.** The study supports this indirectly. Fact checkers were selective, not uniformly skeptical. Every one of them trusted the Academy without reservation once its standing was clear, and they used Wikipedia and a watchdog wiki as stepping stones while checking those against news outlets. The sentence was added mainly to answer Pradhan (see the conflicts section), and the fact checkers are the human model of the selective trust it asks for.
- **Confirm load-bearing claims with an independent second source where possible.** The fact checkers in the minimum wage and Vergara tasks did this, and the Vergara rubric rewarded it. As noted above, the study defined quality partly by corroboration, so it shows that experts corroborate more than it proves corroboration made them accurate.
- **Step 3 of "How" (the lead agent opens the cited primary source).** This rule's main evidence is AI work on citation accuracy, but the fact checker who followed a watchdog wiki's citation into the cited story is a human example of the same check.

The template's source ranking, which puts peer-reviewed and official primary documents first and treats journalism as pointers to primary sources, is not from this study and sits in some tension with it (see below).

## What the skill omits

- **Click restraint.** The template does not ask the subagent to read the full results list and snippets before opening pages. It would plausibly help, but the concept rests on examples from one task and was never counted, and the template is kept short for a small model.
- **Lateral reading as an explicit step.** The template says to judge who produced a claim but not how to find out. It does not tell the subagent to search for what independent sources say about an unfamiliar publisher, a clause the system prompt includes. That step is the study's main lesson, and its absence is the largest remaining gap between the study and the skill. The lead agent's check in step 3 partly makes up for it.
- **Search technique, knowledge of particular sources, and the critique of checklists.** All three are left to the subagent model's own skills and knowledge.

## Conflicts with other references

Of the files cited here, only `claude-code-subagents.md` existed in `chat/skills/spawn-research-subagent/references/` when this file was written; the other entries work from the local full texts of the sources.

- **Claude Code subagents documentation (`claude-code-subagents.md`).** That file notes the practical tension between the judgment that lateral reading takes and the documentation's cost guidance, which points to the smallest model, and records that the skill makes the lead agent's own check the safeguard. This file agrees. That file also says the Wineburg and McGrew full text was not available when it was written; the working paper has since been read in full, and nothing in it changes that file's summary of the findings.
- **Pradhan and Goley, "Trust, but Don't Verify" (`pradhan-trust-dont-verify.md`, arXiv 2606.05403).** Pradhan and Goley find that models, Haiku 4.5 among them, weigh fabricated statistics like valid ones when the source reads as careful analysis, and that verification prompts, even a checklist naming the exact anomalies, make models distrust valid sources along with invalid ones. The human study shows the same shortcut in historians, who trusted the College because it looked like science. The remedies point different ways: the study suggests checking a source from outside it, while Pradhan suggests that a scrutiny instruction can produce blanket skepticism. The template answers this by asking the subagent to weigh each source on its own record instead of distrusting all of them equally, which describes the fact checkers' selective trust. No test shows that the sentence produces selective trust in a model. Pradhan found that even an oracle checklist failed to, so a short instruction to a Haiku subagent might still slide into blanket skepticism. The lead agent's check in step 3 stays the safeguard.
- **The template's source ranking.** The fixed order of source types (peer-reviewed, official, reviews, journalism, preprints, other web) is a category rule, a lighter version of the checklists the study criticized. In the study, fact checkers used Wikipedia and a watchdog wiki, which would rank as other web, as fast routes to provenance and then confirmed in news outlets. The template permits this by treating lower-ranked sources as pointers and requiring labels, not exclusion, so the conflict is one of emphasis.
- **Onweller et al., "Cited but Not Verified" (`onweller-cited-not-verified.md`, arXiv 2605.06635).** Onweller finds that deep research agents' citations support their claims only 39 to 77 percent of the time, and less as tool calls grow. That result agrees with the study's picture of experts who read less and check more, and it is the direct evidence for step 3. No conflict.
- **"Scaling Inference-Time Compute with Teams of Coding Agents" (`coding-agent-teams.md`, arXiv 2610.17471).** That paper concerns how to allocate compute across coding-agent teams and says nothing about source evaluation. It has no tension with this study.
