# Scaling Inference-Time Compute with Teams of Coding Agents

- **Source:** Keenan Samway, Daniel D'souza, Omar Darwiche Domingues, Marzieh Fadaee, Tan Yi-Chern and Nouha Dziri (Cohere Labs and Cohere), "Scaling Inference-Time Compute with Teams of Coding Agents," arXiv preprint arXiv:2610.17471, first version 12 October 2026.
- **Type:** Preprint (not peer-reviewed), from an industry research lab
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17471
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/spawn-research-subagent/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

The paper asks where extra inference spending should go once a single coding agent is no longer enough. It treats team design as a budget problem: starting from one agent, the next dollar can buy a stronger lead, stronger or cheaper workers, more workers, or workers aimed at one stage of the job (planning, implementation or verification). The authors build a small lead-and-worker harness, change one design choice at a time and trace which configurations give the most reward per added dollar. The main result is that teams beat solo agents but cost three to seven times as much, and that most of that extra spending is wasted unless it goes to the right stage and the right model.

### Method

The harness is deliberately minimal. Every agent has a bash tool and runs a reason-then-act loop. The lead receives the task and has one delegation tool that either spawns a fresh worker with its own context or resumes an earlier worker with its history intact. A worker sees only the subtask the lead wrote, never the full task or other workers' conversations, and its last message comes back to the lead as its report. The lead either waits for each worker (blocking) or keeps working while workers run and absorbs their reports as they arrive (asynchronous). By default no messages pass while a worker is running; one experiment adds a messaging tool. The lead could spawn at most five distinct workers unless a study changed the cap.

Two prompting regimes frame the lead. Under minimal guidance it is told only to give workers non-conflicting tasks and how the tools behave. Under stage guidance it is told to run a plan, implement and verify workflow and to use workers only in one named stage, or in all three (called PIV).

The main benchmark is ProgramBench: 200 tasks in which the agent gets a black-box executable and its documentation and must rebuild a compatible program, scored against hidden behavioral tests. The behavior has to be discovered by probing the executable, so the specification itself is part of the work. A secondary benchmark, Commit0-Lite, gives 16 Python libraries to reimplement from a supplied skeleton of files, stubs and unit tests. The authors report it only in an appendix because it has few tasks and is close to saturated.

The main models are three tiers of one OpenAI family, which the paper calls GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra and Luna, all at medium reasoning effort. Luna's listed per-token price is about one twentieth of Sol's. Appendix runs add Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Opus 4.8 and two Gemini Flash models. The efficiency measure is reward gained per $100 of added cost relative to the solo agent given the same guidance. The design search is coordinate-wise (stage first, then models, then team size), so the reported configurations are good operating points that may not be optimal.

### Findings

**Teams win, at a price.** Homogeneous Sol teams beat a solo Sol agent under both guidance regimes. A solo agent made about 20 tool calls per task; teams made 44 to 140. Blocking teams took about 3.5 to 3.8 times as long as the solo agent. Asynchronous teams were faster, but under minimal guidance they scored about 6 points lower than blocking teams, because the lead often acted on partial results or finished before its workers did. Telling the lead to collect every report first closed that gap. Gains were largest on easy tasks and smaller on hard ones.

**Planning is where workers pay.** When the lead delegated only exploration and planning, then wrote and checked the code itself, it matched the reward of a lead that delegated all three stages at about half the cost. Planning returned 3.8 reward points per $100 added, against at most 2.4 for any other stage and 1.4 for full delegation. The full-delegation lead issued about the same number of delegation calls before the first edit as the planning-only lead, then about 830 more during implementation and verification that added no reward. The authors tie this to the task: probing a black box parallelizes well. On Commit0, where the skeleton already supplies the decomposition, implementation was the most useful stage to delegate in eight of ten settings, and planning was never clearly best. The best stage to delegate therefore depends on which part of the task is the bottleneck.

**Cheap models work as information gatherers and fail as implementers.** With a Sol lead and Luna workers, planning guidance raised reward and cut cost relative to full delegation. A strong lead with cheap planners recovered 47.5 percent of the reward gain of an all-Sol team for 13.9 percent of its added cost, and each dollar spent on Luna planners bought about 13 times the reward of a dollar spent on Sol planners. Aimed at implementation, the same Luna workers pushed reward below the solo Sol agent while raising cost, so each added dollar lowered reward. Aimed at verification, they left reward about where it was. The authors' reading is that cheap workers help when they collect facts for a strong lead to act on, and hurt when they produce code the lead has to integrate.

**The lead sets the ceiling.** Holding workers at Luna and upgrading the lead from Luna to Terra raised reward by about 21 percent, and from Terra to Sol by a further 15 percent. A Terra lead with Luna workers still trailed a solo Sol agent by about 10 percent. Cheap workers lower the cost of approaching the lead's ceiling but cannot lift it.

**More workers help only in planning.** With a Sol lead and 2 to 16 Luna workers, planning workers improved reward at every size, and 16 of them came within 1.4 points of an all-Sol team at 70 percent lower cost. Implementers and verifiers improved from two to four and then flattened. Collisions were rare (implementers wrote the same file in 0.1 percent of cases). The cause was dilution: each planner probed the executable about seven times regardless of team size, so information grew with the team, while extra implementers split a fixed plan into smaller pieces, shifted more of the code to the weaker model and left the lead's final check unchanged. Raising the default cap from five to 20 workers for lead-sized teams changed reward by at most 1.6 points either way and raised cost by 5 to 17 percent in seven of ten settings.

**Messaging does not help.** With a messaging tool, 84 percent of messages went from workers to the lead, and workers rarely talked to each other even when told to collaborate. Stronger models sent more text, but reward did not rise for Sol or Terra teams and fell by 4.4 to 7.1 points for Luna teams. About half of Luna's messaging calls were malformed.

**Claude and Gemini behave differently.** On the full benchmark (single runs), Claude Opus 4.7 almost never delegated under minimal guidance and so matched the solo agent; under full stage guidance it gained 4 to 6 points at 1.7 to 2.0 times the solo cost. Claude Haiku 4.5 delegated readily, yet every team configuration scored at or below the solo Haiku agent, at about twice the cost. On a 30-task subset, Claude Opus 4.8 delegated and gained 5.9 points at 2.3 times the solo cost. The authors conclude that extra workers help only when the lead both chooses to delegate and can use what comes back.

### Strength of the evidence

The core GPT results are solid for what they measure. Each main configuration ran three times on all 200 tasks, the benchmark-level mean varied by at most 0.014 between runs, and the comparisons use intervals paired by task. Individual task scores were noisy (two runs of one configuration differed by 0.06 to 0.11 per task on average), so single-task stories in the paper carry less weight than the averages. Several pieces are thinner: the fixed-size team sweep, the raised-cap study and every Claude result are single runs, and the Commit0 stage reversal rests on 16 tasks with wide intervals. The heterogeneous results use one model family, so the cheap-worker findings show the behavior of a 20-to-1 price gap within GPT-5.6 and say nothing direct about other pairs. No mid-tier worker was ever placed under a strong lead. The paper is a preprint from a lab that builds models, and it reports no safety or error-propagation measures.

### Transfer to a chat agent following a system prompt

The paper studies autonomous coding with a hidden test suite. A chat agent's subagents are closer to its planning workers when they research, and closer to its implementers when they edit files or run code for the user. The planning result transfers best in spirit: cheap agents can gather facts when a strong agent decides what to do with them and checks the result. The caution is that ProgramBench planners probed a program that always tells the truth, while research subagents read sources of uneven reliability, so a cheap worker's error is harder to catch in research than in probing. The Claude results come from older models (Opus 4.7, Opus 4.8 and Haiku 4.5) and from single runs; current Claude models may delegate and integrate differently.

## What the skill applies

The paper is the only source behind the skill with measured quality data on cheap subagents. Its strongest result, that cheap workers pay off when they gather information for a strong lead, is close to what the skill does. It never tested the skill's exact arrangement: a strong Claude lead with Haiku workers doing web research.

- **Haiku subagents for research (How, step 1).** The skill sends every sub-question to a Haiku subagent and calls Haiku the speed and cost lever. The paper's cheap planners are the closest analogue: Luna workers that only explored and reported to a Sol lead bought about 13 times the reward per dollar of Sol workers in the same role, and recovered 47.5 percent of an all-strong team's gain for 13.9 percent of its added cost. The gap between source and skill is wide in three places. The paper's cheap model is about one twentieth of the strong model's per-token price; the price gap between Haiku and the lead depends on which models the harness serves. The paper's only Haiku data come from all-Haiku teams (Haiku 4.5 as lead and workers), which never beat a solo Haiku agent. And the paper's planners probed a program that always answers truthfully, while research subagents read sources of uneven reliability.
- **One subagent per sub-question, all sent together (How, step 1).** Planning workers were the one role where adding workers kept helping, from 2 up to 16, because each one gathered new information. That supports fanning out to many research subagents when the sub-questions are distinct. Two qualifications apply. The 16-worker run was a single run, and when the lead sized its own team, raising the cap from five to 20 added cost without reward.
- **The lead checks load-bearing claims itself (How, step 3).** The paper found that the lead sets the team's ceiling: cheap workers lowered the cost of reaching it but couldn't raise it, and a mid-tier lead with cheap workers stayed below a strong agent working alone. In the planning setup the lead also wrote the code and ran its own final checks. Both findings support keeping verification and synthesis with the strong main agent and treating Haiku reports as leads to check. The skill's stated reason for step 3 (reports can misattribute sources) comes from other sources; this paper's support is structural.
- **Act once the reports return (How, step 3).** The skill tells the main agent to check claims when the reports return. The paper found that an asynchronous lead acting on partial results, or finishing before its workers did, lost about 6 points, and that telling it to collect every report first closed the gap. The skill's wording implies waiting for all reports without stating it.
- **A fixed report format and no channel between subagents (Template).** Each subagent returns one report in a set shape, and subagents don't talk to each other or to the lead mid-task. The paper's messaging experiment supports this design: messaging didn't raise reward for strong teams, cut it by up to 7 points for cheap-worker teams, and about half of the cheap model's messaging calls were malformed.
- **Reasoning effort left at default (How, step 1).** The paper ran every model at medium effort and didn't vary it, so it gives no evidence either way.

## What the skill omits

- **The failure of cheap workers as implementers.** Cheap workers that wrote code lowered reward below the solo agent. The skill is limited to research, so the omission is correct; the system prompt sends operational work to Sonnet instead.
- **Task dependence.** The paper's best stage to delegate changed with the benchmark: planning on ProgramBench, implementation on Commit0. The skill's trigger (independent sub-questions or heavy reading) is a reasonable proxy for tasks where discovery is the bottleneck, but the paper doesn't test it.
- **Single-fact lookups.** The skill's rule to skip subagents for single facts rests on its own timing test. The paper's nearest finding, that team gains were largest on easy tasks, concerns difficulty, not task size, and doesn't bear on the rule.
- **Cost and latency figures.** Teams cost three to seven times a solo agent and took up to 3.8 times as long. The skill states the cost of one subagent from its own test and doesn't cite these figures.
- **Spawn caps.** The paper's raised-cap result suggests limiting how many subagents a lead spawns when it chooses the number itself. The skill ties the number to the sub-questions and sets no cap.

## Conflicts with other references

When this file was drafted, only `claude-code-subagents.md` existed in this skill's references folder. Before finishing, I also read the relevant passages of the Onweller, Pradhan and Wineburg files that other reviewers had added to the folder.

- **Claude Code subagents documentation (`claude-code-subagents.md`).** The page presents Haiku as a way to cut cost and latency and makes no claim about the quality of its answers. This paper adds the quality evidence the page lacks, split by role: a cheap model was effective at gathering information for a strong lead and harmful at writing code. That split supports the skill's use of Haiku for research only.
- **Cited but not verified (`onweller-cited-not-verified.md`, arXiv 2605.06635).** That paper evaluates whether citations in deep-research reports lead to working, relevant and supporting sources, and starts from the premise that such citations can't be trusted as written. This paper's planners reported on a program that couldn't mislead them, so its finding that cheap gatherers are good value assumes reports a lead can trust or cheaply check. The skill's step 3 closes that gap by having the lead open every load-bearing source. That paper also measured Claude Haiku 4.5 directly: its citations supported the claim 69 percent of the time, second among 14 models and above every Sonnet model tested, though it completed fewer queries than any other frontier model. Read together, the two papers suggest Haiku is a reasonable research worker whose reports still need the lead's check. I read that paper's abstract and its reference file in this folder, not its full text.
- **Trust, but don't verify (`pradhan-trust-dont-verify.md`, arXiv 2606.05403).** That paper reports that models can spot fabricated statistics in isolation but give them the same weight as valid ones when synthesizing several sources. A cheap subagent synthesizing many pages could pass along such numbers with confidence. The skill's step 3 and the template's demand for the exact sentence behind each key figure are the guards; this paper offers no evidence on them. Its reference file in this folder reports that Haiku 4.5 was the least swayed of the Claude models tested, which again points to the lead's check as the safeguard more than the choice of subagent model. I read that paper's abstract and its reference file, not its full text.
- **Wineburg and McGrew, lateral reading (`wineburg-mcgrew-lateral-reading.md`).** The study is about how human fact-checkers judge sources and makes no claim about delegation, so it doesn't conflict with this paper.
