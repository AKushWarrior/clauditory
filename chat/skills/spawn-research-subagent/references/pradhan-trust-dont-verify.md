# Trust, but Don't Verify: Epistemic Blind Spots in LLM Source Evaluation

- **Source:** Rohan N. Pradhan and Steve Goley, "Trust, but Don't Verify: Epistemic Blind Spots in LLM Source Evaluation," Amazon, arXiv preprint, first posted June 3, 2026, revised July 8, 2026, arXiv 2606.05403 (version 2 read)
- **Type:** preprint (not peer-reviewed; the paper marks itself as under review)
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05403
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/spawn-research-subagent/SKILL.md` (a companion file at `chat/system/references/pradhan-trust-dont-verify.md` documents the same source for the system prompt)

## What the source covers

The paper asks whether language models judge the quality of evidence when they combine several sources, or whether they respond to how the evidence looks. Its answer is that they respond to how it looks. Models can spot a statistically impossible figure when they review it alone, yet the same models give an impossible figure almost the same weight as a valid one when it sits among other sources. The authors trace the behavior to an internal signal that tracks whether text reads like competent analysis and ignores whether its numbers are internally consistent. They also test three system-prompt fixes and find that none of them makes the models selective: the prompts that lowered trust in fabricated numbers lowered trust in valid numbers too.

### Design

Each trial is a workplace chat thread in which four sources give conflicting estimates of one quantity: a senior colleague, an outside institution, an internal analyst, and a third party. The model is asked for a single number. There are three domains: customer retention for a venture deal, return on ad spend for a marketing campaign, and disease prevalence for a county health department. The internal analyst is the focal source, and only that message varies. It comes in six forms. The first is a bare claim. The next three are a plausible method with valid statistics, the same method with the statistics removed, and the same method with statistics that cannot be true given the stated design. The last two are a method that uses real but inappropriate statistical jargon, with fit statistics, and the same jargon without them.

The impossible statistics differ from the valid ones by a few characters. In the venture case, a 95 percent confidence interval spans 0.06 percentage points on 2,400 accounts, which would need a sample in the millions. In marketing, a p-value sits below the smallest value a permutation test over 14 regions can produce. In public health, a correlation exceeds what the measurement reliability of the data allows. The three cases form a difficulty ladder, from one that arithmetic alone exposes to two that need specialist knowledge.

The design crosses presentation with social support (zero to three other sources agreeing with the analyst), with disclosed or undisclosed conflicts of interest, and with a seniority title on the senior source. That gives 2,304 conditions per domain, sampled 20 times each, for more than a million trials in total. The measure of influence is the Source Preference Index, borrowed from studies of advice-taking: zero means the model's estimate tracks the consensus of the other sources and one means it tracks the analyst.

Six models from four families were tested: Claude Opus 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Haiku 4.5, Qwen3 32B, OLMo 3.1 32B Think, and GPT-5.4. Thinking was on by default, and the Claude and Qwen models were also run with it off. Four post-training checkpoints of OLMo 3 were run to see where the behavior comes from.

### Behavioral findings

**Detection works in isolation.** Shown the analyst's claim alone with a prompt to review its statistics, every model criticized every claim, valid ones included, so the authors score whether the model named the specific flaw. Every model caught the impossible interval in the venture case at rates of 95 to 100 percent and caught the inappropriate jargon at high rates. The marketing p-value was almost never caught (0 to 23 percent), and the public-health correlation was caught by most models but only 20 percent of the time by GPT-5.4.

**Detection fails in synthesis.** In the four-source thread, with the analyst as the lone dissenter, a plausible method pulled every model most of the way toward the analyst. Impossible statistics added about 74 percent of the extra pull that valid statistics added. In the authors' reading, precise numbers work as a credibility cue whether or not they hang together. Inappropriate jargon, in contrast, earned little more weight than a bare claim. So the models do discount a method that reads as wrong; what they miss is numbers that contradict a method that reads as right.

**The models report the fabricated source as credible.** When asked which source they would rely on most, without producing an estimate, the models picked the analyst with impossible statistics 76 to 100 percent of the time. They named it least trustworthy no more than 10 percent of the time. This rules out the idea that the effect is only numeric anchoring.

**The reasoning flags the problem and endorses the source anyway.** A model-based judge read the chains of thought. In the venture case, the models questioned the impossible interval in 12 to 29 percent of trials; in the other two domains, mostly under 7 percent. When a model did question it, it went on to endorse the source in roughly half to nine-tenths of cases, depending on model and domain. One Sonnet 4.5 trace in the paper calls the interval suspicious and then gives the analyst heavy weight in the same answer.

**Turning thinking off or on changes nothing about the blind spot.** For each model that could run both ways, trust in impossible statistics stayed within 0.03 of trust in valid statistics in both modes.

**Authority labels and conflict disclosures barely mattered.** A disclosed conflict of interest moved the index by about 0.02 on average. A seniority title moved it by essentially zero. The presentation effect was about 0.45. What moved these models was the register of the analyst's message. Who was speaking and what they stood to gain hardly registered.

**Post-training does not build the check.** Across the OLMo 3 checkpoints, from supervised fine-tuning through preference tuning and two rounds of reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards, the model got better at discounting inappropriate jargon (0.44 to 0.61 on the authors' contrast). Its discrimination between valid and impossible numbers stayed near zero (0.02 to 0.06).

### Prompting mitigations

The three fixes were appended to the system prompt, with the analyst isolated. A generic instruction to think carefully and evaluate all sources critically had no measurable effect. A statistical instruction told the model to check that each source's numbers are internally consistent and flag any that are not. An oracle checklist named the three kinds of anomaly in the experiment: precision against sample size, effect sizes beyond known ceilings, and fit between method and design. It told the model to downweight failures regardless of how authoritative a source sounds. The authors call the checklist a ceiling test, since a real deployment would not know the anomalies in advance.

Their summary is that no prompt produced selective discernment: every model that trusted fabricated numbers less also trusted valid numbers less. The table behind that summary is more mixed than the summary, and the difference matters for this repository. Under the checklist, Opus 4.5 cut its trust in fabricated statistics by 0.39 and in valid statistics by 0.18. Sonnet 4.5 cut them by 0.45 and 0.30. Both opened a gap between valid and fabricated evidence while losing ground on valid evidence. Under the realistic statistical instruction, Opus 4.5 cut trust in fabricated numbers by 0.24 and in valid numbers by only 0.05, which is close to the selective behavior the authors say no model showed. The other models either moved both together or moved the wrong way; Qwen3 and OLMo trusted valid numbers more under the checklist. The table reports point estimates without intervals, so these differences are suggestive and unconfirmed. The fair reading is that verification prompts mostly bought skepticism across the board, with the strongest Claude model the partial exception.

### Mechanism

The authors probed two open models, Qwen3 32B and OLMo 3.1 32B, with simple linear probes trained on one domain and tested on another. The probes separated sound from inappropriate method text well, with areas under the curve of 0.83 and 0.92 at an early layer. They separated valid from impossible numbers only at chance (0.51 and 0.56). Within a single domain and in isolation, the numeric-validity signal was decodable and partly transferred across domains (about 0.87). In the four-source setting it fell to chance. Causal tracing on Qwen3 found that the method text drives the estimate through early layers. Agreement from other sources acts like a volume knob on that signal: with a majority agreeing, the presentation tokens lost almost all their influence. Component-level attribution found no part of the network that pushes back against impossible numbers. The mechanistic work covers two open models only and uses a pre-reasoning setup. It cannot rule out signals the probes miss, and the authors say so.

### Strength of the evidence

The behavioral core is strong. The factorial design isolates presentation, the impossible and valid conditions share identical method text, the sample is very large, and the result replicates across six models, three domains, and both reasoning modes. The source-choice experiment closes off the anchoring explanation. The judges were checked against an author's labels on 100 trials per task. Agreement was good on endorsement and on isolation detection (kappa 0.74 and 0.90) and moderate on questioning (0.46). The authors argue the detection rates are lower bounds.

The limits are real. The task is narrow: one thread structure, one numeric estimate, synthetic sources with no history, and a fabrication that lives entirely in internal arithmetic. Real research involves sources with track records, links, and outside commentary, which give a model other ways to judge them. The mitigation study is three prompts, one wording each, with no confidence intervals in the main table, and it is the least developed part of the paper. The authors' headline on blanket skepticism is stronger than their own numbers for Opus 4.5. The dual-process framing and the account of why training builds the shortcut are the authors' interpretations; the paper does not test them.

### Transfer to a frontier model following a system prompt

Transfer is better than for most papers in this area, because the mitigations sat in the system prompt and the models include recent Claude models. The gaps are generation and task. The newest Claude model tested is Opus 4.5, which is older than the models this prompt targets. The one model that came close to selective discernment was the most capable one, so a newer model may do better than the paper's averages suggest. The direction should carry over. Analytical register and numeric precision are likely to read as credibility. A model that can catch an error in isolation may still pass it during synthesis. And generic or checklist verification prompts risk lowering trust in good evidence as much as in bad. How large these effects are for a current model in open-ended research, with real sources it can look up, is untested.

## What the skill applies

**Judge sources by provenance and method, and treat polish, popularity, and authority cues as weak signals (Template, third paragraph).** The template tells each subagent to judge sources by who produced a claim and how they know it, and to give little weight to polish, popularity, and authority cues. The paper supports the polish part directly: analytical register and precise-looking numbers won trust whether or not the numbers could be true. The method half needs a caveat. The paper's models did judge method, in the sense that they discounted inappropriate jargon, but what they rewarded was method that reads as sound. None of them checked whether the reported figures could come from the stated method. A subagent told to judge method may do the same thing, so the instruction guards against one failure the paper found and leaves the central one open. The authority part gets little from this paper, because seniority titles and disclosed conflicts barely moved the models to begin with, and the paper did not test popularity.

**Weigh each source on its own record instead of distrusting all of them equally (Template, third paragraph).** The template ends its source rules with this clause, as a counterweight to the blanket skepticism the paper found: the prompts that lowered trust in fabricated numbers lowered trust in valid numbers too. The system prompt carries a matching sentence for the lead. The paper did not test an instruction like this one, and its oracle checklist, which was far more specific, still failed to make most models selective. The clause states the right goal, but nothing shows that a Haiku subagent follows it, so step 3 stays the safeguard.

**Check every load-bearing claim against the primary source (How, step 3).** This check is the skill's strongest use of the paper, though the support is indirect. The paper's central contrast is that a model catches an impossible figure when it reviews the claim alone and misses it when the claim sits among other sources. Step 3 has the lead open each cited source and check each load-bearing claim one at a time, which is closer to the isolation condition than to synthesis. The lead also runs on the main conversation's model, which is likely stronger than the subagent. The gap is in what step 3 checks: whether the source says what the report claims. A fabricated or impossible statistic that the subagent quoted faithfully passes that test. Catching it requires the lead to ask whether the source's numbers could be true, which the step does not ask.

**Quote the exact sentence for key figures (Template, second paragraph).** Asking subagents to quote the sentence that carries a key figure gives the lead the number and its context in one place, which makes an isolated check cheap. The paper did not test this. It follows from the detection-in-isolation finding only as a design inference.

**Haiku subagents with a stronger lead (How, step 1, and step 3).** The paper tested Claude Haiku 4.5, so its numbers bear on the skill's subagent model directly. That assumes the haiku alias resolves to that model or a close successor, which I did not verify. Haiku 4.5 was the least swayed of the Claude models by the lone analyst overall, with an index of about 0.39 against about 0.65 for Opus and Sonnet. It was no better at telling valid numbers from impossible ones. It caught none of the marketing fabrications in isolation. It questioned the impossible figures in synthesis in under 7 percent of trials outside the easiest domain. And it barely responded to either verification prompt. The skill's design already assumes subagents will pass along what they read and puts the check in the lead, which is the right response to this evidence.

**List conflicts between sources (How, step 4).** In the paper, agreement among sources turned down the influence of a dissenting source almost completely, whatever its quality. Asking the lead to list conflicts keeps a lone dissenting source visible to the reader instead of letting it be averaged away. That reading is mine; the paper did not test conflict reporting.

**Independent second source (Template, third paragraph).** The template asks for an independent second source on load-bearing claims. The paper shows models already weight consensus heavily, which is different from independent confirmation. The word "independent" carries the distinction, and the paper does not test it.

**No checklist of named checks (Template).** The template gives a source ranking and general rules but no list of statistical checks. That fits the paper's finding that even an oracle checklist mostly lowered trust in valid and fabricated evidence alike.

## What the skill omits

**A check that numbers fit the stated method.** Neither the template nor step 3 asks whether reported figures could follow from the method described, which is the paper's exact failure. The paper's own evidence argues against putting such a check in the subagent brief. A nearly identical instruction lowered Haiku 4.5's trust in impossible figures by only 0.04. If the check belongs anywhere, it belongs in step 3, where the stronger lead model reviews each figure alone. The skill leaves it out to keep step 3 short; that is the clearest gap this paper exposes.

**The mechanism and the training account.** The probe, tracing and post-training results explain the failure but give a skill nothing further to act on.

## Conflicts with other references

Of the other sources behind the skill, only `claude-code-subagents.md` existed in `chat/skills/spawn-research-subagent/references/` when this file was written. For the rest I worked from the sources: the abstract of Onweller et al. and the abstract of the coding-agent teams paper, read locally. I did not read Wineburg and McGrew.

**Claude Code subagents documentation (`claude-code-subagents.md`).** The documentation treats Haiku as a lever for cost and speed and makes no claim about quality. This paper adds evidence on the quality side for one narrow task: Haiku 4.5 did no better than larger models at spotting impossible figures in synthesis and responded least to verification prompts. The two do not conflict. Together they say the subagent choice saves money and should be expected to pass along well-presented errors, which is why step 3 matters.

**Cited but not verified (`onweller-cited-not-verified.md`, arXiv 2605.06635).** Onweller et al. found that frontier deep research reports had working, relevant links but cited pages that supported the claim only 39 to 77 percent of the time. That failure is misreporting a source; this paper's failure is misjudging a source read correctly. Step 3 as written catches the first and not the second. The two papers together argue that the lead's check should cover both whether the source says it and whether what it says could be right.

**Teams of coding agents (`coding-agent-teams.md`, arXiv 2610.17471).** That study found that a lead which delegated planning to cheap workers and did verification itself matched a team that delegated verification, at half the cost. This paper's isolation finding gives a reason the arrangement might work for research too: checking is more reliable when one model reviews a claim on its own than when it absorbs the claim during synthesis. The link is my inference, since neither paper tests research verification by a lead.

**Wineburg and McGrew (`wineburg-mcgrew-lateral-reading.md`).** From the study's widely reported findings, professional fact-checkers judged unfamiliar sites by leaving them and checking what others said about the source. The template's provenance rule draws on that. This paper's fabrications could only be caught from the content, because its sources had no outside record to look up. The two point at different halves of the template's rule: provenance for sources with a record, and method for claims whose numbers can be checked against themselves. I did not read the study, so this comparison rests on its reported findings.
