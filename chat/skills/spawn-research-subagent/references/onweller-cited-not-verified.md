# Cited but Not Verified: Parsing and Evaluating Source Attribution in LLM Deep Research Agents

- **Source:** Hailey Onweller, Elias Lumer, Austin Huber, Pia Ramchandani, Vamse Kumar Subbiah and Corey Feld (Commercial Technology and Innovation Office, PricewaterhouseCoopers U.S.), "Cited but Not Verified: Parsing and Evaluating Source Attribution in LLM Deep Research Agents," arXiv preprint, version 1 submitted May 7, 2026, arXiv 2605.06635
- **Type:** preprint (not peer-reviewed)
- **Where to find it:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06635
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-11-05
- **Used by:** `chat/skills/spawn-research-subagent/SKILL.md`

## What the source covers

The paper measures whether the citations in reports written by deep research agents support the claims attached to them. Its answer is that they often don't, and that the usual quick checks miss it. For the frontier models tested, cited links almost always worked and almost always led to a page on the right topic, but the page backed the specific claim only 39 to 77 percent of the time. An ablation on two models found that letting the agent make more tool calls lowered that rate further.

### Method

The authors built a three-stage pipeline. An agent with web search writes a Markdown report with inline citations. A parser turns the report into a syntax tree, splits it into sentences and pairs each sentence with its citations, without using a model. When a citation closes a passage, the parser assigns it to every uncited sentence before it in that passage, on the reasoning that models often cite once for several related claims. The pipeline then fetches every cited page and scores each claim and citation pair on three yes-or-no tests:

- **Link works:** the address returns content, as opposed to an error, a timeout or a block. No model is involved.
- **Relevant content:** a model acting as judge decides whether the page covers the claim's topic, reading the first 5,000 characters of the page.
- **Fact check:** a judge model decides whether the facts, numbers, dates and assertions in the claim are supported by the page. Anything contradicted, absent or uncertain scores zero. The authors say they calibrated this judge by reviewing 50 to 100 of its decisions by hand.

The tests form a ladder: a citation can pass the first and fail the second, or pass the first two and fail the third.

The study covers 14 models: four from OpenAI (GPT-5.2, GPT-5.4, GPT-5 Mini and Codex), five from Anthropic (Claude Sonnet 4.5, Sonnet 4.6, Opus 4.5, Opus 4.6 and Haiku 4.5), two from Google (Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemini 3 Flash) and three open models (OSS-120B, Llama 4 Maverick and Pixtral Large). The queries come from DeepResearch Bench and BrowseComp; the paper says 130 queries in total. Every model ran in the authors' own agent harness, with one system prompt that set the citation format and a minimum search depth. These are not the vendors' own deep research products. For the ablation, the authors capped the number of tool calls at 2, 10, 30, 50, 70, 100 or 150 for GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6.

### Findings

**Working, relevant links hide unsupported claims.** Twelve of the 14 models passed the link test on more than 94 percent of citations, and every frontier model passed the relevance test on more than 80 percent. Fact-check rates ranged from 24 percent (OSS-120B) to 77 percent (Claude Opus 4.5). Among frontier models the range was 39 percent (GPT-5 Mini) to 77 percent, with most between 45 and 60 percent. In practice, a reader who clicks a citation in one of these reports will almost always find a live page on the right subject, and for the frontier models that page fails to support the specific claim somewhere between a quarter and three-fifths of the time.

**Providers differ, and more citations went with lower accuracy.** OpenAI's models completed every query and produced the most citations (GPT-5 Mini made 1,272 attributions) but scored 39 to 59 percent on the fact check. Anthropic's models completed 83 to 97 percent of queries and scored highest: 77 percent for Opus 4.5 and 69 percent for Haiku 4.5, against 52 to 59 percent for the Sonnet models and 54 percent for Opus 4.6. Google's two models scored 45 and 49 percent. The open models struggled with the task itself, completing only 17 to 40 percent of queries and making far fewer tool calls (7 to 60, against 73 to 211 for frontier models). The authors suggest that synthesizing from more passages raises the chance of misattributing or mixing up facts, and that selective citation may be the better strategy. They offer this as a hypothesis; the comparison across providers can't separate it from other differences between the models.

**More search, lower accuracy.** In the ablation, fact-check accuracy fell for both models as the tool-call cap rose, while the link and relevance tests stayed above 92 percent at every depth. GPT-5.4 fell from 79 percent at two calls to 17 percent at 150, with the largest drop between two and 10 calls (79 to 46 percent). Claude Opus 4.6 fell from 80 percent to 58 percent. The paper's headline figure, a drop of about 42 percent on average, is the mean of these two drops in percentage points (62 and 22). In relative terms the drops are about 79 percent and 28 percent. The decline is not strictly steady for either model; each has one step where accuracy rose slightly. The authors call this an information overload effect: models keep finding live, relevant pages but synthesize their facts less accurately as the number of sources grows.

The error analysis on broken links found the expected causes (pages removed, paywalls and bot blocks, timeouts). GPT-5.4 had one failed link in 2,159 checks, while two open models failed on 16 and 19 percent of links.

### Strength of the evidence

The central finding, that link and relevance checks pass while factual support often fails, is well grounded in what the pipeline measures, and the gap is large and consistent across 11 frontier models. The specific numbers are weaker than the paper's presentation suggests.

- **The judge is unnamed and loosely validated.** The paper doesn't say which model judged relevance and factual support, and it reports no agreement rate for the hand calibration of 50 to 100 decisions. Judge error affects every fact-check figure.
- **The scoring leans toward failure.** Absent or uncertain support counts as unsupported. If the fact-check judge saw only part of a long page, as the relevance judge did, claims supported further down would fail. The paper states the 5,000-character limit only for relevance. The parser's rule that a citation covers the uncited sentences before it can also charge a citation with claims its author never meant it to support. All three effects would push fact-check rates down, so the true rate of unsupported citations may be lower than reported. None changes the direction of the finding.
- **Sample sizes are small and partly unclear.** The success rates fall on multiples of one-thirtieth (16.7, 83.3, 93.3 and 96.7 percent), which suggests about 30 queries per model, not 130; the paper doesn't explain the difference. The ablation reports no number of attributions per depth and no confidence intervals, and some of its cells look small (one model's 92.3 percent link rate at 10 calls fits 12 of 13).
- **Selection may flatter some models.** Anthropic's models failed to complete more queries than OpenAI's. If the queries they dropped were the hardest, their fact-check rates are measured on an easier set.
- **The ablation confounds depth with volume.** A higher tool-call cap also means more citations and longer reports, so the ablation can't say whether accuracy falls because each synthesis has more sources or because the report makes more claims. Only two models were tested, and the cap is a maximum, not the number of calls actually made.
- **The harness is the authors'.** One system prompt that demanded a minimum search depth may have pushed models to cite pages they had skimmed. Vendor research products with their own citation pipelines might behave differently.

On transfer: every model tested predates Claude Opus 5 and Opus 5.5. Anthropic's Opus 5.5 prompting guide says that model is much less likely to cite the wrong source, without giving figures. The exact rates here should not be assumed for a current model. The qualitative points are likely to hold: a live, on-topic citation is weak evidence that the claim is supported, and reading more sources doesn't make a synthesis more accurate. Both describe the structure of the task more than the weaknesses of a particular model.

## What the skill applies

**The lead agent's check on every load-bearing claim and citation (How, step 3).** Step 3 tells the lead agent to open the cited primary source for each load-bearing claim and citation in a subagent's report before relaying it, and gives the reason that subagent reports can misattribute sources. This paper is the main evidence for both halves. It shows that live, on-topic citations fail to support the specific claim in a large share of cases, between about a quarter and three-fifths for the frontier models tested, and that only reading the source against the claim catches the failure. The paper measured each agent's own report, not a report handed from one agent to another. A subagent's report is the same kind of artifact, so the evidence carries over, but the paper doesn't test whether misattribution grows or shrinks when the report changes hands. The step's second reason, that subagents overstate how much they read, has no support here; the paper never compared what agents said they read with what they fetched.

**Haiku subagents (How, step 1).** The skill runs its subagents on Haiku for speed and cost. This paper is the one source behind the skill that measures Haiku on a research task. Claude Haiku 4.5 had the second-highest fact-check rate of the 14 models, 69 percent, above every Sonnet model, Opus 4.6 and every OpenAI and Google model. It also completed the fewest queries of any frontier model, 83 percent. The result suggests the smallest model is not the weak link for citation accuracy. It also means that about three in 10 of a Haiku subagent's citations, by this paper's strict scoring, may not support the claim attached to them, which is why the skill makes the step 3 check mandatory. The model tested was Haiku 4.5, and the skill's alias may resolve to a later Haiku.

**Citations and exact sentences in the template (Template, second paragraph).** The template asks for a URL on every claim and, for key figures, the exact sentence from the primary source. The paper shows why a URL alone is weak evidence. The exact-sentence request gives the lead agent something to check the source against quickly, a step the paper's fact-check judge had to infer from the whole page. That use is the skill's own design; the paper doesn't test whether asking for supporting sentences improves accuracy.

**The stopping rule in the template (Template, third paragraph).** The template tells each subagent to stop when every part of its question is answered from a primary source or shown unanswerable. The paper's depth ablation supports stopping early on quality grounds: accuracy fell for both models tested as the tool-call cap rose. Splitting a task among several subagents, each with a narrow question, keeps each one's synthesis smaller, which by the paper's information overload account should help. That last point is an inference; the paper never tested multiple agents.

**Reporting which claims were verified (How, step 4).** Step 4 tells the lead agent to say which claims it verified. The paper's ladder of checks gives that report a useful distinction: a citation whose link was checked is not a citation whose claim was checked.

## What the skill omits

**A cap on subagent tool calls.** The depth ablation could justify limiting how many searches each subagent makes, and the skill sets no limit. The ablation tested two models, confounds search depth with the number of claims in the report and gives no basis for a particular number. The template's stopping rule does the same work without a fixed cap.

**Link and relevance checks.** Step 3 asks only for the strongest check, that the source supports the claim. The paper's weaker checks pass almost always, so naming them would add steps that rarely catch anything.

**Checking every citation.** The skill limits the lead agent's check to load-bearing claims. The paper's pipeline checks everything, which an offline evaluation can afford and a conversation usually can't. The paper doesn't test whether a subset is enough.

**The evaluation pipeline and provider comparisons.** The parser, the judge models and the model rankings are tools for studying citation quality across many reports. They don't translate into steps for one research task.

## Conflicts with other references

The skill's reference folder held only `claude-code-subagents.md` when this file was written. The notes on the other sources behind the skill come from the sources themselves.

- **Claude Code subagents documentation (`claude-code-subagents.md`).** The page presents Haiku as a cost lever and makes no claim about its answer quality. This paper supplies the missing quality evidence for one task: Haiku 4.5 cited at least as accurately as larger models. That supports the skill's choice of Haiku, with the step 3 check as the safeguard for the citations that still fail.
- **Samway and colleagues, coding agent teams (arXiv 2610.17471).** In software tasks, cheap worker models paid off for exploration and planning, a lead that verified the work itself did as well as one that delegated verification and extra verifiers added little. That matches the skill's split: Haiku subagents gather and the lead agent checks. That study concerns code, not citations, so it supports the division of labor, not the citation check itself.
- **Pradhan and Goley, "Trust, but Don't Verify" (arXiv 2606.05403).** They find that models detect fabricated statistics in isolation but not while synthesizing several sources. That fits this paper's information overload account and supports making citation checking a separate, focused step for the lead agent, as step 3 does, instead of trusting the subagent to check as it writes.
- **Wineburg and McGrew, lateral reading (2019).** The template asks subagents to judge sources by provenance and to seek an independent second source, which follows the fact-checkers' practice of leaving a site to see what others say about it. This paper's check goes the other way, into the cited source. The two answer different questions (whether a source is trustworthy, and whether it says what is claimed), and the skill asks for both: lateral reading in the template and the source check in step 3.
- **Anthropic's Opus 5 and Opus 5.5 prompting guides (read for the system prompt's references).** The Opus 5 guide warns that explicit verification instructions cause over-verification and that subagents should not be used to verify the model's own work. Step 3 is an explicit verification instruction, but it applies to another agent's citations, not the lead agent's own answer, and the subagents gather while the lead checks. The Opus 5.5 guide says that model cites the wrong source much less often, with no figures. The lead agent may be Opus 5.5, but the citations it checks come from Haiku subagents, so the guide's claim doesn't remove the need for step 3.
